QUOTA ON FIRM VALUE

The Smooth Transition: A Study on The Impact of the French Board Gender Quota on
Firm Value

Abstract:

   This study examines the impact of the French board gender quota on firm value, drawing com-
   parisons with previous research. Utilizing methodology akin to Ahern and Dittmar (2012), who
   investigated the Norwegian board gender quota, we analyze the effects of the French quota enacted
   in 2011. We use the pre-quota cross-sectional variation in board gender diversity to instrument for
   exogenous changes to corporate boards resulting from the law. Contrary to prior findings on the
   impact of board gender quotas, our results reveal no evidence supporting the notion that the French
   quota negatively affects firm value. Despite the quota mandating an involuntary increase in female
   board members, we observe no significant impact on immediate stock return or long-term indicators
   such as Tobin’s Q and return on equity. Additionally, board size and tenure remain unaffected by
   the quota, suggesting a smooth transition rather than forced adjustments. By comparing with the
   Norwegian experience, characterized by rushed compliance, we argue that the extended compliance
   period and softer sanctions upon failure to comply in France facilitated a gradual adjustment pro-
   cess, mitigating adverse effects on firm value. These results challenge prior assumptions and provide
   policymakers with insights on implementing gender quotas without compromising firm performance
   while advancing gender equality in corporate leadership.

Keywords:

   Board Gender Quota, Cop´e Zimmerman law, Diversity, Firm Value, Board skills

1 Introduction

Gender equality in the workplace has become a prominent political issue, resulting in the implemen-
tation of board gender quotas in several countries. Historically, women have been underrepresented in
higher-level positions, commonly referred to as a “glass ceiling” (Bertrand, Black, Jensen, and Lleras-
Muney, 2019). To address this issue, European countries have passed hard laws to increase board
gender diversity, such as the ones in Norway, Italy, Spain, and France. Others, like Luxembourg, the
UK, and Sweden, have encouraged a specific percentage of female board members through soft laws
(Ferreira, Ginglinger, Paris-Dauphine, and Skalli, 2021). As stated by the directive passed by the
European parliament in 2022, starting in July 2026, all listed companies in the European Union must
have at least 40% of their non-executive directors and 33% of their executive directors of the under-
represented gender (European Commission, 2022). This new EU directive will require many firms to
replace their current board members to meet the new requirements, making it crucial how a gender
quota affects firms and their boards. There is, however, limited evidence on the effect of gender quotas
on board and firm outcomes. A prominent study by Ahern and Dittmar (2012) on the Norwegian
gender quota from 2003 found significant costs to shareholders and a decrease in board skills. As such,
this paper employs a methodology akin to Ahern and Dittmar (2012) to examine the impact of the
French gender quota imposed in 2011. The objective is to determine whether, irrespective of policy
implementation details and contextual factors, gender quotas have adverse effects on board skills and
firm value.
   Our study addresses the following research question:

      Are board gender quotas unfavorable for firm value?

   In 2011, the Cop´e-Zimmerman law was enacted in France, mandating that 40% of board members
must be of the underrepresented gender by 2017, with an interim target of 20% by 2014. This law
applies to all firms headquartered in France that are publicly listed and non-listed firms with over 500
employees or revenues exceeding €50m over the previous three years (R´epublice Fran¸caise, 2011). Any
board appointment that violates the stipulated provisions or does not help remedy the non-compliance
will be considered invalid. In addition, failure to comply with any of the mandated proportion re-
quirements leads to a suspension of payment of director fees until the gender diversity in the board of
directors meets the stipulated quota threshold.
   We have used both the event study methodology and instrumental variable approach used by
Ahern and Dittmar (2012) and compared our findings to their prominent study on the Norwegian
gender quota. Thus, understanding the French context in relation to the Norwegian one is crucial.
Publicly listed companies in Norway were initially encouraged in 2003 to voluntarily meet a gender
quota threshold of 40% within one and a half years. However, when compliance fell short, companies
were granted a two-year transition period to reach the same threshold, but this time, facing penalties if
they did not comply. Upon no fulfillment in 2008, the company would be dissolved. This is in contrast
to France, where firms had six years, and not two years as in Norway, to fulfill the requirement
stipulated by the law. Additionally, the penalty for non-compliance in France, suspension of payment
of director fees, is more lenient than in Norway, where firms risk being dissolved. Not only did policy
implementation details differ between the two countries, but the female labor supply setting also
differed, with France having a less favorable situation, as found by Adams and Kirchmaier (2015).
   According to our research on the French example, the implementation of gender quotas does not,
regardless of context and policy implementation details, have an adverse effect on firm value. The
results of the regressions performed to investigate the effects of the French law showed no unfavorable
results. Our analysis first reveals that the stock market anticipated a smooth transition, as there was
no difference in industry-adjusted immediate returns for companies that were closer or further away
from the quota threshold. Secondly, our long-term study on how firm value was affected also did not
support the finding of any adverse effects. This is in contrast with the study performed by Ahern and
Dittmar (2012), which found both a negative immediate stock market effect and unfavorable outcomes
on long-term firm value following the law’s implementation. In Norway, it was found that the skill level
of board members decreased as a result of the law, which subsequently caused the decline in firm value
(Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). We attribute the lack of adverse firm value effects in France to the fact
that our regression showed that the law had no negative effect on the percentage of board members
who had industry-relevant backgrounds or strong financial skills. We argue that this was enabled by

the more extended transition period, which enabled a gradual replacement of new competent female
board members replacing the incumbent male directors.
   To conduct our analysis, we gathered data from a diverse set of sources. Data on our treatment
group, companies located in France that were listed on Euronext Paris, along with our control group,
US-situated companies listed on American exchanges, was mainly sourced from Refinitiv Eikon. For
firms that had some missing data points, we acquired the remaining data using alternative sources.
Missing financial data was obtained from CapitalIQ, and missing data on the board was gathered
from the companies’ annual reports. Our dataset included almost 100 French firms, a sample size
comparable to that used by Ahern and Dittmar (2012).
   Our research involved an event study, per the methodology of Ahern and Dittmar (2012), to
investigate the impact of the quota announcement on companies’ stock prices. We divided the French
companies into two groups based on their female board participation at the time of the event study:
Low diversity and High diversity. We chose a threshold value at the median of 8.33% in order to create
two approximately equally large groups.
   Since the law had been frequently debated in parliament prior to its enactment, it was expected
that a well-informed market would have already factored in the changes before the date when the
statute was passed. Instead, we examine the point in time when the bill was initially brought up in
parliament, January 20th, 2010. On the same day, an attention-grabbing news article called “Shaking
up the corporate world” (France 24, 2010) was published. We found no significant difference in the
average industry-adjusted stock returns between companies with fewer or more female directors in
France, as the difference of 0.83% was insignificant. This suggests that the stock market did not
foresee any negative impact on companies with fewer female board members. Our study results are
robust and consider various factors, including board size, firm size, and assets. We also had a control
group consisting of American firms listed in the US.
   In order to evaluate the long-term influence of the quota on firm value, an instrumental variable
procedure was used. Similar to Ahern and Dittmar (2012), we utilized the pre-quota cross-sectional
variation in female board representation to instrument for exogenous changes to corporate boards
following the quota. As all public limited firms were required to have at least 20% female directors
in 2014 and 40% in 2017, those companies that had a higher percentage of female directors prior
to the quota were less restricted than the ones with lower female representation. The results from
instrumental variable estimates reveal that the quota did not negatively impact Tobin’s Q, as the
point estimate -0.279% was insignificant. These results include firm and time-fixed effects to control
for both observed and unobserved heterogeneity between the firms and yearly trends. Placebo tests
run on US firms, as also done by Ahern and Dittmar (2012), show no significant connection between
the increase of women on boards due to the quota and subsequent changes in firm value. These
outcomes demonstrate that the quota mandated by the law and its subsequent board reorganizations
did not have any unfavorable effect on firm value. Furthermore, a robustness check was conducted
by examining how the law altered the firms’ return on equity (ROE), and consistent with our prior
findings, no significant effect was found.
   To gain a deeper understanding of why there was no loss in value, we analyzed how women were
added to the boards and how the quota affected the skills of directors. This is important as Ahern and
Dittmar (2012) concluded that the negative effect on firm value resulting from the Norwegian gender
quota was attributable to a decrease in board skills. In this study, we define board-specific skills as the
percentage of board members who have either an industry-specific background or a strong financial
background. Our instrumental variable regression on board skills showed a positive coefficient of
0.161%, which was insignificant, meaning that board skills cannot be concluded to have been negatively
affected as a result of the law.
   We conclude that our study did not find any negative effects of the gender quota as a result of
board-specific skills not having declined, as they did in Norway (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). This was
probably made possible by the gradual replacement of board members in France, whereas in Norway,
the negative effects were a result of firms making drastic adjustments to meet the deadline of the quota
(Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). As the female supply setting was less preferable in France than in Norway
(Adams and Kirchmaier, 2015), our study concludes that the smooth replacement was enabled by the
longer transition period and possibly the more lenient penalty for non-compliance. These results may
offer guidance to policymakers on maintaining firm value while promoting greater gender equality in
the boardroom.

   Related literature This paper contributes to the large and growing literature on board gender
quotas’ impact on firm value (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012; Adams and Ferreira, 2009; Bernile, Bhagwat,
and Yonker, 2018; Ginglinger and Raskopf, 2023; Likitapiwat, Treepongkaruna, Jiraporn, and Uyar,
2022; Greene, Intintoli, and Kahle, 2020; Burrow, Fedorets, and Gibert, 2018). More specifically,
by studying the immediate stock price reaction at the announcement of the board gender quota and
studying its long-term impact on Tobin’s Q in a Norwegian firm setting, Ahern and Dittmar (2012)
argue that forced increased board gender diversity caused deterioration of firm value. They saw a
significant negative long-term impact, as well as an adverse immediate stock market reaction. A
similar study by Burrows et al. (2018) investigated the financial outcomes of the German law from
2015 that mandated a supervisory board (two-tier system) gender quota for approximately 100 large
corporations. The authors found no significant effect on the financial outcomes of the firms. Also,
Greene et al. (2020) studied the immediate stock market reaction in 2018 to the announcement of the
first mandated board gender quota in the US. In California, firms were allowed a transition period
of a bit over one year to have at least one female on the board, and upon no fulfillment, firms were
fined $300,000. The authors (2020) found that announcement returns decreased on average by 1.2%.
We contribute to this literature by providing evidence on the impact of the board gender quota on
firm value in another context and where the policy implementation details differ. Firstly, the French
quota was implemented in a way that allowed firms a relatively longer time to comply with the law. In
comparison to Norway’s two-year transition period and California’s slightly over one-year transition
period to avoid penalties for non-compliance, French firms were allowed six years to change their
boards. Secondly, the consequence of failure to fulfill the quota was relatively softer in France than
in Norway. In France, firms were punished with suspension of paying director fees, but in Norway, it
was dissolution. Finally, France had a worse female supply setting relative to Norway (Adams and
Kirchmaier, 2015). Ultimately, we shed light on what factors play a crucial role in how firm value is
affected by the implementation of a new gender quota at the board level.
   Furthermore, we contribute to the literature on how board gender quotas affect the characteristics
of the board members. Several papers cover how the skills and experience of board members changed
as a result of the board gender quota (see Ferreira et al., 2021; Ahern and Dittmar, 2012; Bertrand
et al., 2019). In particular, Ferreira et al. (2021) compared the characteristics of the females hired
before and after the quota. The authors (2021) found that female directors hired after the quota
were more independent, experienced, internationally diverse, and no less academically qualified than
those hired before the quota. However, they make no conclusions about how the females hired after
the quota differed from their male predecessors. As such, we contribute to the literature by not only
comparing the skills of the new and incumbent females but also by comparing the new females to their
male predecessors.

2 Data and Methodology

2.1 Data Gathering
We primarily used Refinitiv Eikon to obtain the necessary data for our study. We downloaded a
large dataset containing variables for both the event study and the long-term analysis to ensure that
the same companies participated in both studies. We limited our dataset to public companies to
ensure similar characteristics and also follow the same approach as in Ahern and Dittmar (2012). For
the event study, we used a cross-sectional dataset with company names and corresponding variables’
values on the 20th of January in 2010, the main date of our event study. These variables included
GICS Sector, GICS Industry Group, board size, board gender diversity, market capitalization, and
total assets. Stock returns for a five-day period, two days before until two days after 2010-01-20, were
also acquired. We downloaded these statistics for our treatment group, companies incorporated in
France listed on the Euronext Paris, and for our control group, companies incorporated in the USA
listed on a US exchange.
   A panel dataset was used for the long-term study. We gathered data for all companies, both French
and US, between 2009 and 2021. The variables we obtained included board size, board gender diversity,
market capitalization, annual stock return, common stock, assets, ROE, and board-specific skills. The
latter variable is defined as the percentage of board members who have either an industry-specific
background or a strong financial background. We gathered all variables for both our treatment and

                                            4
control groups.
   We had to exclude some firms from our study as Refinitiv Eikon did not have data points for all
variables between 2009 and 2021 for all firms. The main reason for exclusion was the lack of data on
board gender diversity. After screening the raw data, we were left with a sample size of 69 French firms
for the event and the long-term study. To ensure a robust result, we collected data on the remaining
French firms in the SBF120 index using alternative sources, either excluded due to a few missing data
points or having no data at all. CapitalIQ was used to gather financial information, and data on board
gender diversity was collected from the companies’ annual reports.
   The process of acquiring female board participation from annual reports was a systematic one. We
first identified the members of the board of directors in the annual report and calculated the board
gender diversity by dividing the number of female directors by the total number of board members.
Employee representatives were included in our calculations to match with the data downloaded from
Refinitiv Eikon. To ensure the accuracy of the board gender diversity, we verified the gender of
each person. We first checked if pronouns were mentioned in the description of the director. When
no pronouns were used, we visually determined the gender. This involved looking at the attached
photograph of the board members. If there was no photography available, we determined the gender
based on the first name of the director. However, it can be challenging to distinguish between male
and female names, especially for non-French speakers. For instance, Fran¸cois and Fran¸coise are male
and female names respectively. Therefore, we consulted the Namsor database, which provided the
frequency of males and females in France having the inserted name, to guide us in our decision-making
process.
   With the downloaded data, Tobin’s Q was calculated using the following formula, per Ahern and
Dittmar (2012):

                                  ′
          T obin sQ = (T otalAssets − CommonEquity + M arketEquity)/T otalAssets (1)

                  ′
   T obin sQ is calculated as the sum of T otalAssets (reported) and M arketEquity, less CommonEquity
(reported) divided by T otalAssets. Market equity, in turn, is the aggregated market value, which is
determined by multiplying the price per share by the total number of outstanding shares on Refinitiv
Eikon.
   Our sample size of French firms used in the event study is 89, and in the long-term study, a total
of 93 firms were used, resulting in 1116 firm-year observations.

2.3 Corporate Governance in France, Europe and the US
Formally adopted on January 27, 2011, the Cop´e-Zimmerman law requires a minimum of 20% female
representation on company boards by January 2014, with the proportion increasing to 40% by January
2017. This quota applies to all board members, except for employee representatives, regardless of
whether they are independent or not. Soci´et´es Anonymes (limited liability companies), Commandites
par actions (limited partnerships), and Societas Europaea (European Societies) are all subject to this
law. Non-listed companies are also subject to the law, provided they have at least 500 employees or

                                            7
have earned €50 million over the previous three years. Firms that failed to adhere to the quota were
punished with suspension of paying director fees (R´epublice Fran¸caise, 2011). The law was submitted
to the French National Assembly on 2009-12-03, and was adopted in the first reading on 2010-01-
20. Parliamentary debates continued from that point until it was officially adopted in January 2011.
Companies listed on the Euronext Paris that are not based in France are not obligated to comply with
the law (Ginglinger and Raskopf, 2023).
   The issue of quotas on boards has been a topic of extensive debate in Europe for several years.
Norway became the first country to adopt a law in December 2003 which mandated that at least 40%
of directors must be female by July 2005. However, voluntary compliance failed, and in 2006, the
law became compulsory with a two-year transition period. Firms that failed to adhere to the quota
would be dissolved (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). Additionally, hard laws regarding women on boards
have been adopted by several European countries, such as Italy, Spain, the Netherlands, Belgium,
Austria, and Germany. Soft laws have been adopted for boards in Luxembourg, the UK, and Sweden
(Ferreira et al., 2021). In November 2022, the European Commission adopted a directive that sets a
minimum objective that listed companies in Europe must have 40% of the underrepresented gender in
non-executive board member positions by 2026 (European Commission, 2022).
   During the six-year transition period for French firms between 2011 and 2017, there was no gender
quota in place in the US. However, in September 2018, California passed a law that required one
female director on the boards of listed companies. The law stated that all companies headquartered
in the state must have at least one female director by the end of 2019. Firms that did not fulfill the
quota were fined $300,000. This gender quota was overturned by a Californian court in May 2022. We
still determine the United States as a valid control group since only one of the 50 states had a gender
quota, and it was implemented after the main period of interest between 2011 and 2017.
   While European companies could have been a suitable control group, most of them had already
implemented board gender quotas or soft laws during this period. Therefore, the USA was the best
country to control with as the largest developed economy with no quota on female directors during
the period of interest. This is also in line with the choice of placebo group used by Ahern and
Dittmar (2012) in their Norway study and Ginglinger and Raskopf (2023) who looked at how the
French gender quota affected E&S performance. Additionally, French and US boards share similar
characteristics (Ferreira et al., 2021), further strengthening the choice of control group.

2.4 Methodology
As we categorize the quota’s impact on firm value by looking at it from a short- and long-term
perspective to determine the causal relationship, we have divided the methodology into two separate
parts. Firstly, we investigate the immediate impact on stock prices by conducting an event study that
analyzes the market reaction when the gender quota was first brought up in parliament. Secondly,
we address the firm’s long-term value by studying if there is a relation between the forced change in
                                                                                                                        ′
female board participation and the change in firm value (T obin sQ). The two different methodologies
used are further specified below.

2.4.1 Event Study
Although the French parliament passed the statute on the 27th of January, 2011 (R´epublice Fran¸caise,
2011), discussions in parliament started earlier (Ferreira et al., 2021). Therefore, the stock market
would have already anticipated the law’s effects before it was formally enacted, making the 27th of
January, 2011, an inappropriate time to study. To investigate the market’s actual response, we selected
the 20th of January, 2010, as the date of our event study. This was the day when the proposed gender
quota was first brought up for discussion in parliament (Ginglinger and Raskopf, 2023). On the
same day, an article about the revolutionary quota proposal was France24’s second most-read article
released. The wholly government-owned news media France24 called the article ”Shaking up the
corporate world”, emphasizing its disruptive nature. In the article, it is brought up how a center-
right lawmaker describes the bill as “a much-needed electric shock” (France 24, 2010) emphasizing the
unexpectedness of the law. Thus, 2010-01-20, is a suitable day to study the stock market’s reaction.
   Our study was conducted over a period of five consecutive days, two days before the news announce-
ment and two days after, 2010-01-18 to 2010-01-22, in line with Ahern and Dittmar (2012). These
dates have also been checked to verify that they occur during weekdays when the stock market is open

                                            8
for trading. We used industry-adjusted stock returns to control for industry effects. Before controlling
for industry effects, it was ensured that firms with higher or lower female board participation did not
tend to belong to a particular industry. We also confirmed that every French firm belonged to a GICS
industry group with at least five US firms. To calculate the industry-adjusted returns, we subtracted
the median stock return of US firms within the same GICS Industry Group, as per the approach of
Ahern and Dittmar (2012).
   The French firms were divided into two groups based on their female board participation at the
time of the event study, in line with the methodology of Ahern and Dittmar (2012). One group is
called High diversity firms, and the other is called Low diversity firms. This was to examine whether
there was a difference in returns for firms closer or further away from the threshold. We created two
approximately equally large groups, one group with a female board participation under or equal to
the median of 8.33%, and one group with a board gender diversity above the median. The number
of firms in the High diversity group was 41, and 48 companies belonged to the Low diversity group,
resulting in a similar proportion between the two groups as Ahern and Dittmar (2012) had.
   The Low diversity group only had an average female board participation of 3.68% at the time of the
event study, while the High diversity group had an average of 15.57%, meaning that the first group only
fulfilled 9.20% of the 40% quota while the second fulfilled 38.93% of the threshold value. This means
that the second group needs to replace a much larger quantity of board members compared to the first
group. As Table 3 shows, the two groups are not dissimilar when examining several characteristics,
making them two good groups to compare. The only significant difference is female board participation,
as that variable was used to define the two groups. Table 4 shows that the two groups are also not
statistically different in terms of which sector they belong to. The only significant difference is that real
estate firms are more likely to have fewer females on their boards. In their study, Ahern and Dittmar
(2012) also had one significant sector difference but concluded that as it is only one sector, it does not
affect the result’s credibility. For prudence, an additional test is run to ensure there is no correlation
between pre-quota board gender diversity and industry belonging. Fisher’s exact test of the difference
in the distributions of industries by female board participation finds no statistical difference between
the likelihood of a firm to be in any one particular sector based on its percentage of female directors
in 2009. This implies that differences between the two groups in subsequent regressions should stem
from the difference in board gender diversity.

2.4.2 Long-term
Our analysis that assesses the long-term consequences of the gender quota on firm valuation uses
Tobin’s Q as the dependent variable, in line with prior research (Coles et al., 2008; Yermack, 1996;
Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). Tobin’s Q is the most suitable variable to examine, as it measures the value
the company has above its assets, including future growth opportunities. It is calculated in accordance
with the formula stated in Equation 1. By studying this measure, we can investigate the company’s
ability to generate value and whether this ability has changed after the mandated changes of directors
resulting from the law. This was also the variable used by Ahern and Dittmar (2012), making our
results comparable.
   To ensure the validity of our findings, we have taken measures to address any potential endogeneity
concerns. One issue is that companies may respond to the gender quota in different ways. Another
issue is that they may attribute their positive or negative performance to the quota, even when it is
not related. For example, a company may hire more women before experiencing favorable(unfavorable)
financial outcomes and attribute the success(failure) to the gender quota. We have accounted for these
possibilities in our analysis. To overcome these problems, we adopt an instrumental variable approach,
taking inspiration from studies by Ahern and Dittmar (2012) and Stevenson (2010). Specifically, we
leverage the pre-quota cross-sectional variation in board gender diversity as an instrument to capture
exogenous variation in mandated changes in female board participation over time.
   Ahern and Dittmar (2012) used data from one year before the implementation of the Norwegian
gender quota. In our study, we have utilized data from 2009, which is two years before the French law
was implemented in 2011. We have chosen this year to further ensure instrument exogeneity, meaning
no correlation between the instrument (zi) and the error term (ui), (corr(zi, ui) = 0). Going further
back would eliminate many companies from the study due to data unavailability. For the firms where
the data on board gender diversity is available, we compared the 2008 and 2009 values, concluding
that they were very similar, indicating that the board composition in 2009 was not impacted by the
law in 2008.
   Since the gender composition of a company’s board is not random but something that is actively
chosen, we observe whether there are any links between the pre-quota board gender diversity and
certain firm characteristics and sectors. As shown by Table 3 and Table 4, no such relationship is
found apart from in one sector, similar to Ahern and Dittmar (2012). To further ensure that there
are no links between industries and pre-quota crossectional variation, Fisher’s exact test finds no
significant difference (p = 0.78) in industry distribution based on pre-quota board gender diversity. In
this analysis, we also adjust the dependent variable for US industry returns as a precautionary measure,
per Ahern and Dittmar (2012). Hence, the dependent variable is not Tobin’s Q but industry-adjusted

                                           10
Tobin’s Q. Similar to the event study, we subtract the median value of American firms in the same
GICS Industry Group.
   To identify the effect of the gender quota on firm value, we estimate the following equation:

                    Qi,t = α + βpercentf emaledirectorsi, t + θi + πt + ϵi,t (2)

   In this analysis, we consider the industry-adjusted Tobin’s Q for firm i during the year t, denoted as
Qi, t. The model has a constant, α, and a coefficient β that establishes the strength of the relationship
between Qi, t, and the percentf emaledirectors. To control for any constant differences in Qi, t among
different firms, we add firm fixed effects (denoted as θ). In all regressions with firm-fixed effects, we
cluster standard errors within the company. This is to avoid potential problems of serial correlation
in the time series of within-firm variation that can be observed in differences-in-differences variables
(Bertrand et al., 2004). In addition, we add time-fixed effects (denoted as π) to account for any
booms or slumps during particular years. Furthermore, we run placebo tests for US firms alongside
our regressions on French firms. We instrument the percentage of female directors using female board
participation in 2009 interacted with year dummies.
   In order to ensure the robustness of our analysis, we conducted a similar instrumental variable
regression using a different performance measure, return on equity (ROE). ROE is calculated as profit
before tax divided by book equity, and it reveals a company’s profitability by measuring how much
profit it generates with the money the shareholders have invested. This measure is essential for our
study because it assesses a company’s efficiency without considering market value, as Tobin’s Q does.
A high ROE value indicates a profitable and efficient company that generates a good return on the
capital invested by the company’s shareholders.
   Another important part of our long-term study is to see how the skill level of the board is impacted
by the mandated quota. This is interesting because of two reasons. Firstly, Ahern and Dittmar (2012)
saw a decline in the skill level of the board as incumbent board members were ousted in favor of female
directors who could help fulfill the quota. Secondly, the authors attribute the decline in firm value to
the decline in board skills (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). Hence, this study will examine how the Cop´e
Zimmerman law affected the skill level of the board. Our dependent variable is board-specific skills,
which is defined as the percentage of board members who either have an industry-relevant background
or strong financial skills. As boards provide value by monitoring and advising the CEO (Fama and
Jensen, 1983), we posit that these two traits serve as good indicators of expertise.
   We will investigate how board-specific skills is impacted by the increased board gender diversity
stemming from the French gender quota per the instrumental variable methodology used by Ahern
and Dittmar (2012) to study how education and experience among board members were affected by
the Norwegian law.
   We thus use the following equation:

             boardspecif icskillsi,t = α + βpercentf emaledirectorsi,t + θi + πt + ϵi,t (3)

   We also aim to examine the effects of the law on board size and board tenure. Our goal is to assess
whether new female directors were added following the law or if they replaced existing male directors.
Once more, we employ an instrumental variable method, using the pre-quota percentage of female
board participation to instrument for exogenous changes to corporate boards.

4 Discussion

The short- and long-term studies in the result section are consistent regarding the board gender quota’s
impact on the firm value of the affected French companies. In the short term, Table 5 and 6 provide
insight into the stock market’s reaction to the news announcement about the board gender quota.
Table 5 shows the difference between the average industry-adjusted returns for the High diversity
group and Low diversity group to be 0.83%. However, the result is insignificant. Moreover, the
same study done on our control group of US firms showed no significant result because they were not
affected by the announcement of the French board gender quota. In contrast, Ahern and Dittmar
(2012) found that Low board gender diversity firms had, on average, a 3.56% worse return than the
High diversity group in relation to the announcement of the board gender quota. The authors’ (2012)
findings were significant. Also, the stock market reaction for Californian firms in response to the
quota announcement was -1.2% on average return (Greene et al., 2020). Thus, we can see that for the
French law, the stock market reaction differed in that it was of a smaller magnitude and insignificant,
compared to the reactions to the Californian and Norwegian gender quotas.
   Furthermore, the OLS regression in Table 6 shows a similar result. The fact that no one out of the
four top variables in the table was significant implies that there was no significant relationship between
firms’ industry-adjusted stock return in response to the quota announcement and their female board
participation. This goes for both groups of board gender diversity of French firms. In the Norway
study, the insignificant variables were the same apart for the country dummy variable interacted with
the High diversity dummy variable (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). This value was 3.5% and significant,
implying that the High diversity firms performed relatively better than the Low diversity firms. The
corresponding number in our study was -0.5% and insignificant. Hence, a difference in performance
cannot be concluded.
   Furtado and Rozeff (1987) state that the wealth effects of board shifts reflect the capital market’s
view on the change. We argue that the differing stock market reactions to the French and Norwegian
(Ahern and Dittmar, 2012) laws are related to investors interpreting the impact of the quota differently
in the two scenarios. As such, we will next analyze what could have caused the disparate views by
comparing the differences in the long-term firm value development.
   The mandated increase in board gender diversity from the French board gender quota does not
have a significant impact on the long-term development of Tobin’s Q. Table 7 illustrates that in our
instrumental variable regression, using the 2009 pre-quota cross-sectional variation in board gender
diversity as our instrumental variable, French firms’ Tobin’s Q decreased by 0.28% on average as a
result of the law. However, the result is insignificant. This implies that even though the board gender
quota forced an involuntary increase in board gender diversity, it had no significant impact on the
value of firms. This is further confirmed by our robustness test using ROE as our dependent variable
in Table 12 (see Appendix), where the result was also insignificant. As expected, the result for our

                                           15
control group, US firms, was insignificant as the firms were not subject to the French law. Our results
differ from the paper by Ahern and Dittmar (2012), who found that firm value decreased by 1.9% as
a result of the quota. Once again, their result from a different setting was of a larger magnitude and
significant. In addition, we also studied whether there was a lag effect by looking at data until three
years after 2017. It could be the case that it takes some time before any impact from a change in the
board of directors is reflected in the company’s operations and value. Table 9 (see Appendix) shows
that at no point in time, the pre-quota variation in board gender diversity interacting with Tobin’s Q
variable is significant. These findings are in line with Ahern and Dittmar (2012), who neither found any
lagged impact on firm value, although only looking at one year after the quota deadline. Consequently,
our result does not show support for the forced board gender diversity impacting firm value under or
after the transition period.
   Our findings suggest that the impact of a board gender quota on firm value varies depending on the
context and is not always negative. Ahern and Dittmar (2012) demonstrate that firm value declines
in both the short- and the long-term in their examination of the Norwegian law. Both quotas have
had the same overarching goal in society, to increase gender diversity in corporate boardrooms, which
they have been successful in achieving. However, one example led to shareholders bearing a cost for
the increased female board participation, and not the other. The contrasting results imply that French
firms could cope with the transition more successfully than firms in Norway.
   A possible explanation as to why no long-term performance effect was found in our French experi-
ence could be explained by the law not having a negative impact on board-specific skills. Ahern and
Dittmar (2012) considered the decline in board skills to have caused the decline in firm value. In Table
8, our instrumental variable regression on board-specific skills, which is defined as the percentage of
board members having either an industry-relevant background or strong financial skills, with board
gender diversity as the independent variable, illustrates an insignificant relationship between the two.
Moreover, as can be seen in Table 13 and Table 14 (see Appendix), the board size nor average board
tenure was not significantly affected by the forced increased board gender diversity. This suggests
that board size remained roughly constant and that incumbent male directors were replaced instead
of additional females hired. Thus, our result implies that French firms, in contrast to Norwegian com-
panies, were able to replace male directors with female board members without negatively impacting
the board-specific skill level. Ferreira (2021), who also studied the effects of the French board gender
quota, found that females who entered the board as a result of the quota were more independent,
experienced, and equally academically educated compared to the females hired before the quota. Our
result thus adds to this finding as it shows that even though there was a forced replacement of board
members, the new females in France were not only as skilled as the incumbent female board mem-
bers as found by Ferreira et al. (2021), but equally as skilled as their male predecessors. Ahern and
Dittmar (2012) found that the new female directors who entered the Norwegian firms’ boards had
significantly less experience and relevant characteristics than the previous male directors. The authors
(2012) suggest that the quota posed a demand shock for women with relevant board characteristics,
and the supply did not match, causing subsequent performance deterioration. However, Adams and
Kirchmaier (2015) show that Norway had a better female supply setting than France. The authors
(2015) examine what country-specific factors pose challenges for women to enter the boardroom dur-
ing the Norwegian transition period (2012) and one year prior to our French event study. Adams and
Kirchmaier (2016) conclude that France had a lower level of female labor supply is less family-friendly,
which reduces women’s ability to manage both family and work and has a culture of strong family
values, making it difficult for women to accept board positions, relative to Norway. As such, this
suggests that Norwegian firms should have had less of a problem finding suitable females relative to
French firms. Hence, the supply factor fails to explain why French firms were more successful than
Norwegian firms (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012) in maintaining their skill level on the board.
   We argue that if firms had to rush to fulfill the quota, it affected their ability to maintain the
same skill level on the board. In Norway (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012), board gender diversity rapidly
increased between 2006 and 2007, relative to the previous years when the quota was voluntary. This
is illustrated by the spike during those years in Figure 3. The authors (2012) also find that their
significant result on Tobin’s Q is driven by the two years before the quota deadline. In our study, we
find that France was able to adjust smoothly to the new legislation. In Figure 1, board gender diversity
can be seen increasing with a roughly constant slope between the years 2011 and 2017. The result
is similar for both the High and Low diversity groups. This suggests that firms were not required to

                                           16
    Figure 3: Average gender diversity between 2001-2009 in Norway (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012)

make drastic adjustments at any point in time. Evidently, the various settings pushed Norwegian firms
to behave differently than French firms. Next, we explain potential reasons why these firm behaviors
could be seen.
   The reason for the different adjustment patterns can be explained by the various context and
policy implementation details in the quota. Firstly, the sanction upon failure to comply with the
quota was more extensive in Norway (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012) relative to France. In the case of
France, firms were punished with suspension of paying director fees if they did not fulfill the quota when
compliance was due. On the other hand, Norway had a more dramatic outcome upon non-compliance.
The company would have been dissolved if the Norwegian firms had not fulfilled the quota in time.
As such, Norwegian firms were facing a more acute threat. This can explain why Norwegian firms’
board gender diversity spiked as the deadline approached (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). Secondly, the
time window firms were allowed to adapt to the quota is another critical explanation for the various
behaviors. Bagues and Campa (2021) emphasized that in the enactment of a gender quota, more time
allowed for adaptation is needed to find relevant and desired candidates. In France, firms were allowed
six years to change their boards, compared to Norwegian firms, which had two years. The different
lengths of the transition periods likely influenced the way firms dealt with the issue. French firms were
probably less pressured to conform to the quota and had more time to find a suitable replacement
plan. Consequently, the process of substituting the male predecessors with equally skilled females was
not inhibited by a relatively worse supply. In contrast, Norwegian firms faced harder punishments and
had less time. As a result, they probably rushed the process and were unsuccessful in finding female
board candidates with similar skill levels (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012), despite having a relatively better
supply setting.
    Ultimately, our result sheds light on the critical factors that are needed for legislators to successfully
implement board gender quotas. The evidence supports the idea that board gender quotas do not
necessarily have a negative impact on firm value, which shows that the goal of increased board gender
diversity is attainable without shareholders bearing a financial cost. By comparing results, policy
implementation details, and context with the Norwegian study (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012), we can
identify the factors that made the French experience more successful. In our study, we could see that
firms were effective in maintaining their board-specific skill level despite replacing their freely selected
board members, as indicated by the board size and tenure not being affected by the law. This evidence
can help alleviate potential concerns about board gender quotas in the corporate sector and address
worries regarding the soon-to-be-implemented EU directive on board gender diversity. The ability to
keep the board-specific skill level relatively unchanged is what we suggest enables French firms to not
deteriorate in firm value. This then can be explained by the specifics of the French gender quota.
The characteristics of the Cop´e-Zimmerman law allowed firms a total of six years and had a relatively

                                           17
lenient sanction upon failure to fulfill the quota. We argue that it was the uniqueness of the time
period and possibly the penalty level that led to French firms’ ability to gradually adapt to the new
quota and not make drastic changes at the expense of firm performance. Policymakers can draw lessons
from these findings to successfully achieve increased gender diversity in corporate boardrooms without
incurring a financial cost.

5 Conclusion

In conclusion, we find no support for the French board gender quota impacting firm value negatively.
Although the law forced an involuntary increase of female board members to meet the threshold
stipulated by the quota, we found no significant results in either the short-term study on immediate
stock returns at the time of the news announcement or the long-term study on Tobin’s Q and return
on equity. We found that board size remained roughly constant and was not increased due to the law.
Board tenure was also not significantly impacted by the quota. This likely points to the new female
directors replacing incumbent male board members instead of being hired as complementary directors.
Our results also show no significant signs of the average skill level of boards decreasing.
   This study uses methodology from Ahern and Dittmar (2012) who studied the Norwegian gender
quota, and compares results. The authors found the quota to affect firm value negatively and believed
this to stem from the fact that the quota led to a decline in the average skill level of the boards. These
results were driven by the two years before the quota threshold when firms made drastic adjustments
to fulfill the quota. We argue that our results showed no adverse effects of the French gender quota
as firms did not have to rush to fulfill the quota but could adjust smoothly to the new legislation,
as shown by the data. In contrast to the setting for Norwegian firms, which had two years to fulfill
the mandated quota, French firms had six years. Also, the French board gender quota had a softer
punishment for compliance failure than in Norway, which can reduce the risk of drastic adjustments.
This study thus shows that, contrary to prior research, a gender quota does not need to negatively
affect firm value, which may provide policymakers guidance on maintaining firm value while providing
greater gender equality in the boardroom.

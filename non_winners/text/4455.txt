The Era of Socially Responsible Investing: Performance of Swedish Socially
Responsible Funds During Market Crises

Abstract:

   Using a unique dataset of 160 Swedish equity mutual funds over the 2000-2016
   period, this paper provides a comprehensive analysis of the performance of socially
   responsible funds. Compared to characteristics-matched conventional funds, socially
   responsible funds outperform during periods of market crises. The positive abnormal
   returns of socially responsible funds during bad economic states is driven mainly by
   funds that employ negative screens. In contrast, we find little evidence of significant
   differences in performance between socially responsible funds and their conventional
   counterparts during good economic states. Lastly, we show that the choice of
   screening criteria is a significant determinant of the risk-adjusted returns of Swedish
   socially responsible funds, whilst the impact of screening intensity is trivial.

Keywords:

   Socially responsible investing, Equity mutual funds, Investments, Swedish fund
   market, Fund performance evaluation, Market crises

1. Introduction

1.1. Background

Over the past decade, socially responsible investing (SRI) has grown from a niche to a
major market segment. Increasingly, investors seek to obtain long-term competitive
financial returns in combination with positive social impact. Fundamentally, “doing good
by doing well” is no longer considered an oxymoron. Contrariwise, the global investment
community has widely acknowledged sustainability as an important driver of superior
financial returns.

As client demand has accelerated, sustainability incorporation has become imperative for
asset managers across the globe. The industry is burgeoning, and in the US alone,
sustainable mutual funds enjoyed a growth rate in excess of 38% over the 2016–2018
period (USSIF, 2018). Globally, socially responsible investing accounts for
approximately $23 trillion, which is more than one-fourth of all assets under professional
management (Boston Consulting Group, 2017).

Unsurprisingly, the increased popularity of socially responsible investing has engendered
great academic interest. The prevailing view in the global investment society is that
sustainability incorporation has a positive impact, if any, on mutual fund performance.
However, several studies oppositely suggest that sustainable investment strategies are
associated with underperformance. Knowledge concerning the performance of socially
responsible investing thus remains highly fragmented.

Problematically, the majority of academic papers neglect the range, variety, and diversity
of sustainable responsible investing strategies. At present, there exist two dominant SRI
screening strategies. The first strategy is negative screening, intended to exclude firms or
industries with undesirable ethical characteristics. The second strategy is positive
screening, wherein investors invest in stocks with desirable attributes. Additionally, SRI
comprises four major screening criteria; environmental screens, product-based screens,
ethical screens, and governance screens.

The bottom line is that socially responsible mutual funds should not be considered a
homogenous group, as the choice of SRI strategy is likely to influence fund performance.
Moreover, most studies have examined the performance of sustainable portfolios
throughout an entire evaluation period without distinguishing performance in different
market states. This is a considerable limitation as recent studies suggest that the
performance of socially responsible investing strategies is highly influenced by the state
of the market cycle (e.g., Areal et al., 2013; Nofsinger and Varma, 2014).

2. Literature review

2.1. Performance of socially responsible funds

The increased popularity of socially responsible investing has been accompanied by a
significant body of academic literature. Nevertheless, knowledge concerning socially
responsible fund performance remains inadequate. Financial papers have failed to
produce unanimous results, and at present there exists three competing academic views.

Bollen (2007) supports the “doing well by doing good” view, as he suggests that social
screens allow fund managers to identify undervalued stocks. Likewise, Derwall et al.
(2005) find that portfolios comprising environmentally clean firms enjoy positive
abnormal returns. Also, Nofsinger and Varma (2014) present evidence that socially
responsible mutual funds outperform conventional funds with similar characteristics
during periods of market crises, thus providing investors valuable downside protection.

In contrast, Geczy et al. (2003) argue that the cost of socially responsible investing is
considerable. Specifically, SRI funds apply screens that limit the full diversification
potential and thus shift the mean-variance frontier towards less favorable risk-return
tradeoffs than those of conventional portfolios. The “doing good but not well” view is
shared by Humphrey and Lee (2011), who find that the smaller subset of investment
choices dictated by the sustainability constraint engender substantial sector biases and
increase idiosyncratic risk. Furthermore, Renneboog et al. (2008) show that SRI funds
typically underperform their domestic benchmarks by 2.2% to 6.5%.

However, the most common view is unquestionably that the performance of socially
responsible funds is not significantly different from that of non-SRI funds (e.g., Mallin et
al., 1995; Bello, 2005; Bauer et al., 2006). Guenster (2012) concludes that whilst SRI
screens help fund managers finding undervalued stocks by generating valuable
information, they also exclude some positive alpha firms and limit the diversification
potential, thus ending with normal performance.

2.2. Performance of Swedish socially responsible funds

Socially responsible investment practices differ from one country to another. To the best
of our knowledge, only two academic papers analyzes the performance of Swedish SRI
funds. Renneboog et al. (2008) studies all American, Asian, and European mutual funds
and find that risk-adjusted performance of SRI funds in Sweden is between −4% and −6%
per annum, strongly indicating that Swedish investors are paying a considerable price for
ethics. In contrast, Leite et al. (2017), who focus primarily on the Swedish SRI fund
market, find no evidence of significant differences in performance between Swedish
socially responsible mutual funds and conventional mutual funds with similar
characteristics in terms of age, size and age. However, the explanatory power of the paper

                                   6
is limited as it does not distinguish between the performance of SRI funds in different
market states.

2.3. Influence of screening strategies

Leite and Cortez (2015), investigate how different investment styles affect fund
performance in different market states and find that negative screening strategies cause
significant underperformance of socially responsible funds in good economic states.
Kempf and Osthoff (2007) suggest that negative screens constrain the risk return
optimization. Moreover, Statman and Glushkov (2009) find evidence showing that
shunning controversial stocks from the portfolio worsens financial performance, while
the absence of negative screens generally enhances risk-adjusted return.

Nofsinger and Varma (2014) find that positive screens are associated with positive alphas
during periods of market turmoil but underperform conventional funds during non-crisis
periods. On the other hand, Goldreyer and Diltz (1999) suggest that positive screens
reduce the total risk of the fund and typically lead to positive abnormal returns throughout
the market cycle. Furthermore, Humphrey and Lee (2011) argue that positive screens
reduce the total risk of the fund.

2.4. Impact of screening criteria and screening intensity

Socially responsible funds consider different screening criteria in their investment
processes. According to Renneboog et al. (2008) the screening criteria of SRI funds are a
significant determinant of the risk-adjusted returns. Specifically, they suggest that whilst
governance screens have a negative impact on performance, environmental screens have
a positive impact. In contrast, Derwall et al., 2011 find that governance screens have a
profound positive effect as firms with high employee satisfaction often experience
positive abnormal returns and experience lower bankruptcy risk. Hong and Kacperczyk
(2005) argue that product screens excluding “sin” stocks has a profound negative effect
                                                                                              2
on fund performance as these stocks typically earn high risk-adjusted returns.

In terms of screening intensity, Humphrey and Lee (2011) suggest that funds with more
screens provide better risk-adjusted performance by enabling SRI fund manager to find
underpriced stocks that would otherwise not have been discovered. This is a surprising
finding because most papers find that high screening intensity limits the universe of
securities, leads to poor diversification, and reduces the risk-adjusted returns of mutual
funds (Barnett and Salomon, 2006; Capelle-Blancard and Monjon, 2014; Renneboog et
al., 2008).

3. Hypotheses

Considering the discordant findings of previous academic studies, it is difficult to
formulate trustworthy hypotheses regarding the performance of socially responsible
funds. On the one hand, SRI fund managers apply screens that limit the full diversification
potential of their investment portfolios (e.g., Geczy et al., 2003; Guenster, 2012). Thus,
socially responsible mutual funds risk being under-diversified and may underperform in
the traditional mean-variance optimization framework.

On the other hand, it has been shown that SRI funds outperform their conventional peers
during market crises (e.g. Nofsinger and Varma, 2014). For instance, it has been
suggested that socially responsible investing dampens downside risk as SRI funds
typically invest in firms with superior governance, better able to navigate the dynamic
challenges of crisis periods. Based on the above, we propose the following hypothesis:

    Hypothesis 1: Socially responsible funds outperform conventional funds with
    similar characteristics during crisis periods but underperform them during non-
    crisis periods.

In addition, we examine the performance of different screening strategies. We envision
that negative screens have a profound negative impact on mutual fund performance as
many sin stocks have historically outperformed the market (Hong and Kacperczyk, 2005).
Applying negative screens may consequently prevent fund managers from investing in
financially attractive investment opportunities.

In contrast, positive screens are likely to generate positive abnormal returns (e.g.,
Goldreyer and Diltz, 1999). Leite and Cortez (2015) show that SRI funds that use only
positive screens typically outperform SRI funds that apply negative screens. Our second
hypothesis is, therefore:

    Hypothesis 2: Positive screens generate positive abnormal returns whilst
    negative screens are associated with negative abnormal returns.

Lastly, we study the influence of screening intensity on mutual fund performance.
Although being a rather unexplored topic, existing literature generally suggests that a high
screening intensity is associated with poor diversification and low risk-adjusted returns
(Barnett and Salomon, 2006; Capelle-Blancard and Monjon, 2014; Renneboog et al.,
2008). Accordingly, we propose the following hypothesis:

    Hypothesis 3: A higher screening intensity reduces the performance of socially
    responsible funds.

4. Data

In the following section, we introduce our unique dataset of Swedish equity mutual funds.
First, we describe the processes used to identify socially responsible funds. Second, we
explain the method applied to create a convenient sample of characteristics-matched
conventional funds. Thenceforth, we introduce our performance data and discuss
potential data limitation. Finally, we present summary statistics in order to provide a
comprehensive overview of the dataset.

4.1. Identifying socially responsible funds

To determine the universe of socially responsible mutual funds domiciled in Sweden, we
rely on two separate data sources. Firstly, we use classifications from the Morningstar
Direct database. Morningstar labels funds that explicitly indicate any kind of
sustainability, impact, or ESG strategy in their prospectus or offering documents as
“sustainable overall” (Morningstar, 2018). Thus, we consider all socially responsible
investing information accessed from this database to be highly trustworthy.

Secondly, we use the information provided by Swesif’s sustainability declaration for
funds, Hållbarhetsprofilen. Essentially, Hållbarhetsprofilen is a standardized
information leaflet that allows fund companies to report how their funds take into account
sustainability in an industry-uniform way (Swesif, 2018). Problematically, all information
on the leaflet has been reported by the fund companies themselves without being reviewed
or approved by Swesif. Therefore, to verify the information, we carefully examine the
sustainability characteristics of the funds on the leaflet before adding them to our sample
of socially responsible mutual funds.

4.2. Control group and matching methodology

In order to investigate the impact of SRI on the returns of Swedish mutual funds, we
create a control group of conventional funds which are matched with SRI funds based on
fund characteristics. The matching-fund approach is employed by the great majority of
studies on the topic and allows us to distinguish the sustainable characteristics from other
determinants of performance (e.g., Goldreyer et al., 1999; Bauer et al., 2005; Bollen,
2007).

To create our matched portfolios, we employ three matching criteria. First, for each
socially responsible fund, we identify all conventional funds with similar investment

4.3. Dataset

The unique dataset used in this paper comprises 160 equity mutual funds with domicile
            7
in Sweden.  More specifically, the sample comprises 80 socially responsible funds and
80 matched conventional funds with similar characteristics in terms of investment style,
geographical focus, and age. The sample comprises 13 global categories of funds, 10
investment areas, and 45 professional fund companies.

Surprisingly, we find that all socially responsible funds in our sample use negative
screens, while only 31 of them apply positive screens. Moreover, 31 funds employ a
combination of positive and negative screens, implying that no fund uses positive screens
in isolation. This is an unexpected finding because studies on other European markets
have typically found positive screening to be the most popular SRI screening strategy
(e.g., Bollen, 2007; Leite and Cortez, 2015).

Concerning screening criteria, the vast majority of SRI mutual funds apply product related
screens, implying that funds exclude firms that derive revenue from alcohol, tobacco,
weapons, pornography, gambling, or other products deemed harmful. The second most
popular criterion is environmental impact, wherein funds consider aspects such as
climate, pollution, and carbon footprint in their investment processes. Additional
screening criteria used by the SRI funds is ethical screens and governance screens. Ethical
screens consider aspects such as community development, religious aspects, and gender
diversity whilst funds applying governance screens consider board related issues,
management practices, and employee satisfaction etc. Table 2 in section 4.6 provides
comprehensive overview of the SRI fund sample and the various SRI strategies applied.

4.5. Data limitations

The most evident limitation of our data is that we are unable to identify any obsolete SRI
funds, implying that our data is likely to suffer from survivorship bias. Survivorship bias
occurs when obsolete funds are excluded from the performance analysis (Carhart et al.,
2002). This limitation is practically inevitable as neither Hållbarhetsprofilen nor
Morningstar Direct provide SRI classifications to funds that have ceased to exist. The
possibility of a survivorship bias reduces the credibility of this paper as the performance
of SRI funds risks being overestimated.

Moreover, it can be difficult to distinguish between socially responsible funds and
conventional funds. Indeed, Duuren et al. (2016) show that many conventional fund
managers occasionally integrate sustainability information in their investment processes.
Hence, it is possible that some funds classified as conventional de facto integrate
sustainability criteria in their investment processes. Also, it is imaginable that some funds
classified as SRI funds today, have not incorporated SRI throughout the sample period.
Likewise, we cannot neglect the fact that some SRI funds most likely have altered their
SRI strategies over the course of our period of interest.

5. Methodology

In the following section, we carefully outline the methodology employed to examine the
performance of socially responsible investing strategies across different market states.
Specifically, we start by identifying crisis periods and thenceforth thoroughly describe
our performance evaluation models. Lastly, we run robustness tests in order to confirm
the validity of our results.

5.1. Identifying crisis periods

In order to explore the performance of socially responsible investing strategies over
different market states, we first need to identify crisis periods. Whilst previous papers
typically use an “eye-ball” approach, we opt for a more accurate statistical method, as
suggested by Pagan and Sossounov (2003). Accordingly, we start by locating peaks and
troughs by finding points that are higher or lower than a window of surrounding points.
Specifically, we use the following algorithms:

5.3. Performance evaluation models

Having created equally weighted monthly time-series returns for each portfolio of funds,
we combine three complementing models to estimate the risk-adjusted performance of
socially responsible funds compared to their conventional counterparts. Fundamentally,
we base our analysis on the capital asset pricing model (CAPM) and add the risk factors
from the Fama and French (1993) three-factor model and the Carhart (1997) four-factor
                                                                              15
model respectively to enhance the explanatory power of our regressions.  This yields a
reliable explanation of the variation in returns and, thus, a credible assessment of the risk-
adjusted performance for the different fund categories (Leite and Cortez, 2015). The aim
of this thesis is to analyze the performance of the SRI funds. Hence, when running our
regressions with the CAPM, the Fama-French three-factor model and Carhart four-factor
model the coefficient we are going to primarily focus on is alpha. The alpha coefficient
indicates whether funds outperform their factor benchmarks. We use the following
hypothesis:

𝐻0: 𝛼 = 0

𝐻1: 𝛼 ≠ 0

Essentially, the null hypothesis indicates that no relationship exists between the factor
and the risk-adjusted return, whilst the alternative hypothesis indicates that a relationship
exists. We explore the hypothesis in different market states by following the methodology
of Nofsinger and Varma (2014). In further detail, we separate non-crisis and crisis periods
by including two dummy variables. This method allows us to separate crisis and non-
crisis alpha coefficients while still analyzing the entire set of monthly time series of
returns.

5.3.1. The Capital Asset Pricing model

The capital asset pricing model is the first model that we apply to determine the risk-
adjusted abnormal return of the equally weighted portfolios in crisis and non-crisis
periods. In brief, the CAPM model explains the relationship between systematic risk and
expected return. Unlike idiosyncratic risk, systematic risk cannot be diversified away and
must, therefore, be compensated for by the market. Return in excess of what is predicted
by the CAPM model is described as the fund’s alpha. Thus, the alpha can be used as a
measure of fund performance. The CAPM model is presented below:

5.4. Robustness tests

Throughout this analysis, we use ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions. Our regression
analysis is unbiased and efficient under five assumptions: linearity in parameters, no
perfect collinearity, strict exogeneity, homoscedasticity, and no serial correlation. Thus,
to verify our results, we perform a number of statistical tests. Firstly, we run the Variance
Inflation Factor (VIF) test to investigate if our data suffer from multicollinearity.
Subsequently, we use the Breusch-Pagan test to test for heteroscedasticity and ultimately
the Durbin-Watson test to preclude serial correlation.

5.4.1. Variance Inflation Factor test for multicollinearity

Firstly, we study whether our dataset is exposed to multicollinearity, which occurs when
the independent variables are highly correlated. Problematically, multicollinearity implies

                                  18
that it is hard to separate the effects of different explanatory variables on the dependent
variable. In order to ensure that our data does not suffer from serial correlation, we run
the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) test. Luckily, the test indicates that our dataset is not
                                  16
exposed to serial correlation.

5.4.2. Breusch-Pagan test for heteroscedasticity

Heteroskedasticity implies that the variance of the error term, given the explanatory
variables, is not constant. We use the Breusch-Pagan test to investigate whether our data
is heteroskedastic. The test does not reject the null hypothesis of homoscedasticity,
                                                                                                        17
indicating that our data most likely does not suffer from any heteroscedasticity.
Nonetheless, we run the regressions with robust standard errors to account for any
potential problems resulting from heteroscedasticity.

5.4.3. Durbin-Watson test for autocorrelation

Furthermore, we run the Durbin-Watson test to preclude serial correlation between the
error terms in our time-series data. Fortunately, we are unable to reject the null hypothesis
                                                        18
that the standard errors are serially uncorrelated.   Consequently, we find that all
assumptions of OLS that we have tested for appear to hold, which suggests that our
regressions are unbiased and trustworthy.

SRI mutual funds and conventional funds are somewhat state dependent. For instance,
Leite and Cortez (2015) find that SRI and conventional funds show significant differences
in risk exposures during non-crisis periods but exhibit relatively similar investment styles
during crises.

7. Conclusion

Academic interest has followed the rapid growth of the socially responsible investing
industry. Nevertheless, knowledge on the topic remains inconclusive. The main
contribution of this paper is to fill the knowledge gap by comparing the performance of
Swedish socially responsible funds relative to characteristics-matched conventional funds
across different market states. Additionally, the paper provides important insights into the
performance of different screening strategies, screening criteria, and screening intensities.

In the first part of the analysis, we investigate the performance of Swedish socially
responsible funds over the 2000-2016 period. In short, we find that the alphas of socially
responsible funds and conventional funds do not differ notably in good economic states.
However, during periods of economic crisis, SRI funds significantly outperform their
conventional counterparts. Fundamentally, the incorporation of socially responsible
investing appears to dampen downside risk. Kahneman and Tversky (1979) Prospect
Theory reveals that investors are more negatively impacted by losses than they are
positively impacted by gains of similar magnitudes. Consequently, we believe that the
asymmetric performance pattern of socially responsible funds would be valued highly by
investors seeking downside protection.

Concerning the diversity of screening strategies used by socially responsible funds, we
find that funds employing only negative screens significantly outperform conventional
funds during periods of market crises. Thus, in contrast with Nofsinger and Varma (2014),
we find that SRI outperformance during crisis periods is driven primarily by negative
screens. Seemingly, negative screens help fund managers exclude the firms most likely
to suffer badly during economic crises. One explanation for this phenomenon is that
people take more notice of negative corporate behaviors during poor economic times than
in good times (Hirshleifer, 2008). Another conceivable explanation is that non-SRI firms
generally enjoy poorer reputations, implying that risk-averse investors are less willing to
hold their stocks during periods of market turmoil.

Additionally, we investigate the performance of various screening criteria. This is a very
interesting topic, as it has been suggested that product-related screens have a profound
negative impact on mutual fund performance (Hong and Kacperczyk, 2009). We do not
find any evidence in support of this view. In fact, our empirical tests show that SRI funds
using product related screens outperform significantly during non-crisis periods whilst
performing insignificantly better than their conventional peers during crisis periods.
Another interesting observation is that funds screening on governance seem to perform
significantly better than conventional funds during crisis periods. According to Nofsinger
and Varma (2014), one explanation is that firms with good governance may be more
capable of navigating the dynamic challenges of crisis periods. The only screening
criterium that appears to have a negative impact, if any, on fund performance is ethical
screens.

                                  30
In our final analysis, we examine the performance of SRI funds using different screening
intensities. Our hypothesis was that there exists a negative relationship between screening
intensity and mutual fund performance. However, we find little evidence in support of
this view. As a matter of fact, even the SRI funds employing the highest screening
intensity, significantly outperform their conventional peers during non-crisis periods. We,
therefore, conclude that a high screening intensity does not restrain Swedish mutual fund
managers from diversify away idiosyncratic risk and is not associated with low risk-
adjusted returns.

To summarize, we believe that this paper offers some imperative insights to Swedish
investors seeking to combine positive social impact with competitive financial returns.
Most fundamentally, we show that socially responsible funds offer investors downside
protection during periods of market crises. Likewise, we provide evidence indicating that
negative screens drive the outperformance of SRI funds during periods of crises.
Nevertheless, it is important to emphasize that this paper has a number of significant
limitations. Most notably, our sample is likely to suffer from a considerable survivorship
bias as we were not able to locate any obsolete SRI funds. Conclusively, the performance
of SRI mutual funds domiciled in Sweden must be examined further before drawing any
definite conclusions.

To conclude this paper, we share some ideas on future research. Firstly, throughout our
research, we have learned that it is very difficult to distinguish between socially
responsible funds and conventional funds. Indeed, when studying the Morningstar Direct
database, we noticed that SRI funds do not necessarily enjoy higher sustainability scores
than their conventional counterparts. Therefore, it would be very interesting for future
research to examine to what extent socially responsible funds differ from conventional
funds in terms of holdings and investment styles. Another interesting topic for future
papers to consider is the impact of non-financial crises on SRI fund performance. Indeed,
it is imaginable that non-financial crises such as natural disasters influence the demand
for sustainable stocks and thus influence the relative performance of SRI funds. The
bottom line is that despite the great body of literature, there exists a great need for further
research.

9.3. Robustness tests

We conduct several tests in order to test the robustness of our results. In this section we
give examples of the results from the tests performed. Firstly, we run the Variance
Inflation Factor (VIF) test to investigate if our data suffer from multicollinearity.
Subsequently, we use the Breusch-Pagan test to test for heteroscedasticity and ultimately
the Durbin-Watson test to preclude serial correlation. In this section we give examples of
the results obtained from running the test on our equally weighted monthly time-series
returns based on the general fund types (SRI, and Conventional). Our results remain
similar for all equally weighted portfolios of funds in our sample (screening strategy,
screening criteria, and screening intensity).

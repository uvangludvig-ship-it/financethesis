
In The Eye of Climate Change: Air Pollution and Investor Attention

Abstract
   Climate change unfolds gradually over decades, making its impacts difficult to
   perceive in daily life, yet it poses profound challenges for financial markets. Air
   pollution, by contrast, is acutely salient to individuals and contains components such
   as black carbon and sulfates that directly contribute to global warming. This paper
   investigates whether local experiences of abnormal air pollution act as a “wake-up
   call” that heightens investor attention to climate risks and influences capital
   allocation. Using the stock market as a natural laboratory, we examine the
   performance of brown versus green firms through a long–short
   emissions-minus-clean portfolio, focusing on exchange cities where pollution shocks
   are most likely to affect investor behavior. We extend the analysis to the European
   context, where pollution remains a relevant concern with documented cognitive
   effects. Our study addresses two central questions: (1) does abnormal air pollution
   increase investors’ attention to climate change, and (2) how do local pollution shocks
   affect the returns of firms with differing climate sensitivities? We contribute to the
   growing literature on the pricing of climate risks, offering new evidence on how
   environmental shocks shape the divergence in returns between green and brown
   firms. This work advances understanding of how environmental salience interacts
   with financial markets and informs debates on whether climate-friendly firms benefit
   from their impact on the environment materially.

Keywords
   PM2.5, PM10, air pollution, climate change, global warming, investor attention

1. Introduction

Climate change is among the most pressing global challenges facing the world today.
Unlike short-lived shocks, it unfolds gradually over decades, making its impacts
difficult to perceive in daily life. It encompasses phenomena such as rising global
temperatures, sea-level rise, and an increasing frequency of extreme weather events,
while global warming refers specifically to the long-term rise in average temperatures.
Despite its slow-moving nature, climate change has profound implications for financial
markets, which allocate capital toward or away from firms with varying environmental
profiles. The stock market responds not only to fundamentals but also to investor
experiences and perceptions, raising the question of whether these experiences can be
harnessed to address climate risks through capital allocation.
       Air pollution is a critical driver of climate change and, unlike climate change
itself, is acutely salient to individuals. Particulate matter (PM2.5 and PM10) is
perceptible through visibility, smell, and irritation, and both pollutants contain
components such as black carbon, sulfates, and nitrates that contribute directly to global
warming (WHO, 2025; Shindell et al., 2012). Thus, air pollution simultaneously
degrades local environments and accelerates climate change. Importantly, pollution may
act as a “wake-up call” by making environmental risks tangible, potentially shifting
investor attention toward climate change.
       This raises key questions for the financial market: can local experiences of
pollution alter investor perceptions of climate risk, and do these shifts translate into
differential pricing of firms with varying climate sensitivities? The stock market
provides a natural laboratory to examine these dynamics. In particular, we study
whether abnormal pollution events influence the performance of brown versus green
firms, as captured by a long–short brown-minus-green portfolio. Our analysis focuses
on exchange cities, where local pollution shocks may shape the behavior of investors
trading on those exchanges, including blockholders likely located near the exchange.
       Although much of the existing literature on pollution and finance has
concentrated on countries facing severe air quality challenges, such as China and India,
relatively little attention has been devoted to the European context. Yet recent evidence
demonstrates that air pollution remains a relevant concern across Europe, with
documented effects on cognition and decision-making. Existing finance research has
primarily examined pollution’s impact on market efficiency and investor behavior, for
example, how exposure influences trading performance and biases (Li, Massa, Zhang,
and Zhang, 2021; Levy and Yagil, 2011). Far less work has considered pollution as a
salient environmental signal that may reallocate capital across firms with differing
climate sensitivities. By situating our analysis in Europe, we contribute to filling this
gap and provide new insights into how environmental salience interacts with financial
markets. Specifically, we address the following two research questions:

    1. Does abnormal air pollution increase investors’ attention to climate change?
    2. How do local experiences of abnormal air pollution affect the stock returns of
        firms with different climate sensitivities?
We study 36/33 exchange cities across 32/29 countries in Europe between 2012 and
2024. We study their monthly abnormal levels of PM2.5 and PM10 and their attention
to climate change. Then, we examine how abnormal levels of PM2.5 and PM10 affect
monthly returns of a long-short emissions-minus-clean (EMC) portfolio. We expect to
see heightened attention to climate change during higher abnormal levels of pollution
due to the salience of our chosen pollutants, as well as negative returns on the EMC
portfolio, in line with existing literature on “green” stocks outperforming “brown”
stocks during periods of increased perceived climate risk, increased awareness of
climate risk and climate change, greeniums and the costs to “brown” firms due to
climate risk and regulation.
       This paper builds upon the expanding literature on the pricing of climate risks.
Recent work examines whether and how climate risks are reflected in asset prices across
different markets, including the stock market (Bolton and Kacperczyk, 2021, 2023), the
corporate bond market (Huynh and Xia, 2021), and the real estate market (Baldauf,
Garlappi, and Yannelis, 2020). Following the increased salience of climate risks, returns
may diverge between “green” and “brown” firms. The central question, whether
climate-friendly firms benefit from lower costs of capital (a “greenium”) or whether
investors demand a premium to hold carbon-intensive firms, remains unresolved.
Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021, 2023) document a carbon risk premium, whereas
Aswani, Raghunandan, and Rajgopal (2024) find no effect of emissions on stock
returns. Other contributions highlight challenges in inference, pointing to noisy ESG
ratings (Berg, Kolbel, and Rigobon, 2022) and cross-country heterogeneity (Alves,
Krüger, and van Dijk, 2024). We contribute to this literature by examining how climate
shocks, specifically shocks to air pollution, affect the pricing of stocks across an
emission differential.
        Furthermore, our findings connect to research on the role of behavioral biases in
how environmental conditions influence investors’ decision making. Goetzmann, Kim,
Kumar, and Wang (2014) show that cloudier days affect perceptions of mispricing and
trading decisions among institutional investors, while Hirshleifer and Shumway (2003)
demonstrate that sunshine hours impact stock returns. Similarly, Alok, Kumar, and
Wemers (2020) find that institutional investors overreact to large climatic disasters by
underweighting disaster-zone stocks, a behavior consistent with salience bias. Inspired
by health science evidence that air pollution impairs cognition, Li, Massa, Zhang, and
Zhang (2021) document that air pollution intensifies disposition effects among
investors.
       A more recent strand of literature highlights the financial relevance of air
pollution itself. Exposure to poor air quality has been shown to influence investor
attention, mood, and risk-taking, generating systematic patterns in trading and returns.
Dong, Fisman, Wang, and Xu (2019) provide evidence that analysts exposed to higher
pollution during site visits issue more pessimistic earnings forecasts, attributing this to
mood effects. Experimental psychology links bad mood to increased risk aversion,
while financial economics shows that higher risk aversion leads investors to sell stocks,
and lower risk aversion encourages buying (Kamstra, Kramer, and Levi, 2003). Levy
and Yagil (2011) further investigate whether these pollution-induced mood effects
translate into stock returns. In contrast to this literature, our paper focuses on whether
salient pollution experiences can draw investor attention to climate change and thereby
affect the pricing of brown versus green stocks.
       While Liu, Kang, Guo, and Sun (2021) study how pollution-induced changes in
investor attention toward specific firms translate into stock price movements, our
analysis considers a broader information channel. We show that air pollution does not
necessarily heighten attention to climate risks nor consistently generate return effects.
Consistent with this distinction, Fisman, Ghosh, Sarkar, and Zhang (2023) find that
increased accessibility to air-quality information, rather than local pollution exposure
itself, shifts investment away from brown industries. Taken together, these findings
suggest that pollution episodes do not reliably raise climate attention or produce return
responses, and our paper provides new insights into the limits of pollution’s effect in
financial markets.
       Finally, our work is closely related to Choi, Gao, and Jiang (2020), who examine
how updated beliefs about global warming following local experiences of abnormally
warm temperatures are reflected in asset prices and trading behavior. This is consistent
with related work in behavioral economics, showing that personal experiences with
global warming increase perceptions of climate risk (Zaval et al., 2014; Broomell,
Budescu, and Por, 2015). We view our study as complementary, focusing instead on
whether extreme local air pollution alerts investors to climate change and how such
experiences affect stock market outcomes. Understanding when and how investors
respond to local air pollution is important, as it can inform the design of more effective
strategies to mitigate climate change by taking air pollution into account. Indeed, there
is growing recognition in policymaking that air pollution and climate change are deeply
interconnected and should be addressed jointly rather than in isolation.

The structure of this paper is as follows. Section 2 introduces hypotheses and
methodology. Section 3 describes data collection and variable construction. Section 4
presents the empirical results. Section 5 highlights key findings and limitations. Section
6 concludes.
2. Hypotheses & Methodology

We follow the methodology used by Choi, Gao, and Jiang in their article Attention to
Global Warming (2020). While they study the stock market implications of temperature
shocks, this study investigates the impact of air pollution shocks. In particular, we
would like to identify investor reaction to climate change in times of unusually high
local air pollution. In fact, research shows that air pollution and climate change are
closely linked (see e.g., Ramanathan and Carmichael, 2008; Shindell et al., 2012).
However, comparatively few empirical studies investigate whether and how the public
perceives air pollution as a driver of climate change.
       We further deviate from Choi et al. (2020), who study several exchange cities
across the world. An advantage of using an international setting is that the researchers
are able to estimate people’s opinions about temperature shocks in different parts of the
world, and examine how the general public realizes and responds to such impacts of
climate change. Given that climate change is a global phenomenon, understanding
collective beliefs and actions across the world are important determinants of the efficacy
of climate policies and campaigns.
        In this study, we limit our focus to 36/33 cities across 32/29 countries in Europe.
We examine the collective beliefs and actions of a large proportion of the European
public. This means a slightly narrowed focus from that of Choi et al. (2020). While they
investigate global market reactions to local temperature shocks, we study whether
markets with relatively strong environmental regulation recognize the impact of air
pollution shocks on climate change. In Europe, research on the financial implications of
air pollution remains limited. However, given Europe’s substantial geographical
variation and differences in national regulatory frameworks compared to most of the
Asian countries studied in prior literature, a European-focused investigation offers
valuable insights into investor behavior under environmental stress.

2.1 Air pollution and investor attention

Investor reaction to climate change is first measured by an exchange city’s Google
Search Volume Index (SVI) of the topic “global warming”, which proxies for the local
people’s attention to climate change. The idea follows Da, Engelberg, and Gao (2011),
who use SVI of stock tickers to study investor attention. Given an understanding of the
interrelationship between air pollution and climate change, the appearance of abnormal
levels of air pollution may induce investors to consider climate change, thus searching
for more details in, for instance, Google. Related literature suggests that people adapt
beliefs on their experiences of air pollution (Li, Massa, Zhang, and Zhang, 2021; Liu,
Kang, Guo, and Sun, 2021; Dong, Fisman, Wang, and Xu, 2019). Chang, Huang, and
Wang (2018) document a significant effect of daily air pollution levels on the decision
to purchase or cancel health insurance, acknowledging the increased perceptions of
health risks following air pollution episodes. In addition, the study of Fisman, Ghosh,
Sarkar, and Zhang (2023) shows that releasing information about the local air quality
leads retail investors in China to reduce their investments in brown stocks, with
particularly pronounced effects amongst younger investors who tend to be more
sensitive to climate concerns. Their results provide support for the view that salience of
air pollution conditions updates investors’ beliefs about climate risks, thereby increasing
investor preferences towards green investments. In our case, abnormal air pollution may
serve as a signal of deteriorating environmental conditions and, by extension, poor local
performance with respect to climate-related objectives. Such shocks can prompt
investors to reassess the likelihood and financial relevance of climate risks, thereby
increasing their demand for climate-related information. This mechanism would align
with evidence that salient environmental conditions update beliefs and redirect attention
(Choi et al., 2020). Consequently, air pollution shocks are expected to trigger greater
information acquisition about climate change, as reflected in elevated search activity.
Contingent on this analysis, we test the following hypothesis:

       (i) Abnormal air pollution increases investors’ attention to climate change.

2.2 Air pollution and stock return

Next, we examine the reaction of investors in the stock market. A growing body of
literature shows that air pollution could affect stock markets through multiple channels.
First, air pollution could trigger a number of physiological and psychological changes in
investors. A medical study has proven that exposure to urban air pollutants could trigger
increases in bodily cortisol levels (Tomei et al., 2003). Individuals with higher bodily
cortisol levels are less likely to engage in risk-taking behavior (Rosenblitt et al., 2001).
Hence, air pollution could increase investors’ risk aversion, making individual investors
buy fewer stocks (Lepori, 2016; Wu and Lu, 2020). Moreover, higher air pollution
levels are documented to make fund managers pessimistic, finally affecting their trading
behavior and the stock prices of their shareholdings (Wu et al., 2020). These findings
suggest that air pollution may impact investor behavior and thereby the stock market
through mechanisms unrelated to fundamentals, including cognitive performance,
preferences, and affect.
       Second, periods of abnormally high air pollution may heighten the salience of
environmental risks for investors. Salience refers to the tendency for an individual’s
attention to be drawn to particular prominent features, which then exert a
disproportionate influence on decision-making (Chang, Huang, & Wang, 2018).There is
a substantial body of empirical studies that finds evidence of salience in the field (e.g.,
Bordalo et al., 2013, 2014; Koszegi and Szeidl, 2013). Choi et al. (2020) conjectures
that retail investors have limited attention and are therefore more likely to react to
abnormal weather events. They discover that people revise their climate change beliefs
upward when the local temperature is abnormally warm, and conclude that unusually
warm weather is a salient event that attracts attention from and affects behavior of
individual investors. Similarly, unusually high levels of air pollution may also attract
investor attention. On recognizing the impact of air pollution on global warming and
thereby the implied risk of climate change, socially responsible investors may avoid
environmentally unfriendly firms, in line with how sin stocks are shunned by
norm-constrained investors (Hong and Kacperczyk 2009).
       This suggests that air pollution may affect stock return through its impact on
investors’ preferences toward green stocks (Fisman et al., 2023). Indeed, Pástor,
Stambaugh, and Taylor (2022) find that during periods of unexpected concerns about
climate change, green firms’ stock prices rise while brown firms’ prices fall, due to
increased investor demand for greener firms, or increased customer demand for greener
products. As a driver of climate change, air pollution might as well contribute to climate
change concerns. In addition, when air pollution is unusually high and the risks of
climate change are more salient or top of mind, investors may update their own
valuation of firms.
       Investors may believe that the future cash flows of climate-sensitive firms are
negatively impacted because climate change can disrupt production processes, increase
the costs associated with future emissions, or lead to stricter emissions regulations. For
example, fossil fuel firms are particularly vulnerable to carbon pricing policies
(Krueger, Sautner, & Starks, 2020). The introduction (or anticipation) of carbon pricing
can change cash-flow expectations and alter expected returns (Jaccard, Kockerols, and
Schüler, 2025). Liu, Kang, Guo, and Sun (2021) finds that air pollution reduces the
stock prices of polluting companies listed on China’s A-share market, which is known
to be policy-driven. They argue one potential mechanism is that air pollution can force
the government to introduce environmental policies which may influence investors’
strategies.
       In order to understand the role of investors’ attention to climate change on the
stock market’s reactions to air pollution, we study monthly size-adjusted stock returns
of firms with different sensitivities to climate change under abnormally high air
pollution levels. If investors start to recognize the impact of climate change through air
pollution shocks on financial markets, they may buy firms with low climate sensitivity
and sell those with high climate sensitivity, leading a long-short portfolio of high-low
sensitivity firms to earn negative returns. Based on our analysis, we test the following
hypothesis:

   (ii) Air pollution directly leads to the underperformance of brown stocks relative to
                                      green stocks.
3. Data and Variables

3.1 Data collection

Air pollution
We obtain daily and hourly measures of two pollutants, PM2.5 and PM10, from the
                                                                                     1
European Environment Agency (EEA) Air Quality Download Service  and from
corresponding with EEA Spatial Data Infrastructure. The input data used in building
these daily observations contain air quality records from over 83,000 air quality
monitoring stations across Europe since 2002, with significantly lower coverage dating
back to 1999 in some cities. By using the location coordinates, we identify air quality
monitors in the city of 36 European stock exchanges. As a benchmark, Choi et al.
(2020) study 74 cities globally that have major stock exchanges.
       The sample obtained covers the period from January 1, 2002 to 31 December,
2024. Estimating abnormal air pollution requires 10 years of data before the actual test
period, therefore our main test period is restricted to 2012 to 2024, which differs from
the test period from 2001 to 2017 used by Choi et al. (2020). These two time periods
differ mainly in that the 2001–2017 period captures a stage during which climate
change was increasingly discussed but still transitioning into a financial concern,
whereas the 2012–2024 period reflects a stage where climate change is already widely
perceived as a global challenge, with investors, regulators, and firms responding to
rising expectations around environmental performance. Several studies document an
increasing awareness of risks and concerns tied to climate change after the Paris
Agreement in 2015 (see, e.g., Bolton and Kacperczyk, 2021; Pástor, Stambaugh, Taylor,
2022). Given this is within our test period, it can potentially affect our results. One
important concern about including 2020–2022 in the test period is that it overlaps with
large-scale macroeconomic events, the most notable being the COVID-19 pandemic in
2020 and the Russia-Ukraine war in 2022. This raises concerns about confounding
events, with the risk that the estimated effect of air pollution shocks may be influenced
by overlapping structural shocks from the COVID-19 pandemic and the Russia-Ukraine
war. To account for this, we use year-month fixed effects. Time-fixed effects can
remove the effects of confounding events that are constant across all units at a specific
time point, such as a national economic recession or a pandemic. By including time
fixed effects, we control for any shocks or trends that affect all observations at a
particular time, ensuring that any remaining effects are due to changes that vary within
units over time.
       The choice of pollutants is motivated by the findings of Chang et al. (2018).
They show that air pollution, measured by PM2.5, significantly influences individuals’
decision to purchase or cancel health insurance, providing evidence for projection bias

and salience. In our analysis, we examine the perceived salience of air pollution and its

       However, studying the salience of air pollution for financial markets would
ideally take into account the physical location of the traders whose decisions drive stock
prices and their exposure to air pollution. A deviation from the ideal dataset would
introduce the possibility that any effect of local air pollution shocks on the relative
performance of high-emission versus low-emission shocks may be more difficult to
detect, as trading activity from individuals located outside the affected area may
attenuate the results. Since we cannot observe the stock market traders’ actual locations
nor their direct air pollution exposure, we must assume that a substantial fraction of
trading activity, particularly the activity that influences stock prices, is undertaken by
traders physically located in or near the exchange city. This is reasonable given that
exchange cities are important cities where many investors are located, and prices are
affected by domestic investors (Chan, Hameed, and Lau, 2003), who most likely are
exposed to local environmental conditions. We therefore study the impacts of shifts in
environmental conditions in European exchange cities, following the approach of Choi
et al. (2020).

       Similar to SVI, we are limited to country-level measures of environmental
regulation, news coverage and news sentiment. We use these country-level measures to
proxy for the local perception of these conditions by the people in the exchange cities of
the country. This assumes, for instance, that climate news articles or in one city might
still raise attention to and concern about the climate among people in other cities. In
countries with greater land area, we expect inhabitants to be more spread and less
densely populated, therefore our assumption will hold weaker. In larger countries where
the population is more dispersed, this assumption will be weaker. However, as most
exchange cities are important cities with high populations, concentrated capital, and
extensive media coverage (Choi et al., 2020), it may remain plausible to assume that the
local activity in the exchange cities is captured by the overall national signals. Although
deviations from the ideal city-level datasets might introduce measurement error, the
country-level proxies should still capture meaningful variation in the local public
attention and concern.

News media
We use Global Data on Events, Location, and Tone (GDELT) to collect country-level
                                                                                            6
data that assigns scores to news coverage and news tone across 36 countries.  The
GDELT project is considered one of the largest and most comprehensive open
databases, integrating real-time translation of media from over 100 languages. The
underlying news sources cover over 1,000 outlets, such as BBC, Corriere della Sera,
and Aftonbladet. These outlets are highly visible to market participants within each
country, making them well suited to capturing the prominence of climate change–related
discussion that investors can access at minimal cost. Additionally, GDELT evaluates the
tone of news coverage using advanced sentiment lexicons that classify positively and
negatively charged language. By inferring sentiment directly from textual features
rather than relying on a fixed, pre-defined emotion dictionary, this methodology helps
limit prediction bias stemming from subjective judgments (Schumaker et al., 2012).
      There are two types of databases in GDELT, the Global Knowledge Graph
(GKG) and the Events database. In this paper, we use the GKG database to extract daily
European news articles containing the key words in the Climate Change Vocabulary
(CCV) constructed by Engle, Giglio, Kelly, Lee, and Stroebel (2020), their respective
country of publication, and the average tone score of these climate change news
published in the country for a given day. The CCV is a compilation of terms and their
corresponding frequencies with which they appear in an aggregated corpus of
seventy-four authoritative climate change texts produced by governmental agencies and
                       7
research institutions.  However, using GDELT in this way presents a limitation. Terms
that are prevalent across several news articles, as well as those that occur only rarely
within a particular article, are less informative about the content of an individual article.
Rather, the most informative terms are those that appear infrequently overall across
news articles but frequently within a specific article under consideration (Engle et al.,

       To reduce this risk, we restrict our sample to news articles containing only the

texts.  These high-frequency terms are more likely to be core and topic-defining. While
this approach does not eliminate misclassification entirely, it narrows the set of included
articles to those more plausibly centered on climate-related content. Using the GKG
database, we also collect the daily number, country of publication, and average tone
score of European news articles containing the keyword “air pollution”, which is not
included in the CCV of Engle et al. (2020).
       Using news media coverage as a measure of the public’s attention to and
concern about climate change relies on the assumption that discussions about climate
change or air pollution become more frequent when the perceived climate risk rises. The
news media coverage measure implicitly treats the absence of climate change news as
indicative of good news. Although this assumption is plausible, it may lead to
measurement error by conflating discussions of positive climate news (e.g., news about
new mitigation technologies) with increases in the perceived climate risk (Engle et al.,
2020).
       To address this possible concern, we therefore collect the average tone score of
the news articles published on a given day, inspired by Engle et al. (2020). In
constructing climate risk hedge portfolios, Engle et al. (2020) quantify climate risk
using two news-based indices, one of which is specifically intended to capture negative
climate change news. Our data set differs from that of Engle et al. (2020), whose
methodology is used in the construction of our sentiment measure for the news articles
on climate change and air pollution, in two ways. First, Engle et al. (2020) obtain news
data through the data analytics vendor Crimson Hexagon, which relies on news sources
including The Wall Street Journal, The New York Times, and The Washington Post. We
rely on the GKG database provided by GDELT, with coverage beyond top outlets.
Second, the sentiment index of Engle et al. (2020) is constructed as the share of articles
published on a given day that have been assigned to the topic “climate change” and
classified as exhibiting “negative sentiment”. With GDELT, we similarly observe the
sentiment of individual news articles, only the aggregate tone of articles published on
the same day. The article tone, ranging between –100 and 100, is a score defined as
from the difference between positive and negative words divided by total words (Shen,
Xia, Shuai, and Gao, 2022). Accordingly, a limitation of the average tone measure is
that a tone score of zero may reflect either neutral language or the aggregation of
strongly positive and strongly negative words that offset one another in the articles.

       Moreover, we follow Choi et al. (2020) in using MSCI ESG Ratings to access
firm-level carbon emission scores. The carbon emission scores are industry adjusted,
enabling comparisons across industries. Reported on a scale from 0 to 10 for publicly
listed firms only, a higher carbon emission score indicates stronger ESG performance.
Firms assigned MSCI carbon emission scores between 0 and 3 (7 and 10) in the
previous calendar year are categorized as high- (low-) emission firms. We consider the
previous calendar year because investors do not have the MSCI emission scores for the
current year, with the latest scores being based on the previous year’s firm actions. The
data coverage on MSCI carbon emission scores is limited to 2012–2022, and only a
smaller subset of stocks listed on European exchanges. Unless otherwise stated, firms
are classified into high or low emission based on the IPCC definitions throughout the
paper.

Environmental regulation
To proxy for the stringency of environmental regulation, we employ the OECD
Environmental Policy Stringency (EPS) Index, a widely adopted policy analysis
measure developed by Botta and Koźluk (2014). The choice of proxy is based on the
conviction that it is, to date, the least imperfect proxy for environmental regulation
stringency in the context of cross-country comparisons over relatively long periods in
Europe. Following its revision in 2022, the index provides annual measures of
environmental policy stringency for the period 1990–2020 across 40 countries,
encompassing 13 environmental policy instruments focusing on primarily climate
change and air pollution mitigation. These instruments are organized into three
composite sub-indices: market-based measures such as taxation on CO₂, NOx, SOx, and
diesel fuel, CO2 and renewable energy certificates, regulatory, non-market instruments
such as emission limit standards for NOx, SOx, and particulate matter, as well as limits
on sulphur content in diesel, and lastly technology-oriented policies, measured through
public R&D spending and support for the deployment of solar and wind energy. Index

The literature notes that DataStream may suffer from data errors. To account for
potential data errors, we follow Choi et al. (2020), Hou, Karolyi, and Kho (2011), and
Ince and Porter (2006) in excluding monthly raw stock returns exceeding 300% that
reversed within one month, as well as zero monthly returns, which arise because
DataStream repeats the last available stock price for delisted firms. The remaining raw
stock returns are winsorized at the upper and lower 2.5% tails within each
exchange-month combination. We then use the natural logarithm of the raw return and
compute the size-adjusted return as the raw return minus the average raw return of
stocks in the same size quintile within the same exchange and month. Finally, following
IPCC definitions, we construct two city-level portfolios. Portfolio EMISSIONi comprises
all high-emission firms located in city i, while portfolio CLEANi includes the remaining
low-emission firms in that city. All portfolios are constructed using equal weights and
value weights. The portfolio return, expressed in percentage term, is computed as the
average raw or size-adjusted return of stocks in each portfolio in month t, using equal or
value weights. The average size-adjusted returns of stocks included in the portfolios
EMISSIONi and CLEANi are denoted as RET(EMISSION) and RET(CLEAN),
respectively. The corresponding long-short portfolio, EMCi (EMISSIONi minus
CLEANi), is constructed by taking a long position in EMISSIONi and a short position in
CLEANi. We then calculate the raw and size-adjusted EMC portfolio return, denoted
EMC(raw) and EMC(adj), respectively.
       Size-adjusted returns are used as opposed to only raw returns to account for
market capitalization. In replicating Choi et al. (2020), we observe that their measure is
used to estimate a quantity similar to a quintile-based excess return. This helps scale
potentially large raw numbers into meaningful “excess returns” that reflect the true
performance of the firm beyond just what its firm size drives. In addition, this helps
evaluate a firm’s performance by removing the effect of its size, allowing for a more
direct comparison of returns between companies of different market capitalizations.

Climate change news coverage
Inspired by Pástor, Stambaugh, Taylor (2022) and Ardia, Bluteau, Boudt, and
Inghelbrecht (2022), we include a regression analysis with interaction effects taking into
account variables contributing to climate change concern. This represents an extension
of Choi et al. (2020), who only focus on investor attention in relation to local abnormal
temperature. Because of high public awareness about climate change in Europe
(European Commission, 2025), considering the extent to which climate change
information affects investors’ reaction to local abnormal air pollution will positively
contribute to our paper and add valuable insights.
       We use the total number of daily news articles on climate change keywords
provided by GDELT. In order to measure shocks in the quantity of climate change news,
we average the daily values for the total number of news articles containing chosen
keywords to the monthly level, and then construct values of the variable
ClimateNewsCoverageChangeit, as the log changes in the total number of climate
change news articles published in country i in month t, adjusted for seasonality, using
Eq.(1). There are two advantages of using Eq.(1) to measure shocks in quantity of
climate change news: (i) subtracting the log change for the month-of-year adjusts for
seasonality and removes variations in number of climate change news articles that may
occur due to seasonal factors or cyclic events (Shanahan, 2017), and (ii) calculating the
log change removes the potential persistence in the article news count (Shen et al.,
2022). This allows the ClimateNewsCoverageChange values to exclusively capture
unexpected shocks to climate change news.
       In line with research (Shanahan, 2017), we assume that climate change
discussions in news media increase around climate-relevant events such as heatwaves,
climate commitments and climate summits. With increased climate coverage, it is also
reasonable to assume an increased level of concern about the climate. Prior research
documents the significant role of news media in shaping public awareness of and
perceptions toward environmental issues (Schoenfeld et al., 1979; Slovic, 1986;
Boykoff and Boykoff, 2007; Sampei and Aoyagi-Usui, 2009; Hale, 2010). Media
influence public perceptions through several channels, including the informational
content conveyed in news reporting, the intensity of media coverage devoted to a topic,
and the emotional tone embedded in that coverage. Consistent with Ardia et al. (2022),
we argue that these dimensions together make the news-based measure a meaningful
proxy for shifts in climate change concern. This approach is further supported by the
framing theory of Chong and Druckman (2007), which emphasizes that the manner in
which information is presented influences individual attitudes toward a subject.
Accordingly, the level of climate change concern portrayed in news media is expected
to directly affect public concern regarding climate change. In an ideal scenario, we
would want to measure the level of investors’ concern caused by the informational
content of climate change news articles to better capture climate change concerns due to
media coverage on the subject. Ardia et al. (2022) design a score to measure such
concern by combining the levels of discussion about consequences of climate events
and the negativity in each news article with the subject categorization “climate change”.
       Because data directly measuring concern is not available through GDELT, we
rely on the assumption that shocks in climate change news (our variable
ClimateNewsCoverageChange) translates to shocks to climate change concern among
investors. To distinguish months of high and low climate change news coverage, we
construct a dummy variable, High_CNCCit, that takes the value of one when
ClimateNewsCoverageChange is positive. The High_CNCCit captures months, in each
country, with unexpected increases in climate change news that are not cyclical,
seasonal or belonging to a past pattern.

Climate change news sentiment
As described in Section 3.1, the measure of climate change news coverage risks
miscapturing positive news discussions. While the news may reflect increased public
attention to climate change, it might not necessarily capture increased climate change
concerns, limiting the use of ClimateNewsCoverageChange as a proxy of climate
change concern shocks. With inspiration taken from the article by Engle, Giglio, Kelly,
Lee, and Stroebel (2020), we address this risk by applying the sentiment analysis by
GDELT on the climate change news articles.Other research also shows the effect of tone
on how news affects trading behavior (Baker & Wurgler, 2006; Briere et al., 2023) We
use the GDELT tone score, which is the daily average tone of articles with chosen
keywords (same as for CNCC). We average the daily values for the tone score to the
monthly level, then following Engle et al. (2020), we measure shocks in monthly tone
score by constructing values of ClimateNewsSentimentChange for news articles
published in country i in month t as residuals from a first-order autoregressive model,
AR(1), depicted in Eq.(4). The autoregressive model relates the tone score, denoted Y,
to its past values, with the residual representing the part of the current month’s tone
score not predicted by previous month’s tone score. ClimateNewsSentimentChange
therefore captures shocks in the average tone of news articles on climate change. The
rationale for using shocks in tone score rather than the raw tone score is that shocks to
raw sentiments have exerted significant impacts on stock returns (Shen et al., 2022). We
then construct a dummy variable, Low_CNSCit that takes the value of one when
ClimateNewsSentimentChangeit is below the city’s historical monthly median.
Low_CNSS captures months, within each country, where climate change news articles
have unexpectedly low tone scores. In these months, climate change has suddenly been
portrayed mostly negatively in the news media. Following Ardia et al. (2022), we
assume that negative sentiment in climate change news coverage is more indicative of
public concern about climate change.

Monte Carlo Permutation Test
In an ideal scenario, we would like to conduct a placebo test similar to that of Choi et al.
(2020) to examine whether the relationships (or lack thereof) between abnormal
pollution exposure, attention to global warming, and EMC returns existed prior to 2012.
However, we only have access to pollution data starting 2012, meaning a time-based
placebo test is not feasible. We would expect to see weaker relationships between the
three variables, as awareness and attention to pollution and global warming was
significantly lower before, especially before the 2000s (Choi et al., 2020). As a
replacement, we conduct Monte-Carlo Permutation Tests (MCPT) on our key variables.
MPCT randomly shuffles (permutes) several times (we run 100 repetitions) our datasets
to break any underlying pairwise relationships between pollution-attention and
pollution-returns, generating a random distribution of the coefficients. This acts as a
new baseline significance level, benchmarking the regression’s coefficient to the
newfound distribution of coefficients. This helps answer the question whether the
coefficient found is by chance. For brevity, the test results are included in the Appendix.
Charts 1–2 are robustness checks for Table 3–4. Charts 3–6 are robustness checks for
Table 5–6. We compare the two-sided p-value from the MCPT with the significance
levels of the corresponding regression tests and find that the MCPT results are in line
with the findings. Specifically, abnormal PM2.5 or PM10 levels (as opposed to PM2.5
and PM10 quintiles) have no relationship with DSVI (Charts 1 and 2 have two-sided
p-values larger than 10%). Chart 4’s result supports that abnormal PM10 exposure
drives positive significant EMC equal-weighted size-adjusted returns (Table 6 Panel A).
Similarly, Charts 3, 5 and 6 support that abnormal PM10 exposure does not drive EMC
value-weighted size-adjusted returns, and abnormal PM2.5 exposure does not drive
EMC equal- and value-weighted size-adjusted returns.

4.4 Tests of potential mechanisms

Existing research outlines multiple mechanisms through which air pollution and other
environmental conditions may influence stock market outcomes. Our baseline results in
the previous section show no significant effect of abnormal PM2.5 exposure on stock
return, and a significant positive effect of abnormal PM10 exposure that is likely due to
non-energy high-emission firms. However, these tests may not fully capture the
influence of such mechanisms. To address this, we conduct additional tests designed to
evaluate specific channels that could generate effects otherwise undetected in our
primary specifications. By exploring different channels in the air pollution-stock return
relationship, we aim to improve our understanding of the underlying mechanisms
through which air pollution may affect the stock market.

Firm performance
First, through its impact on firm performance, air pollution may affect investors’
valuation of firms and thus their behavior toward trading the stocks of these firms.
There are different ways through which local air pollution may affect firm performance.
One channel is that air pollution may reduce firm performance by causing acute health
problems and therefore reducing work productivity (Fu, Viard, Zhang, 2021). In
addition, skilled workers who tend to have a greater demand for quality of life may have
a greater knowledge and understanding of the harmful effects of air pollution (Arntz,
2010), and firms located in more polluted areas, as a result, become less capable of
recruiting and retaining high-quality individuals, leading to a loss of human capital
(Xue, Zhang, and Zhao, 2021). Xue et al. (2021) document more pronounced effects of
air pollution on firm performance in industries highly dependent on human capital,
including “innovative” industries where talent is essential. In particular, the firm
performance of the following industries are harmed by air pollution: Biotechnology,
Telecommunication, Computers, and Electronics, following the definition adopted by
Ouimet and Zarutskie (2014). Using the SIC codes of these harmed, “innovative”
industries provided by Ouimet and Zarutskie (2014), we hand-match their
corresponding industry names with IPCC subsector names, and find that three of these
four industries have industry subsectors classified as high-emission according to our

Climate change concern
We extend the analysis of Choi et al. (2020) and existing research on the financial effect
of air pollution by analysing whether abnormal air pollution has an impact on stock
return in periods with: (i) high climate change news coverage and (ii) negative climate
change sentiment in news media. These variables capture, although not perfectly, shocks
to climate change concern. As suggested by the findings of Pástor et al. (2022), the
relative outperformance of green assets compared to brown assets in the past decade is
driven by climate change concern shocks; such shocks influence investors’ expectations
about firm cash flows and preferences toward green versus brown assets. They find that
shocks to climate change concern increases the attractiveness and price of green assets.
Similarly, upon recognizing the effect of air pollution on climate change, people may
stay away from climate-unfriendly stocks but prefer climate-friendly stocks, especially
in months with abnormally high air pollution alongside elevated climate change
concerns. We expect increased news coverage and lower sentiment to contribute to
pollution’s effect on EMC returns. Exposure to such news can lead investors to increase
their private perception of climate risk, thereby increasing their expectation of
climate-sensitive firms facing environmental enforcement actions in the future, and thus
expecting lower future cash flows, or given their non-pecuniary preferences shift away
from climate-unfriendly firms.

5. Discussion

5.1 Analysis

Our first hypothesis predicted that abnormal air pollution would increase investors’
attention to climate change, as measured by Google search activity on “global
warming”. Overall, we find limited evidence supporting this hypothesis. Abnormal
PM2.5 and PM10 levels do not consistently lead to higher climate-related search
intensity across European exchange cities. City-level Google search activity related to
climate change tends to be lower during periods of elevated PM2.5 exposure. The
results suggest that higher concentrations of fine particulate matter do not function as a
wake-up call for climate change. This finding further challenges the idea that PM2.5
shocks lead investors to update their beliefs about climate risk. This result deviates from
our initial expectations and from findings in closely related studies, such as Choi et al.
(2020), who show that unusually high local temperatures significantly increase attention
to global warming.
       The second hypothesis posited that abnormal air pollution would lead to the
underperformance of brown stocks relative to green stocks. Empirically, our analysis
reveals inconsistent results across pollutants, specifications, and portfolio constructions.
Overall, abnormal PM2.5 does not drive meaningful or consistent effects on
emissions-minus-clean (EMC) portfolio returns. In contrast, PM10 shows somewhat
stronger, though still inconsistent, effects. PM10 is associated with lower attention
trends at the country level during periods of elevated PM10 exposure relative to periods
of low PM10 exposure and produces more return responses than PM2.5 in some
specifications.This divergence underscores the importance of distinguishing between
PM2.5 and PM10 when analysing their financial impact. While both pollutants are
linked to climate change, PM2.5 generally produces fewer significant results than
PM10, likely reflecting differences in salience and perceptibility.
       The finding of no consistent, systematic pattern in how abnormal air pollution
affects the relative stock performance of brown versus green firms can be compared to
the study of Fisman, Ghosh, Sarkar, and Zhang (2023), which shows that providing
explicit information about local air quality negatively affects retail investors’
investments into brown stocks. Their results emphasize the role of information
disclosure rather than physical exposure. By contrast, our study focuses on actual
experiences of abnormal air pollution, rather than increased access to pollution data.
The difference in findings suggests that information disclosure plays a crucial role.
While pollution information can directly shift beliefs and investment behavior, pollution
exposure alone may not be sufficient to update climate change beliefs.
       The results are not uniform across portfolio construction methods, pollution
quintiles, and industries. There are inconsistencies between equal-weighted and
value-weighted EMC returns, implying that the relationships we investigate may vary
over market cap. For instance, as documented by Pástor et al. (2022), smaller stocks
exhibit a more slow reaction to climate shocks. We observe stronger responses among
large-cap firms, consistent with evidence that larger firms react more to current climate
shocks, while smaller firms respond more to past shocks (Pástor et al., 2022). This size
heterogeneity may explain part of the inconsistency in our results. Another possible
explanation is that green assets may only outperform brown assets during periods when
climate-related stocks sufficiently and unexpectedly strengthen climate concerns (Pástor
et al., 2022; Ardia et al., 2022). If pollution episodes in our sample are not perceived as
“big shocks,” or do not pose significant risk to firms and investors, investor attention
may remain low, limiting their impact on climate-sensitive portfolios. This
interpretation is consistent with research showing that investor attention is often
triggered by salient, extreme events rather than incremental changes.
        Taken together, our findings highlight both the limits and the potential channels
through which pollution may affect financial markets. While PM10 appears more salient
and produces stronger return responses, PM2.5 does not consistently generate effects.
The inconsistencies across weighting methods, industries, and quintiles suggest that
pollution shocks alone may not be sufficient to shift capital allocation toward or away
from climate-sensitive firms. Instead, the interaction of pollution salience, firm size, and
shock magnitude appears critical. Our contribution lies in documenting these limits and
suggesting that pollution episodes, unlike temperature shocks, do not reliably serve as
catalysts for climate-conscious capital allocation.
       Finally, the findings suggest that not all environmental shocks are equally
effective in shaping investor beliefs or asset prices. While air pollution is highly salient
in daily life, it may primarily operate through local health and mood channels rather
than through climate-risk belief updating. In addition, our results highlight the limits of
relying on personal environmental experiences to drive climate-related financial
reallocation. This may help explain why markets remain slow to price certain climate
risks, consistent with evidence of underreaction to slow-moving environmental risks
(Hong, Li, and Xu, 2019).

5.2 Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, we cannot observe the physical location
of investors nor their actual exposure to abnormal air pollution. Trading activity may be
conducted by investors who are not directly exposed to local air pollution, which could
attenuate the estimated effects.
        Second, in an ideal scenario, future research would incorporate investor-level
data to disentangle how attention and trading behavior vary across investor types. Prior
studies show that institutional investors, retail investors, and blockholders exhibit
distinct sensitivities to environmental signals. For example, institutional investors may
overreact to salient natural disasters (Alok, Kumar, and Wemers, 2020), while
blockholders, often located in exchange cities, may be more directly exposed to local
pollution shocks, and often act as buyers of last resort (Choi et al., 2020). As our
analysis does distinguish between investor types, there is a risk of attenuated effects.
        Our results also vary across time periods and quintile specifications,
underscoring the instability of pollution effects in financial markets. Inconsistent
findings across abnormal pollution quintiles suggest that investor responses may not
scale linearly with pollution intensity. Instead, attention and return effects appear
sporadic, raising questions about whether pollution shocks are sufficiently salient to
trigger systematic capital reallocation.
        Finally, our focus on Europe raises both strengths and limitations. On one hand,
the European Union is characterized by higher climate awareness and stronger public
support for climate action (European Commission, 2025). This may confound our
results, as investor attention could be driven more by country-level awareness and
policy discourse than by local pollution shocks. On the other hand, this context provides
a unique opportunity to study how pollution interacts with markets in a region where
climate salience is already high. Whether our findings generalize beyond Europe
remains an open question, and future work should examine cross-country heterogeneity
to assess robustness.
6. Concluding Remarks

This study examines whether abnormal local air pollution acts as a wake-up call to
climate change and whether such shocks affect the relative performance of brown and
green firms in European stock markets. Using data from different European exchange
cities between 2012 and 2024, we analyze the impact of PM2.5 and PM10 on investor
attention and stock return.
        Overall, we find limited evidence that abnormal air pollution increases attention
to climate change or leads to systematic underperformance of brown stocks relative to
green stocks. These findings suggest that air pollution shocks may not constitute strong
belief-updating events about climate risks in the European context.
       Our results contribute to the climate finance literature by highlighting the
importance of the channel through which environmental information reaches investors.
However, future research could extend this work in several directions. First, studying
regions with lower baseline climate concern or more severe pollution, such as emerging
markets, may provide stronger tests of the pollution-as-salience mechanism. However,
our findings highlight the importance of accounting for industry effects in order to
isolate any financial effect of air pollution. This may be achieved by using
industry-adjusted definitions of brown and green firms, or other sources of exogenous
variation in air pollution (e.g., sudden policy changes or plant closures). Then,
combining pollution data with investor-level location and trading data could help better
identify behavioral channels.
       More broadly, understanding which experiences truly shift beliefs about climate
risk remains crucial for both asset pricing and policy design. As climate change
intensifies, identifying the conditions under which markets internalize environmental
risks will become increasingly important.

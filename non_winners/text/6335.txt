When Good Firms Go Private: ESG Performance and Voluntary Delisting

Abstract:

   This paper examines whether firm ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance)
   performance influences voluntary delisting from public equity markets. Using a Cox
   Proportional Hazards model on a panel of over 3,200 public and private firms, we document
   a robust positive association: high-ESG firms have a significantly higher likelihood of
   voluntarily exiting public markets. We further exploit the EU’s Non-Financial Reporting
   Directive (NFRD) as a quasi-exogenous ESG shock. A Difference-in-Differences analysis
   compares voluntary delisting rates of EU firms with matched US firms before and after the
   policy implementation. We find that post-NFRD, EU firms voluntarily delist at significantly
   lower rates relative to US firms, where this decline was driven by the top ESG performers.
   The result suggests that public listing and ESG profiles interact in complex ways: while high-
   ESG firms are more prone to go private generally, disclosure mandates can mitigate this
   effect. These findings have important implications for policymakers and investors
   concerning the credibility of market-based ESG mechanisms and the design of disclosure
   regulations.

Keywords:

ESG, Delisting, Voluntary Delisting, Survival Analysis, Sustainability Disclosure, Difference-
in-Differences, Going Private

1. Introduction
This thesis examines whether a firm’s Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG)
performance influences its propensity to voluntarily delist from public stock markets, and how
regulatory changes in ESG disclosure affect this decision. Understanding this relationship
matters for both investors and policymakers. In recent years the number of publicly listed
companies have been declining globally, notably, as of 2016 there were more delistings than
new listings in the U.S. stock markets (Doidge, Karolyi & Stulz, 2016). This trend has raised
concerns about the vibrancy of public equity markets and prompted questions about what drives
firms to go private. In parallel, ESG performance has become a key indicator of corporate
quality in the eyes of many investors, often associated with lower long-term risks and strong
stakeholder relations. Yet it remains unclear how ESG characteristics influence listing
decisions: whether strong performance encourages firms to remain public to capitalize on
investor interest, or whether ESG-related costs, scrutiny, or strategic preferences instead push
some firms toward private ownership. In this context, our study explores whether ESG
considerations – across the performance spectrum – help explain recent delisting behavior.
   In contrast to the intuition that strong ESG companies face fewer undisclosed risks and thus
remain public, we find higher ESG scores are associated with a greater likelihood of voluntary
delisting. Using a Cox Proportional Hazards model on European firms, we show that one unit
increase in ESG-score is associated with a 13.9% higher hazard of voluntary delisting, all else
equal. We then exploit the European Union’s Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD) – an
EU-wide ESG disclosure mandate in effect since 2018 – as an exogenous shock to reporting
requirements. A Difference-in-Differences (DiD) analysis reveals that after the NFRD’s
implementation, voluntary delistings declined significantly among EU firms relative to U.S.
firms who were not subject to the mandate. Importantly, this post-2018 decline in delisting was
driven by firms in the top ESG performance quintiles (the 4th and 5th quintiles), indicating that
the regulation’s impact was concentrated among sustainability leaders. In summary, the core
contribution of this study is to document a robust link between ESG performance and the
likelihood of going private, and to demonstrate that ESG-shocks can influence firms’ stance to
leave public markets – depending on their ESG profile.
   We tackle the research question with a dual empirical approach. First, we employ a Cox
Proportional Hazards model to analyze the likelihood of voluntary delisting as a function of
ESG performance. This survival analysis framework leverages a broad dataset EU publicly
listed firms, tracking which companies voluntarily delist over time and relating these events to
the firms’ ESG scores and financial characteristics. The hazard model approach accounts for
the timing of exits and allows us to control for time-varying covariates (e.g. firm size, market
valuation, leverage) while handling censoring (firms that remain public). The Cox regression
results show a clear pattern: firms with higher ESG scores exhibit a significantly higher hazard
of voluntary delisting. In practice, firms with higher ESG scores are more likely to exit the stock
market than comparable firms with a lower ESG rating. This finding is statistically robust and
counter-intuitive against the intuition that ESG-leaders would have fewer reasons to leave
public markets. It suggests that ESG outperformance does not immunize firms against going
private; if anything, high ESG performers have been more prone to delist.

                                     2
   Second, we introduce a quasi-experimental test to identify the effect of an ESG-regulatory
shock on voluntary delisting behavior. The EU’s NFRD, which came into force in 2018,
mandated large companies in Europe to disclose standardized non-financial (ESG) information.
We treat this mandate as a natural experiment that increased the transparency requirements (and
possibly public scrutiny), comparability and ESG awareness for EU firms, but not for similar
U.S. firms. Using a Difference-in-Differences design, we compare the change in delisting rates
for EU companies before vs. after 2018 to the concurrent change for U.S. companies (as a
control group). The identifying assumption is that, absent the NFRD, EU and U.S. firms would
have followed similar trends in voluntary delisting; post-2018 divergence can thus be attributed
to the new reporting rule – thus a direct link to ESG.
   The DiD results indicate a marked divergence: European firms saw a significantly larger
drop in voluntary delistings after 2018 compared to their U.S. counterparts. In effect, the
mandatory ESG disclosure dampened the propensity of EU firms to voluntarily go private.
Notably, when we break down the effects by ESG score, the decline in delisting is concentrated
among the highest-ESG firms. Companies in the top ESG quintiles engaged less in
voluntary delisting in the years following NFRD, whereas lower-ESG firms in Europe did not
experience a statistically significant change relative to the control group. This heterogeneity
implies that the policy had the greatest “retention” impact on firms that were already strong on
sustainability metrics. One interpretation is that increased disclosure requirements leveled the
playing field – higher-ESG firms, which may have previously considered delisting, as evident
by our Cox model, chose to remain public once transparency became mandatory for all.
Alternatively, the NFRD may have enhanced the value of being public for ESG leaders by
amplifying their visibility to investors and stakeholders. In any case, the evidence clearly shows
that regulation can influence corporate listing decisions, and in this instance, it appears to
have reduced exits from the public market.
   Together, these findings make several contributions. We show that even after accounting for
factors like size, free cash flow, and dividend a firm’s ESG score has an independent correlation
with its likelihood of going private. Moreover, by leveraging the NFRD’s implementation, we
contribute to the emerging evidence on how mandatory ESG disclosure policies affect firms.
While past regulatory interventions (such as Sarbanes-Oxley Act in the U.S.) often had the
unintended consequence of encouraging firms to exit public markets due to higher compliance
costs (Leuz et al. 2008), the NFRD case suggests a different outcome. For policymakers, the
evidence underscores that listing rules and disclosure mandates can significantly shape the
composition of public markets. Accordingly, ensuring that public markets continue to attract
and retain companies – including those excelling in ESG – is important for market diversity
and for aligning capital markets with sustainability goals.
   In conclusion, this thesis establishes a clear empirical link between ESG performance and
voluntary delisting decisions, it demonstrates how policy changes – like the NFRD – can adjust
that link. The introduction of ESG considerations adds a distinct perspective to the study of
public-to-private transitions, offering evidence that can inform both academic debates and real-
world policy design. Our hope is that this analysis provides a nuanced understanding of the
interplay between corporate sustainability profiles and the decision to remain part of public
equity markets, versus seeking the relative opacity of private ownership.

Prior studies have largely attributed delistings to financial constraints, regulatory burdens,
agency conflicts, and the costs and benefits of public listing (e.g. Bharath & Dittmar, 2010).
   Regulatory and compliance costs have been shown to spur voluntary delisting. A prominent
case was the migration of firms following the U.S. Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002: studies
document a spike in firms “going dark” attributable to the burdens of Sarbanes-Oxley act
(SOX), as many companies deregistered to avoid its stricter reporting and internal control
requirements (Leuz, Triantis & Wang, 2008). More generally, increasing policy uncertainty and
governance costs can make public listing less attractive, leading managers to take their
companies private (Azevedo, Colak, Kalak & Tunaru, 2024). This paper builds on this literature
by investigating whether a regulatory shock in the form of mandatory ESG disclosure have
similar influence on voluntary delisting decisions.
   Previous research has explored the financial and governance related factors that drive firms
to go private. Liquidity constraints, information asymmetry, and short-termism in public
markets have been identified as primary motivators (Bharath & Dittmar, 2010). Public markets
impose disclosure requirements and external monitoring, which may deter firms from pursuing
long-term strategic initiatives, particularly in industries where significant investment in
research and development (R&D) is required (Ljungberg, Farre-Mensa & Asker, 2014).
Additionally, firms with lower financial visibility – often characterized by fewer analysts
covering the stock and lower institutional ownership – are more likely to opt out of public
markets due to reduced access to capital and higher volatility (Mehran & Peristiani, 2009).
Azevedo, et al. (2024) complement this by investigating the economic and strategic motivations
behind delisting, finding that compliance burdens and regulatory uncertainty in part influences
such decisions. We extend this work by moving beyond the general compliance burden to assess
how ESG performance interact with regulatory pressure to influence delisting decisions.
   A critical concern in financial markets is the extent to which short-term market pressures
distort firms’ incentives. Public firms are often subject to quarterly earnings expectations,
leading to myopic investment behaviors that prioritize short-term gains over long-term value
creation (Ljungberg et al. 2014). This environment can be particularly detrimental to firms
attempting to improve ESG performance, as sustainability investments typically yield long-
term benefits rather than immediate financial returns (e.g. Whelan, Atz, Holt, & Clark, 2021) –
this formed the conceptual basis of our study.

participation. Firms with subpar ESG performance face greater public pressure, increased
regulatory compliance costs, and reputational risks (Deloitte, 2024), potentially making private
ownership an attractive alternative.

   In summary, prior research establishes a foundation of motives for delisting that includes
financial visibility, agency costs, and market pressures. The emerging literature on ESG adds a
new dimension: ESG performance might influence a firm’s cost-benefit analysis of being
public. A firm excelling in ESG could leverage its public status to attract dedicated investors
and enjoy a valuation premium; and thus have an incentive to remain listed. Conversely, a firm
lagging in ESG might experience the public market as a punitive environment, providing reason
to seek privacy. Until recently, however, the role of ESG in delisting decisions has been
insufficiently explored. We contribute to the literature by empirically analyzing whether ESG
performance influence a firm’s decision to delist from public market by directly addressing the
question of “How do ESG performance determine if firms stay public or go private, and what
difference can regulation make?”. In doing so, we synthesize two prior strands of research –
one about the increase in delisting and another about the rise of ESG investing – to see if the
pursuit of sustainability in public markets is contributing to the delisting wave.

we conclude in Section 5.

To fill the gap in the literature we propose two separate hypotheses:

H1: Firms with lower ESG scores are more likely to voluntary delist from public markets.

H2: Regulatory ESG shocks, causally increase voluntary delisting for ESG laggards.

2.5 Addressing Endogeneity
Low ESG scores could cause firms to delist, however, reverse causality could also explain the
phenomena. For example, managers anticipating going private might neglect ESG
improvements or disclosure in the years leading up to the delisting, causing a drop in ESG
scores. In such a case, the association between low ESG and delisting would not imply that low
ESG drove the decision; rather, the decision drove the low ESG.
   To mitigate concerns about reverse causality, we use the directive (NFRD) as an exogenous
policy shock. The directive increased ESG disclosure requirements for large EU-listed firms,
thereby amplifying ESG scrutiny without directly altering firm-level fundamentals such as
profitability, capital structure, or operating risk. Although ESG scores themselves may not
respond mechanically to the policy shift in a single year, the regulatory environment introduced
a sudden and external change in the informational and reputational costs of remaining public,
hence a potential incentive to delist. This exogenous variation strengthens our identification
strategy by enabling causal inference on the effect of ESG scrutiny on delisting outcomes.
   Finally, we note that our analyses control for variables that themselves capture many
common reasons to delist – these are drawn from three independent previous research papers,
namely Azevedo et al. (2024), Bharath et al. (2010) & Mehran et al. (2009).

4. Discussion
4.1 Summary of Main Findings
The analysis uncovers several notable patterns regarding ESG performance and
voluntary delistings. First, firms with stronger ESG profiles exhibit a higher propensity to
voluntarily delist from stock exchanges. The Cox Proportional Hazards model indicates that
higher ESG scores are associated with roughly a 13.9% increase in the hazard of voluntary
delisting, all else equal. Second, a clear shift emerges in the post-2018 period: following the
implementation of the EU’s NFRD, European firms experienced a relative decline in voluntary
delisting rates compared to their U.S. counterparts (who were not subject to the NFRD). This
Difference-in-Differences result suggests that the new ESG disclosure mandate corresponded
with fewer companies choosing to leave public markets in the EU, relative to the ongoing trends
in the U.S. Finally, this post-NFRD decline in delistings was not uniform across all firms – it
was driven primarily by companies in the top ESG performance quintiles (the fourth and fifth
quintiles). In other words, high-ESG firms played a central role in reducing overall delisting
activity after the regulation, whereas lower-ESG firms did not show any significant indication
of change.

4.2 Relation to Hypotheses and Expectations
Following our original hypothesis, it would be reasonable to assume that high-ESG firms have
stronger incentives to remain public due to reputational advantages and the growing investor

                                     13
demand for sustainability. The empirical result following our Cox-model, however, suggests
the opposite: higher ESG scores correlate with a greater hazard of delisting. This implies that
even well-regarded firms on ESG dimensions may choose to exit public markets more
frequently than their lower-ESG peers. A result that challenges the assumption that ESG
strength shields firms from delisting and instead points toward potential misalignment between
sustainability-oriented firms and the prevailing incentives of public capital markets.
   The results following the NFRD implementation align more with the expectation that
regulatory ESG shocks could influence listing decisions. The hypothesis that ESG laggards
would be more prone to delist following NFRD (H2) cannot be supported. Instead, we observe
a decline in voluntary delistings among EU firms relative to the control group, suggesting the
NFRD did not drive companies away from public markets – it may have encouraged them to
stay. This outcome supports an alternative expectation: that greater transparency and
standardization in ESG reporting could enhance the appeal of remaining public, particularly for
those with strong ESG credentials. The evidence that this effect is concentrated in high-ESG
firms is crucial. It indicates that the firms expected to benefit most from demonstrating their
ESG quality – those already performing well on ESG metrics – were indeed the ones who stayed
public at higher rates once ESG disclosure became mandatory. This pattern meets our
expectation that firms respond differently to the NFRD, depending on their ESG score. High-
ESG companies appear to have responded positively to the new reporting regime (perhaps
remaining listed to showcase their strengths), whereas lower-ESG companies did not
significantly alter their behavior.
   In summary, the hypotheses find mixed support. The baseline ESG-delisting
relationship turned out opposite to our intuition, highlighting a nuanced reality that high ESG
firms were more likely to voluntarily delist in general. In contrast, the impact of the
NFRD aligns with the view that policy interventions can shape corporate decisions: mandatory
ESG reporting coincided with fewer voluntary delistings, confirming the expectation that such
regulation would matter.

4.3 Implications for Public Markets and ESG
These findings carry several broader implications for public equity markets and the role of ESG
factors. The fact that many high-ESG firms were delisting at higher rates (evidenced by our
Cox model) may raise concerns about public markets’ ability to retain some of their best
corporate citizens. If companies with strong sustainability practices increasingly choose private
ownership, public investors lose access to those firms, and the overall pool of listed companies
could tilt toward lower-ESG profiles. This dynamic would be troubling for proponents of
sustainable investing and for regulators aiming to improve corporate transparency. It suggests
that, in the absence of intervention, there might have been a selection effect where high-ESG
firms were more likely to leave, potentially because they felt undervalued or constrained by
public market pressures. This supports the idea that when the market does not fully appreciate
a firm’s ESG strengths, the firm’s equity can appear undervalued and the benefits of being
public diminish (following the notion of Pástor & Veronesi, 2012). In such scenarios, even
well-performing companies might find going private attractive.

                                     14
   By mandating ESG disclosure, the NFRD likely improved the information environment and
brought ESG performance to the forefront in investors’ decisions. This interpretation is
consistent with evidence that ESG disclosure regulation can enhance transparency and
yield beneficial capital market effects, such as improved stock liquidity and investor
engagement following Krueuger et al. (2024). With better disclosure, high-ESG firms may
receive greater recognition and valuation in public markets, reducing any incentive they
previously had to seek private status. In essence, the NFRD may have leveled the playing field:
firms that were performing well on ESG could now prove it to all investors in a standardized
way, reaping rewards (in terms of reputation, investor demand, and possibly valuation
premiums) that make staying public worthwhile.
   For public markets, a key implication is that policy and transparency can significantly
influence which firms remain listed. The EU’s experience post-NFRD indicates that well-
designed disclosure requirements might slow the erosion of the public firm universe
by encouraging top-performing companies to stay. This could gradually lead to an improvement
in the average ESG standards of publicly traded firms, as those with strong ESG records
continue trading on exchanges and thus likely become rewarded by doing so. Such an outcome
has a twofold impact: public investors retain access to high-ESG companies (supporting the
growth of ESG-focused investment strategies), and the continued presence of these firms sets
a benchmark that could inspire broader market improvements in ESG practices. On the other
hand, it also implies that firms with poor ESG performance might be increasingly absent from
public exchanges if they choose to opt out rather than improve – a possibility that raises
questions about how those companies are monitored once private. In a scenario where low-ESG
firms retreat from public markets, transparency gaps could emerge, since private companies are
typically subject to less stringent disclosure. Thus, while public market ESG quality may rise,
regulators may need to consider the wider impact on stakeholders if underperforming firms go
dark (in line with the scrutiny avoidance mechanism evidenced by Duchin et al. 2024).

4.4 Possible Mechanisms
Several mechanisms could explain the observed relationships. For the higher delisting hazard
among high-ESG firms, one plausible mechanism is the mispricing hypothesis. High-ESG
firms may have felt that public markets were not fully valuing their ESG investments and long-
term orientation. Prior to recent ESG investing trends, such firms might have traded at discounts
relative to their intrinsic value offering unique appeal to delist. Once they perceive the benefits,
the controlling shareholders of high-ESG companies might find going private advantageous –
they can escape the quarterly earnings pressure and focus on long-term initiatives without
market scrutiny. In this sense, strong ESG performance could proxy for overall firm quality and
stability.
   Turning to the post-NFRD dynamics, the mechanisms seem rooted in how mandatory ESG
disclosure alters incentives. The NFRD essentially increased transparency around ESG metrics.
For high-ESG firms, this new regime likely amplified the benefits of staying public: their
superior ESG metrics became a valuable asset in attracting investors and perhaps achieving a
lower cost of capital. Indeed, recent evidence shows that mandatory ESG disclosures can
improve stock liquidity and overall market efficiency (Krueuger et al. 2024), which would make

                                     15
public financing more appealing. High-ESG companies, already performing well on
sustainability metrics, were positioned to benefit most from the improved market conditions –
not only because standardized disclosure enhanced comparability across firms, but also because
it allowed them to credibly signal their quality to investors, thereby strengthening their relative
standing. In contrast, lower-ESG firms would potentially face a less favorable calculus:
mandatory disclosure could expose their shortcomings, potentially invite scrutiny or activism,
and entail costs to improve on ESG issues.
   A key aspect related to delisting is timing. While NFRD became effective in 2018, firms
likely responded with a delay. As Azevedo et al. (2024) highlights, firms undergo a period of
evaluation before taking the decision to delist – it likely involves deliberation, board of approval
and market testing. This aligns with the practical timeline of ESG reporting: firms were required
to disclose 2017 ESG metrics in their 2018 annual reports, typically released in early Q2. Thus,
any investor reaction or media attention based on these disclosures would materialize only after
this point. This lag could explain why we observe a stronger treatment effect (Appendix 7) in
the years following 2018. The effect suggests that improved transparency set off a chain of
                                                                6
market responses, which in turn discouraged delisting .
   Another relevant mechanism involves investor and societal pressures relating to a reduction
in information asymmetry. By the late 2010s, institutional investors in Europe were increasingly
integrating ESG criteria into their portfolios. The NFRD can be seen as both a response to and
a catalyst for this trend. Post-NFRD, investors had better information to compare firms’ ESG
performance. High-ESG firms likely enjoyed greater investor support, as evidenced by capital
flows into ESG-focused funds and higher demand for shares of sustainable companies
(Hartzmark et al. 2019). This support would manifest in higher valuations or more patient
shareholder bases, thereby reducing any pre-existing motive these firms had to leave the
exchange – making the public market environment more hospitable for firms committed to
sustainability. Simultaneously, those firms might have also felt a responsibility to remain public
and set an example in transparency – an alignment of their values with the regulatory
intent. Ultimately, the effectiveness and market impact of ESG scores hinge on their perceived
validity – it is this credibility, reinforced by regulatory disclosure, that enables ESG
performance to reduce information asymmetries and influence firm behavior.

4.6 Limitations
While our findings offer valuable insights into the relationship between ESG performance and
voluntary delisting behavior, several limitations require consideration.

Limitations Related to Control Variables and Sample Composition
A limitation of this study is the inclusion of only seven control variables, due to data availability
and missingness. While we initially gathered a broader set of variables from both Refinitiv and
S&P, merging them resulted in significant sample loss due to missing data. As such, we chose
to retain dataset-specific controls in order to preserve statistical power and ensure the robustness
of our analysis. While this may raise concerns about omitted variable bias, the selected controls
are well-established in the literature and capture key financial characteristics relevant to
delisting decisions. Additionally, model diagnostics indicate no severe multicollinearity or
overfitting, and the results remain robust across alternative specifications.

Sample Bias Toward Larger Firms with ESG Disclosures
Not all firms (especially smaller-cap companies) have ESG ratings; our sample may be biased
towards larger firms that tend to have such disclosures. This means the findings primarily reflect
the behavior of mid-to-large-sized companies; the dynamics for smaller public firms (who
might also be heavily affected by compliance costs) could differ.

Delisting Classification and Potential Misclassification Bias
Regarding our classification of voluntary versus involuntary delistings, we initially considered
using DLSTCD codes. However, due to data limitations, this approach would have resulted in
a substantial loss of observations when collecting data for control variables, thereby
compromising the robustness of the study. As a result, we opted for an alternative method. To
improve accuracy, we applied an AI-based text (specifically OpenAI´s LLM ChatGPT-4o)
classification method to categorize delistings based on narrative descriptions. All voluntary
delisting observations used in our main analysis were manually verified to ensure correct
classification. A limitation of this approach is the possibility of false negatives: that is, some
voluntary delistings may have been incorrectly classified as involuntary and thus excluded from

                                     18
our treated sample. This may lead to a modest underestimation of the size of the voluntary
delisting group.

 Timing Uncertainty and External Shocks in the DiD Strategy
Our DiD identification strategy hinges on the EU’s Non-Financial Reporting Directive,
announced in late 2014 and effective for fiscal year 2017 reports. One could argue that firms
began reacting to the announcement in 2014, while others may have responded closer to 2018,
once compliance and enforcement materialized. We follow Cicchiello et al. (2023) in treating
2018 as the post-treatment year, consistent with the regulatory reporting cycle. However, we
acknowledge that firm responses to the policy could be heterogeneous in timing.
   Relatedly, the time window for observing post-NFRD effects is relatively short in the grand
scheme of corporate decisions. The NFRD became effective around 2018; our data covers the
two subsequent years. It is possible that firms needed time to fully adjust to the new regime, or
that any deterrent effects on low-ESG firms’ listing status might materialize more slowly.
Moreover, the period after 2018 included extraordinary events – notably the COVID-19
pandemic – which could independently affect delisting decisions. This is however mitigated in
our DiD due to the control group – an exogenous shock like COVID-19 is likely to have the
same effects for both markets. However, while our analysis attempts to isolate the policy
impact, readers should bear in mind this broader context.

Inability to Capture Industry-Specific Effects Due to Sample Constraints
Industry-level regressions could not be performed in our DiD due to sample size constraints.
Given this, we opted for a parsimonious regression design to preserve statistical power,
systematically excluding control variables that reduced sample size without significantly
improving model precision. While this supports generalizability, it limits our ability to assess
industry-specific heterogeneity in delisting behavior.

Sample Differences Between Baseline and ESG-Conditional DiD Models
The difference in sample size between the initial DiD regression and the ESG quintile-based
specification arises from data availability. While the baseline model does not rely on firm-level
ESG scores, the triple interaction model requires complete ESG data, resulting in the omission
of firms with missing values. To ensure that this sample restriction does not drive our findings,
we re-estimate the baseline DiD model using the restricted sample employed in the triple DiD
regression (n = 6548). The resulting coefficients are consistent in both sign and magnitude,
indicating that our main treatment effect is robust to the exclusion of observations with missing
ESG scores. This regression output is provided in Appendix 8.

Interpretation of the NFRD as an Exogenous ESG Disclosure Shock
Although the NFRD does not directly link to ESG performance or explicitly target listing
decisions, it represents a material shift in disclosure expectations and public transparency across
the EU. Prior literature has employed the NFRD as an exogenous shock to assess firm-level
ESG responses (e.g., Cicchiello et al., 2023), based on the premise of forced ESG disclosure
and external scrutiny. While the regulation itself does not single out a specific ESG group, it
                                     19
heightens visibility and comparative pressure via peer benchmarking, stakeholder perception,
and investor reaction to disclosed ESG metrics. In this way, a firm’s ESG performance may
perceive increased reputational or regulatory risks, which can alter the perceived cost-benefit
trade-off of remaining public. Although the regulation is not designed to induce delisting, our
identification strategy interprets it as a plausibly exogenous shock that affects ESG-sensitive
firm behavior, including strategic listing status decisions.

5. Conclusion
This thesis investigates whether ESG performance influences firms’ decisions to voluntarily
delist from public markets and whether mandatory ESG disclosure affects this decision. Using
a Cox Proportional Hazards model, we find that firms with higher ESG scores are more likely
to voluntarily delist. Complementing this, a Difference-in-Differences analysis exploiting the
EU’s 2018 Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD) reveals a significant decline in
voluntary delistings among treated EU firms relative to their U.S. counterparts – an effect driven
by those in the highest ESG quintiles.
   For policymakers and investors – ESG reporting mandates can encourage high-ESG firms
to remain publicly listed, thereby reinforcing the credibility of market-based sustainability
mechanisms. More broadly, the evidence underscores that both ESG performance and
regulatory frameworks meaningfully shape firms’ listing behavior. In conclusion, public market
structures and the rules governing them influence which firms find it optimal to remain publicly
listed.

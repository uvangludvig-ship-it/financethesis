DO ACTIVIST TYPES  MATTER?

A COMPARATIVE ANALYSIS OF SHAREHOLDER ACTIVISTS

ALBERT LINDHOLM

FILIP DORDEVIC

Bachelor Thesis

Stockholm School of Economics

2024
Do Activist Types Matter? A Comparative Analysis of Shareholder Activists

Abstract:
   Using a comprehensive dataset of 2,640 activist interventions from 2000 to 2022, we
   categorize shareholder activists into seven distinct  activist types  to examine potential
   heterogeneity among activists. We find that activist types select  different  target firms
   based on size, profitability, and payout policies. Hedge funds, private equity, venture
   capital, mutual funds, and other financial institutions generate significant positive
   abnormal returns of 5% around activism announcements. In contrast, interventions by
   pension funds and individuals result in significantly lower returns. Despite these initial
   differences,  we observe convergence in firm  performance across activist types  over a
   one-year period. This suggests that although activist  type  impacts short-term  market
   reactions, it does not determine long-term firm outcomes.

Keywords:

   Shareholder activism,  Activist heterogeneity,  Activist  strategies,  Abnormal  returns

   Shareholder activism has  emerged as  a pivotal force in  shaping  financial markets.
Among  the  various categories  of  shareholder  activists,  hedge funds  have  garnered
considerable  attention  in  the  media  and academic discourse, primarily  due to their
assertive  strategies and high-profile  interventions.  This focus on hedge fund activism,
while  important,  has  overshadowed  activism  pursued  by  other  activist  types.  A
comprehensive understanding of shareholder activism requires a broader lens  that
incorporates the contributions and strategies of these alternative activist groups.
   The existing literature  predominantly focuses on hedge funds as the primary agents of
activism. Denes, Karpoff, and McWilliams (2017) summarize 30 years of research on the
topic and document abnormal returns of approximately 5–7% for hedge fund target firms
in the short term.  While some researchers  including  Klein and Zur (2009),  Swanson  and
Young and Yust (2022)  have compared hedge funds to a broader category of other
activists, these analyses often treat non-hedge fund activists as a  single  uniform  group.
This approach overlooks potential heterogeneity within  the  non-hedge fund activist
category, such as differences in investment objectives, strategies  and success rates.
Building upon prior research  by  Klein and Zur (2009),  Swanson, Young and Yust (2022)
and  von Lilienfeld-Toal and Schnitzler (2020)  our study extends the literature by
categorizing shareholder activists into seven distinct  activist  types:  hedge  funds,  private
equity,  venture  capital,  pension  funds,  mutual  funds,  individuals, and  other  financial
institutions.  This  extended  classification captures the variations that may influence the
effectiveness and impact of different  activists.  By comparing our  defined categories with
the extensively studied hedge funds,  we seek to deepen the understanding of how activist
type influences the outcomes of shareholder engagement.
   Following the definition by  Kallis and Corbet (2024), we define shareholder activism
as   efforts   by   shareholders   to   influence   a   company’s   management   and
policies  to  enhance  shareholder  value.  We investigate  shareholder activist campaigns
over a 23-year period  ranging  from 2000 to 2022.  Our analysis utilizes a comprehensive
dataset from  ThomsonOne,  encompassing  2,640  activist interventions in U.S.-based
firms. Unlike much of the academic literature, which relies on SEC Schedule 13D filings,
our use of the  ThomsonOne database  allows us to capture campaigns targeting larger
companies where activists may not be able to acquire a 5% stake and thus are not required
to file a  Schedule  13D.
   We find that activist types select target firms based on specific characteristics such as
size, profitability, and payout policies. Private equity, venture capital, individuals, and
other financial institutions tend to target smaller firms compared to hedge funds, as
evidenced by significantly lower market capitalization and total assets.  These activists
also prefer firms with lower profitability metrics, including return on assets and profit
margins. In contrast, mutual funds and pension funds focus on larger, more profitable
firms with stronger financial performance and higher payout yields. These distinctions
suggest that different activist types employ varied strategic considerations in their target
selection, aligning with their unique investment objectives and constraints.
   Our event study  methodology,  defined  in line with  MacKinlay  (1997),  uncovers that
the market response to activist announcements varies notably across activist types.
Interventions by hedge funds  show  significant positive abnormal returns of 5% around
the announcement date, consistent with prior literature.  Similarly,  positive market
reactions are observed for private equity, venture capital, mutual funds, and other
                                   2
financial institutions, indicating investor confidence in these activists’  ability to enhance
shareholder value. In contrast, interventions by pension funds and individual activists are
associated with significantly lower abnormal returns  relative to hedge funds. This  finding
suggests  potential  market skepticism regarding their effectiveness in driving value-
enhancing changes.
   Delving deeper, we examine the specific demands pursued by different activists and
their corresponding success rates. While hedge funds, private equity firms, and other
financial institutions achieve high success rates across various demand categories,
individuals  and  pension  funds  exhibit  notably  lower  success  rates.  Specifically,
individuals and pension funds do experience lower success rates within the same demand
areas   also   characterized   by   significantly   lower   returns.   Individuals   succeed   in
management  engagement and corporate governance demands in only 56% and 43% of
cases, compared to 82% and 69% for hedge funds. Pension funds perform even less
effectively, with success rates as low as 29% in corporate governance initiatives.  These
discrepancies  might  imply that the market anticipates lower probabilities of successful
outcomes from these activists, which is reflected in the less  favorable  stock price
reactions.
   Despite the differences in short-term market reactions, the long-term impact on firm
performance appears to converge across activist types. Over the one-year period
following activist announcements, firms targeted by hedge funds, private equity, venture
capital, and other financial institutions continue to exhibit positive abnormal returns, with
no  evidence  of  reversal.  Notably,  our  findings  reveal  no  statistically  significant
differences in abnormal returns among activist types during this period. Analyses  of
accounting performance metrics further indicate limited differences across activist types
in the post-intervention year.
   Our findings indicate that, although the market initially reacts differently to various
activist types, the longer-term effects on firm performance are largely similar across types
in both market  performance  and operational metrics. This discrepancy between short-
term and long-term outcomes suggests that investors initially perceive activist types
differently, potentially due to variations in anticipated effectiveness and reputation. These
findings  highlight the substantial impact of  underlying  heterogeneity within non-hedge
fund activist groups and underscore  its importance for  market reactions.
   The rest of the  paper  proceeds  as follows. Section I reviews the  background and
literature on  shareholder activism. Section II describes the dataset and classification  of
activist types. Section III  analyses  the pre-event  characteristics  of targeted firms  for each
activist type. Section IV examines stock market reactions and heterogeneity among types.
Section V  explores  the various demands  and their corresponding success rates among
activists. Section VI  investigates  market and operational  changes one year from activist
intervention.  We  conclude our  findings in  Section VII.

           I.  The Evolution of  Shareholder  Activism and Literature Review

   Shareholder activism has undergone significant transformation over the past few
decades and  has become an  influential force in corporate governance and financial
markets. Early manifestations of activism in the 1980s were led by blockholders who

                                   3
sought to improve underperforming companies through asset divestitures and share
repurchases.  Prominent researchers in the field,  Bethel, Liebeskind, and Opler (1998)
create   a   sample   of   activist   blockholders,   financial   blockholders   and   strategic
blockholders.  Their findings demonstrate that activist blockholders differ from financial
and  strategic  investors  by  aggressively  targeting  underperforming  companies  and
implementing operational restructurings. This  form of activism  facilitated  notable
improvements in firm performance compared to the more passive approaches of the other
defined  blockholders.
   The effectiveness of activist blockholders during the early period in activist history led
to defensive measures  taken  by  target  firms,  including the adoption of various takeover
defenses. As a result, change-of-control transactions became increasingly difficult, and
the  frequency  of hostile takeovers diminished.  Soon, institutional investors such as
mutual funds and pension funds began to engage in activism focused on corporate
governance reforms  (Karpoff, Malatesta, & Walkling, 1996).  Despite their efforts, the
impact of these institutions has been mixed. Studies have  reported  that while  mutual and
pension funds can affect minor changes in governance structures, their activism demands
do not significantly influence stock prices or firm performance  (Wahal, 1996; Karpoff,
2001; Woidtke, 2002; Gillan & Starks, 2007).
   Hedge funds  later  emerged as prominent activists in the early 2000s, differing from
traditional institutions in several key aspects.  Hedge funds  are privately organized
investment vehicles managed by professionals who often have significant personal
investments in the fund.  This aligns  their interests closely with those of their investors.
Furthermore, hedge funds are not subject to the same regulatory constraints as mutual
funds or pension funds, allowing them greater flexibility in their investment strategies.
Hedge funds  can  thus  take concentrated positions, use leverage, and employ derivatives,
enabling them to exert substantial influence on target firms  (Partnoy & Thomas, 2006).
   Building on the rapid  emergence  of hedge funds,  Brav, Jiang, Partnoy, and Thomas,
(2008)  analyze a comprehensive sample of hedge fund activism. They  find that hedge
funds attain abnormal returns of approximately 7% around the announcement date. These
findings remain consistent across literature,  with  a  universal acknowledgment of short-
run positive stock reactions to hedge fund activism  (Denes et al., 2017).  One  explanation
for  this phenomenon  is  that  the abnormal  returns reflect the market’s anticipation of
operational improvements, prompting positive stock reactions  (Bebchuk, Brav, & Jiang,
2015).  Additionally,  (Krishnan, Partnoy, & Thomas, 2016)  argue  that  the  reputation  of  a
hedge fund  can  enhance  returns,  as  activists perceived  as  more  likely to  successfully
execute their demands  experience  elevated stock prices.  In contrast,  Greenwood and
Schor (2009)  claim  that the  abnormal returns are  mainly  due to activists’  success in
orchestrating the sale of target firms at a premium.
   Klein and Zur (2009)  make  a  significant  contribution  to  the literature by  further
expanding the scope of analysis to include  a  broader  group  of  other  activist types.  They
demonstrate that both hedge funds and other private activists generate significant positive
abnormal returns around the announcement date. While  they report lower abnormal
returns of 5.1% for  other  activists  compared  to  10.2% for hedge funds,  they find that  the
difference is not statistically significant.  In contrast,  Swanson et al.  (2022)  further
contribute by showing that both hedge funds and other  types of  activists achieve

                                   4
significant   positive   returns   upon   their   interventions,   with   other   activist   types
outperforming hedge funds, particularly when demanding the sale of the company.
   Despite the  inclusion of the broader category of  non-hedge fund  activists, the distinct
activist types have  rarely  been compared to hedge funds.  Private equity, venture capital,
pension funds, mutual funds, individuals, and other financial institutions  all  engage in
activism with varying  demands and  strategies  (Denes, Karpoff, and McWilliams, 2017).
Recognizing this gap,  von Lilienfeld-Toal and Schnitzler (2020)  categorize  blockholders
into nine distinct groups to examine their relative impacts. Their analysis reveals that
while  the  market  reacts  positively  to  blockholder  interventions  across  all  types,
differences in abnormal returns among these categories are  insignificant  in relation to
unclassified events.  However, the  differences in demands, success rates, or long-term
operational and market performance among activist types  remain unexplored.  As a result,
the  potential heterogeneity within the broader group  of non-hedge  fund activist types
remains  poorly understood.

                         II.  Sample Selection and Data Description

A.  The Activism Sample

   We source our activism campaign data from the “Corporate Governance Market
Overview,” a subsection of the Thomson Reuters Eikon database, commonly referred to
as ThomsonOne.  The ThomsonOne database integrates information from multiple
sources, including SEC Schedule 13D filings, media articles, survey data, management
press releases, and dissident solicitation materials.  A  common  approach in shareholder
activism  is to rely exclusively  on SEC Schedule 13D filings to construct datasets.
However, as  Brav et al. (2008)  note,  this  method  may  introduce a  bias  toward  smaller
corporations  due to the 5%  ownership  threshold  required for  Schedule 13D  filings.  For
instance, during Elliott Management’s notable campaign against AT&T, Elliott attained
a peak ownership stake of only 1.2%. Without the comprehensive coverage provided by
the ThomsonOne database, significant  activist  campaigns such as this would remain
undocumented.
   The  ThomsonOne  database contains over  6,600  global activist campaigns  for  the
analysis period, ranging from  2000  to  2022.  While the  ThomsonOne  database  covers
campaigns  globally,  we restrict our dataset to US-based firms to minimize institutional
variability and ensure consistency, as the majority of campaigns target US-headquartered
companies  (Barros, Guedes, Santos, & Sarmento, 2023).  We concentrate the dataset  to
shareholder activism on regular corporations  and  exclude  all  events where the  target is a
closed-end fund or other non-regular corporation  (e.g.  Brav et al., 2008; Greenwood and
Schor, 2009; Klein and Zur, 2009).  Each target is then matched with CRSP for extraction
of  stock market  returns  and trading volumes.  Target firms not containing CRSP  data  for
the shortest  event  window  analyzed in our research  are eliminated.  Finally,  we  obtain
accounting data from  Compustat  for  the firms matched in CRSP.

                                   5
B. Classification

   In accordance with  von Lilienfeld-Toal and Schnitzler (2020), we apply a systematic
approach for classification of shareholder activists and define the following categories  in
our sample:  hedge  funds,  other  financial  institutions,  private  equity,  individuals,  venture
capital,  mutual  funds,  pension  funds  (including sovereign wealth funds),  corporations,
non-profits,  insiders  and  unclassified.  We  provide  a detailed description on  the defined
activist  types  in  Table A.II.
   Due to  a lack of regulatory and institutional definitions for our defined activist types,
we rely on multiple sources for classification in alignment with Klein and Zur (2009) and
von Lilienfeld-Toal and Schnitzler (2020). First, we utilize available databases with
classifications of private equity, venture capital and mutual funds from ThomsonOne and
CRSP, and match them with our database enabling for the classification of a small portion
of our sample.
   For the remaining observations, we draw upon various external sources, including
activists’ websites, investor journals, and news articles, to further classify these entities.
In instances of overlapping strategies, firms are categorized based on their primary
product offerings to investors. Events that remain ambiguously classified are designated
as “Unclassified” to preserve accuracy and minimize potential biases arising from
subjective judgment. Although we acknowledge that the classification process may  not
be entirely flawless despite adhering to the approaches of  Klein and Zur (2009)  and  von
Lilienfeld-Toal and Schnitzler  (2020),  we maintain confidence  that nearly all, if not all,
of our events are accurately categorized.
   As  our  research  focuses on shareholder activism from external shareholders aimed at
improving  financial  performance,  we  exclude  all  observations  involving  insiders,
corporations, and non-profit activists from our analysis.  Additionally, the  unclassified
group is omitted as it is unsuitable for comparative purposes. In alignment with
established practices, we also exclude targeted financial firms (SIC codes 6000–6999)
and utilities (SIC codes 4900–4999) from the sample  (von Lilienfeld-Toal & Schnitzler,
2020).  Ultimately, this positions our dataset  of 2,640 events  among the larger and most
up-to-date sample sizes used in comparable research.

C.  Three  Examples of Shareholder Activism

   To illustrate the scope of  activist types  analyzed in this study, we present three
representative cases  from our sample  and how they progress over time.  The first case  is a
hedge fund’s  intervention  that successfully influenced AT&T’s corporate  strategy. The
second case examines an individual activist’s  corporate governance  initiative with The
Timken Company, which was ultimately adopted despite opposition  from  management.
The third case explores a pension fund’s sustained advocacy for board diversity at
Fortress Biotech Inc,  which management  has continued to resist. These cases highlight
the diverse  demands  and varying levels of success across activist types, emphasizing the
potential  heterogeneity  among activists.

      C.1. Hedge Fund Activist:  Elliott  Investment  Management  L.P.  and AT&T Inc.

                                   6
   On September 9, 2019, Elliott  Investment  Management  L.P.  disclosed a $3.2 billion
stake in AT&T Inc., representing approximately 1.2% of the company's equity. In a public
letter to AT&T’s Board of Directors, Elliott outlined a  “compelling value-creation
{Citation}opportunity”,   criticizing   AT&T’s   acquisition   strategy,   particularly   the
purchases of DirecTV and Time Warner, and called for a strategic review of non-core
assets  (Business Wire, 2019).
   Elliott proposed the  “Activating AT&T Plan,”  which included increasing strategic
focus on core telecommunications services, improving operational efficiency through
cost-cutting measures, adopting disciplined capital allocation prioritizing debt reduction
and shareholder returns, and enhancing leadership and oversight by strengthening
corporate governance practices. Elliott projected that these initiatives could elevate
AT&T's share price by the end of 2021  (La Roche, 2019).
   In response, AT&T acknowledged Elliott’s perspectives and committed to engaging
with the hedge fund. On October 28, 2019, AT&T announced measures aligned with
Elliott’s   demands,   including   adding   two   new   directors   with   relevant   expertise,
committing to achieving earnings per share (EPS) of $4.50 to $4.80 by 2022, and
continuing debt reduction and capital returns to shareholders  (Stelter, 2019). However,
AT&T did not agree to all demands, notably  opposing  the divestiture of DirecTV and its
Mexican wireless business. Elliott expressed support for the announced measures, stating
they would create substantial and enduring shareholder value.  Despite holding less than
a 5% stake, Elliott significantly influenced AT&T's corporate policies.

          C.2. Individual Activist: John Chevedden and  The Timken  Company

   On March 18, 2022, The Timken Company received a shareholder proposal from John
Chevedden, an individual  activist  investor. The proposal requested that the company
amend its articles of incorporation and code of regulations to replace any supermajority
voting requirements with a simple majority vote requirement.
   In its communication to shareholders, Timken's board recommended voting against
the proposal, expressing concerns that eliminating supermajority provisions could
increase the company’s vulnerability to hostile takeovers and actions misaligned with
shareholders’  long-term interests  (The Timken Company, 2022).  Despite the board’s
opposition, the proposal was presented at the annual meeting on May 6, 2022. A majority
of shareholders voted in favor of Chevedden’s proposal, resulting in its approval.

       C.3. Pension Fund Activist:  California Public Employees Retirement System
                         (CalPERS)  and  Fortress Biotech Inc.

   On June 3, 2020,  California Public Employees Retirement System  (CalPERS)  issued
a public letter to Fortress Biotech Inc. shareholders, announcing its intention to vote
against the re-election of four directors  at the Annual General Meeting (AGM). These
include  Lindsay A. Rosenwald (Board Chair), J. Jay Lobell, Dov Klein, and Malcolm
Hoenlein (Nominating and Corporate Governance Committee members). CalPERS cited
the board's inadequate response to multiple requests for enhancing board diversity as the
primary  reason for its stance  (Fortress Biotech Inc, 2020).

                                   7
   CalPERS defined board diversity to include skill sets, gender, age, nationality, race,
sexual orientation, gender identity, and representation from historically under-represented
groups. The pension fund argued that a diverse board enhances effective oversight and
supports the execution of long-term business strategies.  To substantiate  its  position,
CalPERS referenced studies indicating that increased gender and racial diversity within
companies is strongly associated with higher financial returns.  Despite  CalPERS' efforts,
the majority of shareholders continued to support the  incumbent directors in subsequent
annual general meetings.

   Panel D provides a breakdown on the distribution of demands made by various activist
types, expressed as a percentage of total events for each category.  Since demands are not
mutually exclusive, each activist event may encompass multiple demands, but every
event includes at least one demand.
   Demands are categorized using  the methodology  developed  by  Swanson et al. (2022).
Sale  demand encompasses actions in which activists pursue hostile acquisitions and
forced  sales.  Engage  management  refers  to  instances  where  activists  engage  in
discussions with management to explore alternatives without advocating a specific
agenda. Corporate  strategy includes events where activists oppose sales, influence
strategic decisions, or initiate spinoffs. Board  composition involves demands for board
seats or efforts to alter the composition of the board. Corporate  governance covers
demands related to shareholder rights or the opposition of excessive CEO compensation.
Lastly,  other  demands  comprise  requests that do not fit into any of the categories.
   Panel D illustrates that sale demands exhibit significant variation across activist types.
Compared to hedge funds, sale demands are uncommon among individual investors,
mutual funds, and pension funds. In contrast, private equity and venture capital firms
pursue sale demands more frequently, with sale demands accounting for 12% of venture
capital activity in our sample.  This finding  bring explanatory value to  the contrasting
conclusions of  Greenwood and Schor  (2009)  and  Swanson et al.  (2022).  The former
document a higher  frequency  of sale demands by hedge funds, whereas the latter report  a
nearly twice as high proportion of sale demands for  other activist  types  relative to hedge
funds.  Our statistics  suggests that the  divergence in  findings  may  be  attributed  to  the
heterogeneity  among  non-hedge fund  activists.
  A uniform pattern is observed among hedge funds, private equity firms, venture capital
firms, and other financial institutions across most demand types, suggesting that these
categories adopt  similar  strategies in their activist activities. Notably, hedge funds
allocate a greater proportion of their efforts to changes in board composition (45.8%),
whereas private equity and venture capital firms place relatively less emphasis on
corporate strategy demands.  Individual investors and pension funds  demonstrate  notable
similarities  in  their  demands.  Compared  to  other  activist  types,  their  efforts  are
predominantly directed toward engaging  with management and  advocating for  corporate
governance reforms. Specifically, pension funds exhibit a pronounced focus on corporate
governance initiatives, with 95% of their demands falling into this category.

             III.  Characteristics of Firms Targeted by Shareholder Activists

A.  Summary Statistics and Univariate Tests

   Having established that  other  activists initiate various types of demands  in relation to
hedge funds, we seek to further understand the characteristics of the companies they
target. Specifically, we  investigate whether  our defined activist types target different
firms  compared  to  hedge  funds.  Understanding  these  differences  is  essential  for
evaluating how distinct activist strategies translate into target selection  in practice.  To
address  this  question, we analyze  characteristics of accounting variables covering the
areas   of   size,   growth,   profitability,   leverage,   liquidity,   shareholder   payouts   and
expenditures.  All variables are measured using data from the fiscal year immediately
preceding the announcement date of each targeted firm.
   We begin by examining firm size using market capitalization and total assets as
metrics. Table II indicates that private equity,  other financial institutions  and venture
capital  target smaller firms compared to hedge funds, with both mean and median
differences in market capitalization and total assets being significant at the 1% level.
Similarly, individual activists  prefer smaller firms relative to hedge funds. These findings
are consistent with  Klein and Zur (2009).  In contrast,  we find that mutual funds and
pension funds in our sample focus on larger firms relative to hedge funds,  illustrating  the
differences among non-hedge  fund activists.
   Next, we  analyze profitability using return on assets (ROA) and profit margin.  Table
II reveals that  individuals, other financials, and venture capital firms  select firms with
lower ROA and profit margins  compared to hedge funds with these differences  being
statistically significant. Specifically,  we find that  venture capital  target firms with the
lowest ROA, reporting a median value of -4.3%. Interestingly, venture capital targets also
exhibit the highest cash-to-asset ratio. However, we believe this is more attributable to
their low asset base.  These findings align with  Bethel, Liebeskind, and Opler (1998)  and
Klein and Zur (2009)  who observe that other activist types are more likely to target firms
with relatively low financial performance metrics, indicating a strategic focus on
companies with room for financial improvement.
   Conversely, mutual funds and pension funds prioritize firms with stronger profitability
metrics. This aligns with their investment mandates, which emphasize stability and
established financial performance to manage risk and deliver consistent, long-term
returns  (Wahal, 1996). Additionally, pension funds target companies with significantly
higher dividend-  and payout yields compared to hedge funds.  We find that hedge fund
targets have a median payout yield of 1.1%, whereas pension fund targets exhibit a higher
payout yield of 4.1%, with this difference being significant at the 1% level.
   By distinguishing among activist investors, we demonstrate that  activist types  select
targets based on specific financial characteristics such as size, profitability, and payout
policies.  This  finding  of heterogeneity in  selection of target firms  align with differences
in demands and support the  hypothesis  that non-hedge fund activists  exhibit distinct
differences.

                IV.  Market Response to Activist  Announcements

A. Abnormal Stock Returns Surrounding the Shareholder Activism Announcement

   A key question in evaluating activist  interventions  is whether  activists  are able to
generate value for shareholders. To assess this, we analyze abnormal stock returns
surrounding   activist   investment   announcements   across   types.   We   define   the
announcement date as the moment when information about the campaign become public,
either through a Schedule 13 D filing or other reliable source.  Additionally, we examine
abnormal trading volumes around these announcements  to examine  whether  investors
perceive these interventions as sufficiently impactful to prompt changes in their holdings.
By analyzing both price and volume reactions, we aim to uncover the market’s perception
of activism and its alignment with short-term value creation.
   To analyze  abnormal returns we apply an event study methodology outlined by
MacKinlay (1997). We use an estimation window spanning from -120 to -21 trading days
prior to the announcement date, providing a 100-day period  for estimation of risk factor
loadings.  Specifically, we estimate factor loadings for the four factors  RMRF, SMB,
HML,  and the momentum  factor, MOM,  as described  in  Carhart  (1997).
   Our  event window  is defined  in line with  Swanson et al.  (2022), ranging from  -10
trading days to  +5 trading days  surrounding  the announcement date.  We compute
abnormal returns during the event window as the difference between expected returns,
based on the estimated factor loadings, from the actual returns for each firm on each
trading day.  The  abnormal returns are further  aggregated  to cumulative abnormal returns
(CAR) for each activist type.
   Moreover, we define normal trading volume as the average trading volume over the
same  estimation  window  spanning  from  -120  to  -21  trading  days  prior  to  the
announcement date.  Finally, abnormal volume is measured as the percentage increase in
trading volume during the event window (-10, +5) relative to  the normal trading volume.
   Figure 1  reveals varying market responses to activist announcements based on  activist
type.  Consistent with prior literature  (e.g.  Brav et al., 2008; Swanson et al., 2022; von
Lilienfeld-Toal and Schnitzler, 2020),  we observe significant positive abnormal returns
of approximately 5-7% by the end of the event window for hedge funds, private equity,
venture capital, mutual funds, and other financials. This positive response reflects
investor confidence in these  activists’  ability to enhance firm value.
   Interestingly, hedge funds exhibit a pre-announcement run-up in abnormal returns,
significant at the 1% level as early as day  -5 (see Table A.III). There is no such significant
pattern for any other activist type.  This  finding  aligns with the  “wolf pack”  hypothesis,
where  hedge fund  activists coordinate or independently acquire stakes in anticipation of
activism  interventions  (Brav, Dasgupta, & Mathews, 2015; Wong, 2019).
   The two activist categories that do not display a similar abnormal return pattern are
individuals  and pension funds.  While pension funds do not exhibit statistically significant
abnormal returns (see Table A.III), potentially due to limited statistical power, individual
activists generate significant abnormal returns around the event day at the 5% significance
level. However, their  highest achieved  abnormal return  during the event window  is only
2.06%, observed on day  one. The relatively lower t-statistic for individual activists,

                                  13
despite the large sample size of 235 observations, may reflect substantial heterogeneity
within this category. Specifically,  this indicates that  some individual activists might be
perceived by the market as highly credible, while others are not, resulting in varying
market reactions to their actions.

   Figure 1 also  reports  the abnormal volume for each trading day.  We find that  for all
activist types except pension  funds, the investment is accompanied by substantial
increases in trading volume on the announcement day  of up  of around 150% for hedge
funds, private equity,  and other financials. This surge in trading activity indicates
heightened investor interest and suggests that the market anticipates value creation from
their involvement.  This is in line with the findings of  Brav et al.  (2008).  The effect is still
prevalent but to a lesser  extent  for mutual funds  and individuals.  Interestingly,  we see
higher  abnormal volumes  throughout the full event window for  venture capital.  This
pattern suggests the influence of additional, unexamined factors and may be linked to
smaller target firms with low baseline trading volumes.

   Our  results highlight distinct market reactions to activist announcements. Significant
positive abnormal returns for hedge funds, private equity, venture capital, mutual funds,
and other financials indicate the market’s favorable view of their activism  in the short
term. On the other hand,  the weaker response for individuals and pension funds
underscores skepticism about their ability to enhance firm performance.

B.  Differences in Abnormal Stock Returns Among Investor Types

   In this section we aim to further assess if the different market responses among activist
types is robust.  Specifically, we  conduct a  cross-sectional regression  analysis  to  examine
the  influence of activist type in  abnormal returns  around activist  disclosure.  The
dependent variable across all specifications is the abnormal return for each event within
the defined event window. Our regression specifications include dummy variables for all
activist types, excluding hedge funds, which serve as the reference category.
   Moreover, we implement a set of firm-specific control variables that may influence
abnormal returns. We control for firm size, profitability, leverage, Tobin’s Q, cash-to-
assets ratio, and payout yield. Additionally, we incorporate Year-Month fixed effects to
control for time-specific factors, and three-digit SIC industry fixed effects to account for
industry-specific influences. Standard errors are clustered at the three-digit SIC industry
level to address potential within-industry correlations. These  specifications  aim  to isolate
the effect of activist type on abnormal returns, ensuring that our results are not driven by
underlying firm characteristics or broader industry and temporal trends.
   In Column (1)  of Table III, the basic specification includes dummy variables for
various activist types, excluding hedge funds. The results indicate that  individual and
pension  fund activists have significant negative coefficients of  -3.74% and  -7.53%,
respectively, both significant at the 1% level. This suggests that activist events
involving  individual and  pension  fund investors are associated with  significantly  lower
abnormal returns  compared to events involving hedge funds. Other activist types, such
as  private  equity,  venture  capital,  other  financials, and  mutual  funds, do not exhibit
statistically significant differences in  abnormal returns  relative to the hedge fund
reference group.
   Column (2) restricts the sample to events where accounting information is available
prior to the event, reducing the number of observations from 2,640 to 2,413. The results
remain consistent with those in Column (1).  In Column (3), time fixed effects are
introduced for Year-Month. The coefficients for individual and pension fund activists
remain negative and statistically significant, demonstrating that the negative association
of these activist types with abnormal returns are robust to the inclusion of time-specific
controls.
   Column (4) adds industry fixed effects at the three-digit SIC code level, controlling
for industry-specific influences on abnormal returns. The coefficients for individual and
pension  fund  activists  remain  negative  and  statistically  significant,  although  the
significance level for individual activists decreases to the 5% level. This adjustment does
not substantially alter the estimated effects, reinforcing the robustness of the negative
association.

   The preceding analysis  in section IV  highlights significant discrepancies in abnormal
returns across different types of activists. Specifically, individuals and pension funds are
associated with lower abnormal returns. Several potential explanations could account for
these differences. In this section, we delve deeper into this phenomenon by examining
the nature of demands made by  defined  activist types and their respective success rates.

A.  Abnormal Stock Returns by Campaign Demand

   In this section we  examine whether  the observed differences  between activist types
persist across  all  demand categories  or  are concentrated  in  specific  ones.  In Table IV, we
report  abnormal returns for the  -10  through  +5  period surrounding the  announcement

certain cells, we concentrate only on abnormal returns with a minimum of 11 occurrences
(Klein & Zur, 2009).
   Notably, Table IV  reveal differences in abnormal returns for target firms associated
with other financial institutions compared to hedge funds. Specifically, other financial
institutions exhibit significantly lower abnormal  median  returns for corporate strategy
and  engage  management  demands.  These  findings  highlight  that  other  financial
institutions are not uniformly similar to hedge funds, but rather exhibit meaningful
differences in their market impact that vary based on the type of demand pursued.
  An  important pattern emerging from Table IV is that individuals and pension funds
remain inferior in terms of abnormal returns compared to hedge funds. Interestingly,  the
disparities are not consistent across all types of demands  but  are  significantly lower  in
areas related to management engagement and corporate governance.  While individuals

by other activist types are not significantly different from those of hedge funds across all

    VI.  Abnormal  Returns and Operational Changes  One Year  Following Activist
                                  Intervention

   While examining the short-term effects of activist announcements provides valuable
insights into immediate market reactions, it is equally important to assess whether these
effects are sustainable  on a longer time horizon  or subject to reversal. Understanding the
persistence of initial market reactions offers a deeper perspective on  activists’  ability to
create enduring value. This section investigates these dynamics over the one-year period
following activist disclosures, analyzing both market performance  and operational
outcomes across different activist types.

A.  Stock Return Performance in the  Following Year After Activist Intervention

   Similarly  to the methodology applied in  Section IV, we  perform an  analysis  of
abnormal returns  to further  examine the  market  performance of target firms  in the
subsequent year  following  activist intervention.  We  estimate the loadings of monthly
returns on the Fama-French-Carhart four-factor model over the  (-37,  -2)  month window
prior to the intervention (Swanson et al., 2022). Using these estimated factor loadings, we
calculate the abnormal returns  over the  (-1, +12)  month window surrounding the
announcement  month.

Figure 2.  Abnormal Returns  in the Following Year After Activist Intervention  by Activist

   Figure 2  illustrates  that firms targeted by hedge funds, private equity, other financials
and  venture capital  all exhibit  a  positive stock reaction  in  abnormal return  at the
announcement month (Month 0).  Interestingly, targets of these activists experience  an
upward  trend  continuing for several months  after the initial stock reaction. For hedge
funds  the  growth is steady and sustained  without signs of reversal. Private equity and
venture capital targets  display even sharper acceleration in  abnormal returns,  peaking
several months later.  Notably, the abnormal returns  of private equity and venture capital
substantially exceed those reported for hedge funds. This finding  provides valuable
insight as it details which non-hedge funds activists that may explain the higher abnormal
returns  for non-hedge fund  activists  reported  by  Klein and Zur  (2009)  and  Swanson et al.
(2022).
   Other financial institutions exhibit a sharp positive market reaction immediately
following the announcement. However, unlike the sustained growth observed for hedge
funds, private equity, and venture capital, their abnormal returns stabilize after the initial
spike and experience a modest decline in the subsequent months.

                                  21
   In contrast, individual investors do not generate the pronounced positive market
reaction observed during the announcement month for other investor types. As illustrated
in  Table A.IV, the abnormal returns associated with individual investors remain positive
and statistically significant only for a brief period before reverting to insignificance.
These findings align with our discussion in Section IV, highlighting the potential
heterogeneity within this investor category.
   Mutual fund targets experience initial gains in abnormal returns,  however, these are
followed by relatively large fluctuations compared to other activist types. The post-
announcement trend for mutual funds is characterized by high volatility and a lack of
statistical significance (see  Table A.IV  ), which may be partially explained by the small
sample  size  for  this  group.  Similarly,  pension  funds  lack  statistical  significance,
exhibiting minimal market reaction around the announcement month and remaining
relatively stable throughout the subsequent  year.  However, it  should  be acknowledged
that the  lack of statistical power may be attributed to  the  small sample sizes for these
types.
   This graphical inspection reveals  potential  heterogeneity in the market reactions
associated with different activist types, persisting even in the year following activist
intervention. These results suggest not only substantial variations in immediate market
responses but also enduring differences  over longer time horizons.

B.  Differences in Abnormal Stock Returns Among Investor Types  in  the Year Following
Activist Interventions

   We formally test whether the observed differences in the subsequent year remain
robust and statistically significant by employing a cross-sectional regression similar to
that in Section IV.  The dependent variable across all specifications is the abnormal return
for each event within the defined event window, ranging  from  -1 month to +12 months
relative to the announcement month.  Similarly, the  regression specifications include
dummy variables for  all  activist types, excluding hedge funds, which serve as the
reference category.  We  incorporate Year-Month fixed  effects, and three-digit SIC
industry fixed effects. Standard errors are clustered at the three-digit SIC industry level.
Moreover, we control for firm size, profitability, leverage, Tobin's Q, cash-to-assets ratio,
and payout yield.  These  specifications  aim  to isolate the effect of activist type on
abnormal   returns,   ensuring   that   our   results   are   not   driven   by   underlying   firm
characteristics or broader industry and temporal trends.
   In Column (1) through Column (4), the estimated coefficients for private equity,
venture capital, individuals, other financials, pension funds, and mutual funds remain
statistically insignificant,  suggesting no discernible differences in  abnormal returns
relative to hedge funds. The results are robust to sample restrictions (Column 2), time
fixed effects (Column 3), and industry fixed effects (Column 4).  Our  results  show
persistent  insignificant differences among  activist types relative to hedge funds  in the
subsequent year, contrasting to the findings of  Klein and Zur (2009)  which documents
significant outperformance for non-hedge fund activists in relation to hedge funds.

C.  One-Year  Operational  Changes in  Target Firms

   We find no significant differences in abnormal returns between non-hedge fund
activists and hedge funds in the year following their  engagements. Based on this
observation,  the  operational  impacts of different activist types  on firm performance may
be largely similar. To test this hypothesis,  we analyze the impact of activist interventions
by evaluating one-year changes in accounting performance metrics. Specifically, our
analysis  in Table VII  focuses on variables reflecting size, growth, profitability, leverage,
shareholder returns, and expenditures.
   Table VII  indicate that targets of individual investors are the only group exhibiting a
significantly lower development  in leverage ratios  compared to hedge funds. Both book
leverage and market leverage  are negative and  statistically significant at the 1% level for
these firms. This  development  in leverage may reflect the limited influence individuals
have on capital structure decisions compared to  other  activists, who might possess more
resources and bargaining power to effect such changes.  In addition, pension fund and
mutual fund targets experience significant increases in total assets compared to hedge
fund targets. Pension funds show a mean increase in the natural logarithm of total assets
of 0.052  with  statistical significance  at the 1% level. Mutual funds exhibit a mean change
of 0.115, significantly different at the 10% level.  These increases suggest that pension
and mutual fund activism may be associated with strategies that promote asset expansion.
   Regarding profitability, venture capital targets display a higher median change in ROA
compared to hedge fund targets.  As previously concluded from Table II,  venture capital
targets firms with significantly lower ROA, suggesting notable abilities to improve short-
term operational performance  among target firms.
   Overall, we do observe limited differences in accounting measures across activist types
in the  one-year  period following the announcement. This lack of significant variation
corresponds with our earlier findings of no significant differences in  abnormal returns
over the one-year period. These results indicate that, despite the distinct immediate
market reactions observed in the announcement window (-10 to +5 days), the impact of
different activist types on firm performance appears to converge  during the subsequent
year.  This discrepancy between short-term and long-term outcomes suggests that
investors initially perceive activist types differently,  potentially  due to variations in
anticipated effectiveness and reputation  (Krishnan et al., 2016).

                                  VII.  Conclusion
   This paper  provides a comprehensive examination of shareholder activism across
different activist types  and their relation  to established hedge fund activism.  By
categorizing activists into seven distinct  groups:  hedge funds,  other financial institutions,
private equity, individuals, venture capital, pension funds and mutual funds, we document
heterogeneity in target selection, immediate market reactions, demands and success rates.
   We find that activist types exhibit distinct preferences  in  selecting target firms,
prioritizing characteristics such as size, profitability, and payout policies. Private equity,
venture capital, individual investors, and other financial institutions predominantly target
smaller firms with lower market capitalization relative to hedge funds. In contrast, mutual
funds and pension funds focus on larger, more profitable firms with stronger financial
performance and higher payout yields, highlighting the strategic variation across activist
types.
  Our findings show that market reactions to activist announcements differ by type.
Hedge  funds  generate  significant  positive  abnormal  returns  of  5%  around  the
announcement date, with similar positive responses for other financial institutions, private
equity, venture capital and mutual funds. Conversely, interventions by pension funds and
individual activists yield significantly lower abnormal returns, reflecting potential market
skepticism about their effectiveness.  Moreover, our analysis reveals that  hedge funds,
private equity firms, and other financial institutions  consistently  achieve  higher  success
rates across various demand categories. In contrast, individuals and pension funds exhibit
significantly  lower success rates  within  the same  demand categories, which  are  also
associated with weaker  abnormal  returns.  This finding  supports the view that investors
anticipate lower probabilities of successful outcomes from these activists.
   Despite differences in short-term market reactions,  we conclude that  long-term firm
performance converges across activist types.  One-year  post-announcement,  other activist
types show no significant differences from hedge funds. Consistently, accounting metrics
reveal limited variation  for the period, though individual activists are associated with
smaller changes in leverage, suggesting less influence on capital structure.
   Our findings suggest that, although the market initially reacts differently to various
activist types, the  following year  effects on firms  are largely similar  both in terms of
market and operational performance.  This discrepancy between short-term and long-term
outcomes suggests that  abnormal returns surrounding activist intervention are  not
explained  by subsequent operational  improvements.  Rather, these market reactions
appear to  be  influenced by the  differing investor perceptions of the activists’  ability to
achieve  their demands.

                                    Table  A.I
                        Accounting Variable Definitions
This table presents detailed definitions of the accounting variables used in this study. All accounting data
is  based on data from Compustat.  The variables capture key aspects  of  size, growth, profitability,
leverage, liquidity, shareholder payouts and expenditures.

 Variable                 Definition
 Ln(Assets Total)           The natural logarithm of the total value of a company’s assets as reported
                           in its  financial statements  at the fiscal year end date,  measured in USD
                                    millions.
 Ln(Market Cap)           The natural logarithm of market capitalization, calculated as the stock
                            price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding  at  the fiscal year end
                                date,  measured in USD millions.
 Tobin’s Q                The ratio of the market value of a firm’s assets (total assets plus market
                                     capitalization minus book equity) to its total assets.
 Sales Growth              The percentage increase in sales compared to the previous fiscal year.
 ROA                Return on Assets, calculated as operating income (EBIT) divided by
                                     lagged total assets.
 Profit Margin             The ratio of operating  income (EBIT)  to total sales.
 Market Leverage           The ratio of total debt long-term debt plus short-term debt to the sum of
                                     total debt and market capitalization.
 Book Leverage            The ratio of total  long-term debt plus short-term debt to the sum of total
                                   debt and book equity.
 Cash/Assets                The ratio of cash and cash equivalents to total assets.
 Dividend Yield           The ratio of total dividends paid (common dividends plus preferred
                              dividends) to the sum of market capitalization and preferred stock  value.
 Payout Yield             The ratio of total payouts to shareholders (common dividends, preferred
                              dividends, and share repurchases) to the sum of market capitalization and
                                    preferred stock value.
 OpEx/Sales              The ratio of operating expenses to total sales.
 CapEx/Assets             The ratio of capital expenditures to  lagged  total assets.

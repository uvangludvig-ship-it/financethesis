A STUDY OF THE PATENT–PERFORMANCE RELATIONSHIP
IN SWEDEN

Do Patents Pay  Off? A Study of the Patent–Performance Relationship in Sweden

Abstract:

   We  build on  the study  of  Taglialatela & Barontini  (2024)  by analyzing how  the
   different stages of the patenting process – filing and granting – affect firm performance
   in Sweden.  Using  firm-level data,  we test the external validity of their findings  on
   Italian  SMEs  and extend their study  by  examining  heterogeneous effects  across  firm
   sizes. Applying staggered difference-in-differences (DiD) and triple-difference (DDD)
   estimators,  we  find  that  filing  without  securing  a grant is  associated with  reduced
   turnover growth across  all firm groups.  In contrast,  obtaining a  patent  grant  yields
   significant revenue gains for SMEs, while large firms experience no such gains  and
   exhibit lower  profitability  post-grant.  We challenge  the  assumption  that larger firms
   capture  greater  patent returns,  and  instead  suggest that  SMEs are better positioned to
   leverage grants through external signaling. We further highlight the role of policy and
   capital markets in shaping  performance  outcomes  across  the  patenting stages.  Our
   findings suggest that the  economic value of  intellectual property  is  not uniform, but
   contingent on  firm-level characteristics and  the broader institutional context.

Keywords:

   Patents,  Firm  Performance,  Firm Size  Heterogeneity,  Intellectual Property

1 Introduction

Over 3.5 million patent applications were filed worldwide in 2023. This trend, reflecting an increase
of 2.7% over the prior year, highlights the growing importance of intellectual property (IP) in today’s
knowledge economy (World Intellectual Property Organization, 2024). Among the various types of IP,
patents play a pivotal role by granting inventors temporary monopoly rights to protect R&D investments,
incentivize innovation, and signal firm quality to the market. Despite their intended purpose, however, the
true economic impact of patenting on firm performance remains widely debated by policy and academic
circles alike.
   The existing research highlights the empirical challenges of connecting patent activity to firm per-
formance beyond correlation. Scholars point to issues ranging from endogeneity and unobserved hetero-
geneity, to the inherent ambiguity and quality limitations of patent data itself. Prior studies have tackled
these challenges using distinct causal identifcation strategies. Balasubramanian and Sivadasan (2011)
and Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), for example, apply difference-in-differences (DiD) frameworks
with careful control group construction to infer causality; the former finding that labour productivity in-
creased by 3.5%-10.5% after patenting. Other studies, such as Kogan, Papanikolaou, Seru, and Stoffman
(2017) and Farre-Mensa, Hegde, and Ljungqvist (2020), adopt instrumental variable approaches using
exogenous variations from R&D tax credits and examiner leniency respectively, with Farre-Mensa et al.
(2020) reporting that patents granted can increase startup sales growth by as much as 79.5%.
   Building on this growing body of literature, we extend the work of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024),
who uniquely distinguish between the two stages of the patenting process: (1) filing a patent and (2)
being granted a patent. From their staggered DiD approach on Italian small and medium-sized en-
terprises (SMEs), they find that filing alone leads to positive sales increases of 3-4% due to gains in
knowledge stocks, whereas granted patents have no measurable additional benefit. Using firm-level data
from Sweden’s Retriever Business, we test the external validity of the authors’ findings in a different
institutional and economic context. Our main contribution is to assess whether the effects of patent-
ing vary systematically by firm size – an underexplored dimension as prior studies focus exclusively on
startups, SMEs, or large firms, or do not distinguish by firm size at all (Balasubramanian & Sivadasan,
2011; Conti, Thursby, & Thursby, 2013; Farre-Mensa et al., 2020; Taglialatela & Barontini, 2024). In
particular, Taglialatela and Barontini (2024) suggest that SMEs may not benefit from granted patents
due to limited resources to enforce their protection rights – implicitly assuming that large firms with
greater resources should benefit more. We make this assumption explicit and test it directly by incorpo-
rating both SMEs and large firms into our empirical framework. Specifically, we estimate staggered DiD
models overall and by firm size, and introduce a triple-difference (DDD) specification to examine how
filing and granting effects vary both within and across firm sizes. Furthermore, we incorporate method-
ological improvements drawing from prior studies (Balasubramanian & Sivadasan, 2011; Farre-Mensa
et al., 2020; Kogan et al., 2017), including the use of text-matching algorithms to link patent records to
firm identifiers and implementing a stricter sample construction process to improve causal inference.
   For our study, we follow the classifications of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024) and Balasubramanian
and Sivadasan (2011), grouping firms into three categories:

   • Granted filers: Firms that filed for and successfully obtained a patent.

   • Non-granted filers: Firms that filed for a patent but were rejected, withdrew their application,
      or remained pending for more than five years.

   • Non-filers: Firms that did not file for any patents during the sample period.

   We structure our empirical analysis into two parts. To evaluate the filing effect, we compare non-
granted filers (treatment group) to non-filers (control group). To assess the granting effect, we compare
granted filers (treatment group) to non-granted filers (control group). These comparisons allow us to
estimate the effects of filing and granting of a patent separately.
   A key part of our study is our data collection and identification strategy. We source patent data
from PATSTAT and match it to firm identifiers via PAtLink and Serrano databases using text-matching
algorithms, which is then linked to firm-level data from Retriever Business. Following the approach of
Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), we employ a ±4–year event window to analyze the effects of filing
and granting of patents. As Retriever Business covers the period 2005-2024, we thus restrict the sample
to patents filed between 2010 and 2019. Furthermore, we only include patents filed at least three years
apart to isolate the effect of each patenting event. For both the filing and granting analyses, we match
control firms to treatment firms through a two-step selection process: first, we select up to 30 controls

                                             3
based on industry, age, and revenue; and second, we apply one-to-one nearest neighbour matching on
key financial covariates. We notably extend the methodology of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024) by
requiring granted patents, in addition to filed patents, to be at least three years apart to reduce further
confounding effects. Moreover, we consolidate firm structures at the group level to mitigate double-
counting patent applications, and categorize companies into SMEs and large firms using the European
Commission’s definition (European Commission, 2020).
    As the first notable step of the patenting process is filing, we evaluate this treatment effect by applying
a ±4–year event window centered around the filing year following Taglialatela and Barontini (2024). We
find that filing a patent – without securing a grant – has a negative impact on firm turnover growth: firms
experience a decline in turnover growth of around 10 percentage points (pp) in the year following filing.
Using a triple-difference model to distinguish by firm size, we further observe that both SMEs and large
firms experience similar negative effects. Overall, these findings contradict Taglialatela and Barontini
(2024), who report positive post-filing performance effects for Italian SMEs, which they attribute to
knowledge gains derived from the patenting process. We find that in Sweden, firms regardless of size,
appear to not benefit from such knowledge gains. For a possible explanation, we explore differences
in institutional contexts as per Kogan et al. (2017). Compared to Italy, we suggest that Sweden’s less
generous R&D tax regime may have made filing more burdensome than beneficial for firms.
   To examine the impact of obtaining a granted patent, we appply a similar staggered DiD framework
centered around the granting year. Overall, we find no significant post-granting effect when pooling
all firms. However, our triple-difference estimates show that SMEs outperform large firms in terms of
profitability, with ROE higher by 26 pp two years post-grant. This is driven primarily by large firms
underperforming rather than SMEs attaining significant profitability gains. When analyzing absolute
turnover, on the other hand, we find that granted SMEs earn 6–11 MSEK more in revenue relative
to non-granted SMEs, while large firms show no significant effects. These findings again contradict
Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), who argue that SMEs lack the resources to enforce granted patents.
We instead align with Farre-Mensa et al. (2020), who show that patent grants can improve financing
access and firm signaling for smaller companies. Differences in capital market conditions thus offer one
possible explanation for why granted patents are more financially impactful for Swedish SMEs than their
Italian counterparts. Meanwhile, large firms underperform in relation to SMEs post-grant as they often
use patents defensively rather than commercially, in line with Lemley and Shapiro (2005), and may even
face negative returns in relation to other non-granted large firms.
   Our findings reinforce the idea that the economic value of patenting is not uniform across firms and
depends critically on firm characteristics, institutional context, and the stage of the patenting process.
We suggest that filing, in and of itself, offers little benefit in the Swedish context, and that firms,
especially SMEs, should aim to pursue patents with the goal of securing enforceable rights. The absence
of positive outcomes for large firms may reflect strategic misalignment in how patents are used — whether
defensively, bureaucratically, or as portfolio padding — rather than as tools for commercial exploitation.
We also acknowledge a possible sample bias, as our identification strategy excludes frequent patent filers.
Overall, we highlight the importance of firm-specific factors, institutional conditions, and the stage of
the patenting process when evaluating the returns to innovation. The economic impact of patenting, and
IP more broadly, is suggested to be more nuanced than often assumed.
    The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the background and existing literature
on the patent-performance relationship. Section 3 details our empirical methodology, including the
econometric models used, sample construction process, and descriptive statistics. Section 4 presents the
results for our study. We discuss our findings and their implications in Section 5, and conclude the paper
in Section 6.
2 Literature Review

This paper contributes to the extensive and growing literature on patents’ impact on firm performance
(Balasubramanian & Sivadasan, 2011; Budish, Roin, & Williams, 2016; Conti et al., 2013; Farre-Mensa
et al., 2020; Galasso & Schankerman, 2015; Hochberg, Serrano, & Ziedonis, 2018; Kogan et al., 2017;
Lemley & Shapiro, 2005; Shu, Tian, & Zhan, 2022; Taglialatela & Barontini, 2024).
   More specifically, by employing a staggered DiD approach comparing patentees with non-patentees,
Taglialatela and Barontini (2024) find that Italian SMEs between 2005 and 2010 experienced a signif-
icant increase in turnover immediately after filing, which they attribute to knowledge gains triggered
during the patenting process. Other studies similarly investigate the causal impact of patents, but re-
port different findings based on their methodologies. Farre-Mensa et al. (2020) exploit examiner leniency

                                             4
as an instrumental variable in U.S. startups and find that receiving a granted patent led to 55% higher
employment growth and 80% higher sales growth, largely driven by startups’ improved access to external
financing. Conti et al. (2013) support this conclusion in their study using exogenous variation in venture
capital (VC) activity, showing that Israeli startups filed for more patents after their first funding rounds
to signal firm quality to new external investors; filing more patents was positively associated with at-
tracting more VC backing. Similarly, Balasubramanian and Sivadasan (2011) focus on established U.S.
manufacturing firms rather than startups, yet still find that first-time patent grants led to substantial
firm growth, with around 2.7 new product introductions and up to $4.4 million in additional sales relative
to non-patentees. However, Lemley and Shapiro (2005) are among the scholars that remain skeptical of
the economic value of patents, highlighting that in the U.S. only 1–2% of patents are litigated, just 0.1%
proceed to trial, and about 46% of litigated patents are invalidated. The authors suggest that the legal
and economic benefits of patents are probabilistic and often strategic.
   Given the differences in findings across studies, probably due to varying data, methodology, and
outcome variables, our paper contributes to the literature by providing evidence on the impact of patents
on firm performance in another context. First, unlike prior studies conducted in the U.S., Japan, or
Italy, we study Sweden – one of the most innovative economies according to the Global Innovation Index
(Dutta, Lanvin, Rivera Le´on, & Wunsch-Vincent, 2024). Compared to continental Europe and the U.S.,
Sweden offers a distinct legal environment and capital market structure that provides an opportunity to
explore whether prior conclusions hold in a different context (Andersson & L¨o¨of, 2012; Dutta et al., 2024;
La Porta, Lopez-De-Silanes, Shleifer, & Vishny, 1997). Second, we introduce firm size as a moderating
factor. Whereas earlier work tends to focus on either startups, SMEs, or large firms — or does not
distinguish firm size at all — we explicitly examine how patent effects differ across firm groups using
a triple-difference framework. This approach is motivated by existing research showing that SMEs and
large firms exhibit distinct patenting behavior and outcomes: SMEs patent less frequently but often
derive greater commercial benefits through licensing and access to external financing (Acs & Audretsch,
1988; European Patent Office, 2017; Hughes, 2010). Furthermore, Taglialatela and Barontini (2024)
suggest that SMEs may not benefit from granted patents due to limited resource capacity, thus implicitly
assuming that larger firms should benefit more. We design our empirical framework to explicitly test
how patent filing and patent granting vary both within and across firm groups. Lastly, we improve
the identification strategy for causal inference across patenting stages. Compared to Taglialatela and
Barontini (2024), we implement stricter sample restrictions when comparing non-granted filers to non-
filers and granted filers to non-granted filers. These refinements are informed by best practices from
earlier work (Balasubramanian & Sivadasan, 2011; Farre-Mensa et al., 2020; Kogan et al., 2017), and
include text-matching algorithms to link patent records to firms, along with stricter timing constraints
to better isolate treatment effects.
   Ultimately, we provide evidence on how firm performance varies across the different stages of the
patenting process, and the factors that influence this relationship.
3 Data and Methodology
3.1 Methodology

Ideally, two experimental conditions are required to investigate the causal relationship between patents
and performance:

   1. To assess the impact of filing, we would need two groups of firms with the exact same inventions,
      where one group files for a patent and the other does not.

   2. To assess the impact of granting, we would need two groups of firms that filed the exact same
      patent applications, where one group is granted their patent applications and the other is not.

   In a perfect scenario, these treatment decisions would be made randomly. However, since we rely on
observational data in practice, we adopt the framework of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024) to address
potential simultaneity and heterogeneity issues related to studying the causal link between patents and
performance, as addressed by previous studies (Balasubramanian & Sivadasan, 2011; Farre-Mensa et
al., 2020; Kogan et al., 2017). To mitigate potential bias, we carefully construct our sample using
a time-bound window, applying a rule to select only patents filed at least 3 years apart to isolate the
impact of individual patenting events. This rule excludes most high-frequency patenting firms in Sweden,

                                             5
such as Ericsson and AstraZeneca as seen in Table 2, but we consider this trade-off essential for reducing
confounding effects and ensuring a robust identification strategy as per Taglialatela and Barontini (2024).
   Moreover, firms that file for or are granted patents may differ systematically from those that do not
(Kannebley, Sekkel, & Ara´ujo, 2010). We address this by using propensity score matching (PSM) to
create comparable treatment and control groups, aligning with the approach of Taglialatela and Barontini
(2024). The PSM approach allows us to estimate the probability of filing or being granted a patent for
each firm based on observable characteristics – firms with similar probabilities are then matched across
treatment and control groups using nearest neighbour matching with caliper distance 0.2. While this
strict matching reduces the sample size, we consider it a necessary trade-off to construct balanced control
groups and ensure the validity of our design. The treatment and control group definitions for both the
filing and granting analyses are summarized in Table 1. Section 3.3 provides a detailed explanation of
the two-step control group selection process.
   A key addition to Taglialatela and Barontini’s (2024) methodology is our more conservative approach
to corporate group structure. Since financial metrics are often reported and allocated at the group level
(IFRS Foundation, 2024), we consolidate subsidiaries under their parent firm. For example, Volvo Trucks
and Volvo Energy are treated as a single unit under Volvo AB to reduce double-counting. We also make
methodological adjustments, such as an additional criterion to select patents that are granted at least
3 years apart to further reduce confounding effects. Lastly, to examine heterogeneous treatment effects
by firm size, we classify firms according to the European Commission’s SME definition, i.e. firms with
fewer than 250 employees and either an annual turnover of less than €50 million or total assets below
€43 million (European Commission, 2020).

3.2 Econometric Model

Even after matching treatment and control firms based on strict observable factors as outlined in Sec-
tion 3.1, it is reasonable to assume that matched firms may still differ in unobservable characteristics.
To address this, we exploit the longitudinal dimension of our data and apply a staggered difference-in-
differences (DiD) estimator with firm fixed effects, as per Taglialatela and Barontini (2024). This method
complements our matching methodology by controlling for time-invariant unobservable firm character-
istics that matching alone cannot account for – thereby strengthening causal identification in both the
filing effect (comparing non-granted filers with non-filers) and the granting effect (comparing granted
filers with non-granted filers).

impact of patenting events across all firms – without distinguishing between large firms and SMEs – we

3.3 Sample Construction

3.3.1 Data Collection
We collect our data according to the methodology specified above. We begin by obtaining patent data
through PATSTAT, selecting all applications filed by Swedish applicants between 2000-2024 for our
initial dataset. This includes information such as filing year, application status, grant date, totalling
616,625 observations. To link patents to financial data, we need each applicant’s Swedish organisation
number. Due to inconsistencies in PATSTAT data (e.g. missing characters, typos), we use a combination

                                             7
of PAtLink and Serrano to match applicant names to organisation numbers through application IDs,
exact and fuzzy name matching with 95% confidence, and manual verification, as inspired by prior
studies (Balasubramanian & Sivadasan, 2011; Farre-Mensa et al., 2020; Kogan et al., 2017). We cluster
subsidiaries under their parent companies using Serrano data. Following this firm ID mapping, we clean
the dataset by removing duplicates and individual applicants, and retain only organisation numbers
starting with “5” (limited companies), which leaves us with 414,079 observations across 8,964 unique
firms.
   As Retriever Business has available financial data for Swedish firms from 2005-2024, we limit our
patents to those filed between 2010 and 2019 to ensure full coverage for our ±4-year analysis window.
Importantly, we select only patents filed at least three years apart. These filters remove 353,727 applica-
tions and 4,842 firms, including mostly high-frequency filers such as Ericsson and AstraZeneca as seen in
Table 2. For granted patents, we apply a separate 3-year separation rule for grant dates in addition to
the filing-year criterion. Firms with both granted and non-granted applications are excluded, resulting
in a sample of 3,224 applications across 3,090 unique firms.

3.3.2 Control Group Selection Process
For the first part of our empirical analysis – filing effect – we use non-patenting firms as the control
group. We start by identifying all Swedish firms through Serrano and exclude those with patent filings
between 2010 and 2019. After filtering for valid financial data, we retain 239,187 non-patenting firms as
potential controls pre-matching.
    In the second part of our empirical analysis – granting effect – we use our existing non-granted filers
as the control group. Unlike the filing analysis, which requires non-granted filers to have at least a
three-year gap between patent filings to isolate events, the granting analysis focuses specifically on the
timing of patent grants. We therefore include only non-granted filers who have not received a granted
patent within three years prior to their filing. For example, a firm that filed for patents in both 2011
and 2012 but was never granted a patent, would qualify as a control in the granting analysis, but not as
a treated firm in the filing analysis. The same non-granted filer can thus qualify in one part of the study
but not the other, based on which type of patenting effect we aim to isolate.
   Having compiled raw datasets for both control groups, we follow a two-step matching process based
on the approach by Taglialatela and Barontini (2024). In the first step, we select up to 30 comparable
control firms for each treatment firm using three key criteria: industry (SNI digits 2-4), firm age, and
revenue, as defined in Table 11 (see Appendix). For example, if a manufacturing firm filed for a patent
in 2008, we compare it to a non-filing manufacturing firm with data from the exact same year. In
our granting analysis, while we match firms based on available data on the granting year, we add an
additional condition to ensure that both firms filed for patents around the same time. These filters result

4 Results
4.1 Patent Filing and Firm Performance

Filing for a patent marks the first step in the patenting process, and we begin our empirical analysis by
evaluating its effects. Using the staggered DiD framework described in Section 3.2, we estimate three
specifications with non-granted filers as the treatment group and non-filers as the control group.
   The validity of the DiD methodology relies on the parallel trends assumption that, in the absence
of treatment, the outcome variables in the treatment and control groups would have followed similar
trajectories (Roth, Sant’Anna, Bilinski, & Poe, 2023). Since this assumption cannot be formally tested,
we adopt the visual inspection approach used by Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), where Figure 3
presents the trends of the four outcome variables over time. We observe relatively parallel pre-treatment
trends for turnover, turnover growth, and ROE, supporting our assumption. While ROE growth trends
are slightly less aligned, we argue that our two-step matching procedure outlined in Section 3.3 mitigates
concerns about pre-existing differences between groups. Overall, we find sufficient evidence to justify the
DiD approach in this context.

5 Discussion

The findings from our analyses of patent filing and patent granting shed light on how different stages
of the patenting process affect firm performance in the Swedish context. Overall, our results diverge
significantly from those of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), both in terms of direction and magnitude
of the observed effects.
   Tables 5, 6, and 7 examine the effects of patent filing on Swedish firms, both in aggregate and by
firm size. As shown in Table 5, filing is associated with a significant decline in turnover growth at the
aggregate level. When disaggregated by firm size using the DDD estimates in Table 6, we observe that
this negative effect persists for both SMEs and large firms, with declines in turnover growth observed
up to two years post-filing. These results contradict those of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), who
report that Italian SMEs experienced moderate gains in turnover growth following patent filing — 3.5
percentage points in the first year and 2.8 percentage points in the second — attributing the effect
to knowledge stock gains triggered by the filing process as seen in Figure 5. While both studies find
no significant impact on profitability measures such as ROE or ROE growth, our findings suggest that
Swedish firms do not benefit from the filing stage in the same way as Italian firms, pointing to potential
contextual differences in how firms derive value from early-stage patent activity.

Figure 5: Hypothesis A outlined by Taglialatela and Barontini (2024).
The authors propose that patent filing – without a subsequent grant – has a positive effect on SME firm
performance. They find statistically significant evidence in support, attributed to knowledge stock gains.
In contrast, our results for Swedish firms show negative post-filing effects for both SMEs and large firms.

   Table 8, 9, and 10 show how firms respond after being granted a patent. Our results also diverge from
those of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), albeit in a different direction. While the authors hypothesize
a positive impact from granting, as illustrated in Figure 6, their results show no significant performance
effect for Italian SMEs. They attribute this to SMEs’ limited resources to commercialize patent rights.
Implicit in this reasoning is thus the assumption that larger firms, with greater resources, should benefit
more from being granted a patent.
   At the aggregate level, Table 8 shows no significant post-granting effects for Swedish firms, which is
consistent with the findings of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024). However, as this aggregated approach
may mask firm-level differences, we use DDD estimates in Table 9 to distinguish between SMEs and large
firms. The disaggregated results show a notable pattern: large firms experience significantly negative
profitability effects, with substantially lower ROE growth than non-granted large firms in year one
through four. Meanwhile, the interaction terms show that SMEs outperform large firms in relative
terms. However, these relative differences are mainly driven by the under performance of large firms
post-granting, as SMEs themselves exhibit no statistically significant profitability gains. Taglialatela
and Barontini (2024) also report no meaningful benefits for SMEs, with ROE increases of 0.9 pp in
year two being statistically insignificant. In terms of absolute turnover, however, Table 10 shows that
granted SMEs did experience significant gains of 6 MSEK in year one and 11.6 MSEK in year four,
relative to non-granted SMEs. Large firms, in contrast, experience no significant effects. These findings

                                            21
further contradict those of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), who argue that SMEs lack the resources
to benefit from patent grants. Our results suggest that Swedish SMEs achieve tangible revenue gains
in the short to medium term, consistent with prior evidence from studies such as Farre-Mensa et al.
(2020). Meanwhile, large firms – despite having greater resources – fail to convert grants into financial
returns, which challenges the assumption that greater firm size is linked to greater appropriation of
patent returns.

Figure 6: Hypothesis B outlined by Taglialatela and Barontini (2024).
The authors propose that patent granting has a positive effect on SME firm performance. However, they
find no statistically significant evidence in support, attributed to SMEs’ lack of resources. In contrast,
our results for Swedish firms show positive post-granting effects for SMEs and negative effects for large
firms.

    A key divergence between our findings and those of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024) lies in the effect
of filing a patent without securing a grant. While their study – focused on Italian SMEs between 2005-
2010 – suggests that the patenting process itself strengthens firms’ knowledge stocks through learning
activities that translate into financial returns, we do not observe similar benefits among Swedish firms.
A possible explanation for this divergence lies in differences in institutional R&D support, with prior
work by Kogan et al. (2017) underscoring the importance of R&D tax credit regimes in shaping firm-level
innovation outcomes. In Italy, profitable SMEs during 2005-2010 received an average implied R&D tax
subsidy of 4.7%, while Swedish firms during 2010-2019 operated under a flatter and less generous R&D
support system, with an average implied tax subsidy of 2.6% (Organisation for Economic Co-operation
and Development, 2024). Given that the patenting process can be costly – with filing and search fees
alone amounting to at least €1,655 (European Patent Office, 2024) – such institutional support plays
a critical role in shaping whether filing in itself leads to knowledge gains or instead imposes a financial
burden on the firm. Policy differences point to the fact that Italian SMEs were better positioned to
absorb costs and derive internal benefits from the act of filing a patent, as proposed by Taglialatela
and Barontini (2024). In Sweden, however, lesser fiscal support likely rendered the filing process –
without the corresponding benefit of granted monopoly rights – more burdensome and less conducive to
positive firm outcomes. The negative effects we observe are largely short-term, concentrated within the
first two years post-filing and limited to turnover growth. We suggest that in the absence of sufficient
policy support, the initial costs of filing may strain firm resources without generating immediate returns,
whereas stronger incentives — such as those in Italy — can help firms better absorb these costs and
potentially translate filing efforts into future performance gains.
   A second key contrast in our findings concerns the effect of patent granting on SMEs. While
Taglialatela and Barontini (2024) find no significant benefits for Italian SMEs from granted patents
– arguing that SMEs lack resources to enforce patent rights – we observe the opposite for Swedish SMEs.
Specifically, SMEs in Sweden with granted patents earn 6 MSEK more in revenue one year post-granting,
and 11 MSEK more by year four, compared to non-granted SMEs. Unlike filing, patent grants confer
enforceable legal rights that open channels such as licensing, collateralization, and external signaling —
mechanisms that extend beyond the internal knowledge gains associated with filing. To explore a possible

                                            22
explanation, we thus draw on Farre-Mensa et al. (2020), who show that granted patents can serve as
valuable signals to investors and improve access to external capital, particularly for smaller firms. While
our study does not directly examine external financing, we suggest that such a signaling and financing
channel may be stronger in Sweden than in Italy. The European Central Bank (2025) highlights Sweden’s
capital market as one of the most developed in Europe, marked by strong IPO and VC activity, while
Swedish Intellectual Property Office (2025) undescores the nation’s robust legal framework for leveraging
IP through collateral, licensing, and other financial channels. Although Sweden’s flatter R&D tax credit
regime may reduce the internal benefits associated with filing, the broader financial ecosystem appears
more conducive to translating granted patents into external value. By contrast, data from the Bank of
Italy (2025) indicate that Italy’s VC ecosystem remains significantly underdeveloped. Between 2021 and
2023, total VC investment in Italy was only 20% of that in comparable EU countries, and just 3% of VC
exits occurred via IPOs. Institutional investor participation is limited, and major public support ini-
tiatives for VC only began in 2019 – well after Taglialatela and Barontini’s (2024) study period. These
structural differences constitute one plausible explanation for why granted patents may carry greater
signaling value in Swedish capital markets, making them more financially consequential for Swedish
SMEs compared to their Italian counterparts. We suggest that the more developed markets in Sweden
may explain the lagged revenue effects observed among SMEs — with the larger and more statistically
significant gains in year four pointing to medium-term impacts likely driven by commercialization and
financing opportunities that materialize over time, rather than immediate signaling alone.
   However, while the aforementioned factors help explain why Swedish SMEs may benefit more from
granted patents than Italian SMEs, they do not account for an additional finding: that large firms in
Sweden not only underperform relative to SMEs after receiving a patent grant, but also underperform
relative to other non-granted large firms. Firstly, we challenge Taglialatela and Barontini’s (2024) im-
plicit assumption that firm size and greater resources lead to greater appropriation of patent-related
returns. A possible explanation is that a majority of large firms pursue patenting for primarily defensive
and commercially vague purposes, such as litigation deterrence or portfolio expansion, which may not
translate directly into commercial performance gains (Lemley & Shapiro, 2005). Such strategies can even
introduce additional costs and legal burdens post-grant. This may furthermore explain why large firms
show negative effects on ROE measures rather than on turnover — as administrative costs tied to patent
maintenance can depress profit margins without affecting top-line revenue. In contrast, SMEs have been
found to patent less frequently than large firms but derive relatively higher commercial value through
licensing, external financing, or signaling to investors (Acs & Audretsch, 1988; European Patent Office,
2017; Hughes, 2010). To further understand why granted patents appear detrimental for large firms, we
also consider potential sample biases. For example, our 3-year separation rule excludes frequent filers
like Ericsson and AstraZeneca as outlined in Table 2. It is reasonable to assume this exclusion skews
the large-firm sample toward potentially less innovative firms who are less experienced with leveraging
granted patents. This issue is less pronounced for innovative SMEs, however, who file fewer patents and
are thus less affected by the 3-year separation rule in relation to their larger counterparts. Additionally,
the granted large-firm treatment group is small (n = 12), which limits statistical power and increases
the risk of estimation noise. Although the limited sample size reflects our decision to uphold a strict
identification strategy in line with Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), it may introduce sampling bias.
While the internal validity of our methodology is robust, these limitations should be kept in mind when
interpreting the granting effect for large firms.
6 Conclusion

In conclusion, we find that the economic impact of patenting varies by both firm size and the stage of
the patenting process. Our results show that filing a patent — without securing a grant— is associated
with short-term negative effects on turnover growth among Swedish firms. In contrast, granted patents
are linked to significant revenue gains for Swedish SMEs, while large firms experience no such benefit
and in some cases exhibit negative effects on profitability.
    Building on the work of Taglialatela and Barontini (2024), we replicate and extend their identification
strategy by applying it to a new context: Swedish firms with patent applications filed between 2010 and
2019. Using a staggered difference-in-differences approach extended into a triple-difference model, we
go beyond the original study by comparing effects both within and across SMEs and large firms, while
implementing a stricter control group selection process to improve causal inference. This enables us to
evaluate not only the original hypotheses regarding SMEs and patenting, but also the broader implicit
assumption that larger firms are more likely to benefit from patent protection.

                                            23
   Our results challenge both claims. Swedish SMEs show no benefit from filing alone, but do benefit
significantly from granted patents. Meanwhile, larger firms neither gain from grants nor filings, and
may even experience adverse profitability effects. Ultimately, our findings highlight the importance of
firm-specific characteristics in determining innovation outcomes, and emphasize how institutional and
financial contexts — such as R&D policy and capital market conditions — shape the returns to intellectual
property.

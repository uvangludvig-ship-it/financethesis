The Impact of IPO Lockup Expiration: European Evidence

Abstract:

This paper provides evidence that European firms experience systematic return and
trading patterns around lockup expirations, but these effects depend on ownership
structure and market liquidity. We find a pronounced negative price drift ahead of
expiration, consistent with investors gradually positioning themselves for the increased
supply of shares once insiders are allowed to sell. At the unlock date itself, trading
activity rises sharply while price reactions remain muted, indicating that the event
operates primarily as a liquidity shock rather than an information surprise. The post-
unlock period shows a clear reversal  in returns, particularly for non-VC/PE-backed
firms, as temporary price pressure unwinds once insider-related uncertainty dissipates.
These dynamics are strongest on exchanges with deeper liquidity and higher
institutional participation, suggesting that reactions to  lockup expirations reflect the
joint interplay of insider incentives, information asymmetry, investor positioning, and
exchange-level liquidity conditions.

Keywords:

   Lockup expiration, Liquidity, Insider selling, Information asymmetry, Market
   microstructure, IPOs, Venture Capital, Private Equity

1.    Introduction

An Initial Public O↵ering (IPO) brings outside investors into the firm for the first
time, creating a separation between informed insiders and uninformed public share-
holders thereby giving rise to classic problems of information asymmetry and agency.
   Lockup agreements are one of the main tools used to address these frictions. A
lockup prevents pre-IPO shareholders from selling their shares for a fixed period after
listing, typically 180 days. These restrictions usually apply to founders, executives,
employees, and early financial investors such as venture capital (VC) and private
equity (PE) funds. Lockups are contractual rather than legal requirements, negoti-
ated with underwriters to support an orderly aftermarket. The terms, including the
duration and the shareholders covered, are disclosed in the IPO prospectus.
   These restrictions shape the trading environment after the IPO. While the lockup
is in force, insiders cannot sell, and the free float is limited. This constrains the sup-
ply of shares available to trade. Secondary-market liquidity may be thin, and price
discovery may be slow. When the lockup expires, this changes abruptly. Insiders are
allowed to sell, the free float increases, and the market must absorb a potential wave
of selling. The expiration date, often called the unlock date, therefore represents a
discrete and predictable change in supply and in insider trading constraints.
   Empirical evidence from U.S. markets shows that this predictable change mat-
ters. Field and Hanka (2001) find that U.S. IPOs in 1988–1997 experience significant
negative abnormal returns and sharp volume spikes at lockup expiration. Brav and
Gompers (2003) report similar patterns and an average unlock-day return of about
-2% for VC-backed IPOs.  These results indicate that although lockup terms are
public, markets do not fully adjust to them in advance, and that underreaction,
supply shocks, and trading frictions also play a role. Recognizing the narrow U.S.
focus of this evidence, Bruton, G., Filatotchev, I., Chahine, S., and Wright, M.
(2010) argue that IPO research should expand beyond the U.S. and examine how
institutional and market conditions in other regions shape outcomes.
   Evidence for European markets is much limited, and the market environment
has changed markedly since the early U.S. studies. MiFID reforms, electronic order
books, high-frequency trading, and harmonized disclosure standards have increased
transparency and liquidity on Europe’s major exchanges.  Lockup terms are now
more consistently disclosed and are accessible to market participants. It is therefore
unclear whether the classic lockup e↵ects documented for U.S. IPOs still apply in a
modern European setting.
   Against this background, this study addresses the identified research gap by
investigating how lockup expirations a↵ect post-listing stock returns and trading
volumes around the unlock date and what these reactions imply about the current
relevance of classic U.S.-based lockup theories in a modern European context. The
analysis uses IPOs from 2009–2019 listed on the major European stock exchanges:
London Stock Exchange in London, Deutsche B¨orse in Frankfurt, Euronext in Paris,
Nasdaq Stockholm in Stockholm, and Borsa Italiana in Milan.

   We focus on two main research questions:
   1. How do stock returns and trading volumes adjust around IPO lockup expira-
     tions in major European equity markets?

   2. To what extent do firm, ownership, and market characteristics explain these
      reactions?
   Our empirical strategy follows the standard approach used in the IPO lockup lit-
erature, as established by Field and Hanka (2001). First, we conduct an event study
to examine market reactions around lockup expirations.  We compute cumulative
abnormal returns (CAR) and abnormal trading volume (ATV) around each lockup
expiration. CAR is measured relative to a market model. ATV is defined as actual
turnover minus a benchmark level. We consider three windows: pre-unlock (-50 to
-6 trading days), event window (-1 to +1 trading days), and post-unlock (+11 to
+50 trading days). This setup allows us to track anticipatory price movements, the
immediate reaction, and any subsequent reversal, along with the associated changes
in trading activity. Secondly, we estimate OLS multivariate regressions with CAR
and ATV in the event window (-1 to +1 trading days) as dependent variables, while
controlling for ownership structure, firm characteristics, and exchange listing. Re-
gressions are conducted for three subsamples of IPOs: All firms, Venture Capital- or
Private Equity-backed (VC/PE), and neither Venture Capital- nor Private Equity-
backed (non VC/PE).
   Our findings suggest several interpretations. The negative pre-unlock drift indi-
cates that investors discount expected insider selling well before the event. Stronger
pre-unlock declines for VC/PE-backed and long-lockup firms match the idea that
these issuers face higher perceived information risk. The small event-window CAR,
combined with a strong volume spike, suggests that the unlock acts mainly as a
liquidity shock rather than a pure information surprise.   Prices adjust somewhat
but do not collapse on the event day.  Most of the repricing seems to occur grad-
ually beforehand.  The positive post-unlock CAR points to a return reversal once
selling pressure fades and uncertainty about insider behavior is resolved.   This is
in line with short-horizon reversals studied by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) and
with models where temporary price pressure dissipates after order-flow imbalances
clear. The multivariate regression results emphasize the roles of liquidity and mar-
ket structure. Exchange dummies explain a large share of the variation in abnormal
volume and matter for VC/PE-backed CARs. This suggests that microstructure fea-
tures, depth, market-making technology, and the presence of sophisticated traders
shape how lockup-release shocks are absorbed. In addition, viewed through the lens
of Fama’s (1970) semi-strong market e ciency, the results indicate only partial e -
ciency. Prices respond to known lockup dates, but the adjustment is slow and spread
out. Pre-event drift and post-event reversal imply that information asymmetry and
trading frictions still matter, but their impact is milder and more di↵use than in the
earlier U.S. environment.
   Although the market data we use are directly observable, the theoretical con-
structs they are intended to capture, moral hazard and information asymmetry, are
not. These mechanisms depend on insiders’ private information and incentives, and
their influence is observable only through their impact on market-based proxies.

Thus, we interpret a positive price drift before the unlock as an indication that in-
vestors view continued insider ownership as a commitment signal, reducing concerns
about moral hazard. In contrast, a price drop together with higher trading volume
at expiration may suggest that insider sales are interpreted as revealing information
the market did not previously have, reflecting the role of information asymmetry.
   The remainder of the paper proceeds as follows.   Section 2 reviews the theo-
retical background and related literature.   Section 3 describes the data, variable
construction, and empirical methodology.  Section 4 presents the event-study and
cross-sectional regression results. Section 5 interprets these findings in light of our
hypotheses and the broader theories of information asymmetry, liquidity, and market
e ciency. Section 6 concludes.

2.    Literature Review

A. Fundamental Concepts & Related Literature
   The literature starts from a simple tension with the E cient Market Hypothesis
(EMH). Lockup dates are disclosed in the prospectus and are therefore public in-
formation. Under the semi-strong form of market e ciency as formulated by Fama
(1970), a pre-announced event should not generate abnormal returns when it occurs.
Any rational price impact should be incorporated in advance.
   Field and Hanka (2001) show that this is not what happens in U.S. IPOs. They
find significant negative abnormal returns and sharp volume spikes at lockup ex-
piration.   Brav and Gompers (2003) confirm this pattern and report an average
abnormal return on the day of the IPO unlock of about -2% for VC-backed IPOs.
These papers treat lockup expirations as information and supply shocks: when in-
siders are allowed to sell, markets react as if new negative information arrives, even
though the date was known.  The presence of predictable event-window abnormal
returns is hard to reconcile with semi-strong e ciency and suggests underreaction,
limited attention, or trading frictions.
   Later studies deepen this basic result by conditioning on firm and ownership
structure.  Bradley, Jordan, Yi, and Roten (2001) and Brav and Gompers (2003)
show that venture-backed IPOs and firms with weaker governance experience larger
post-expiration price declines. Evidence from the UK in Espenlaub, Goergen, and
Khurshed (2001) points in the same direction, with more pronounced reactions
among firms facing greater uncertainty and information asymmetry. These findings
indicate that lockup e↵ects are amplified when informational and agency problems
are more severe. In EMH terms, the “anomaly” is not uniform but concentrated in
settings where public information is less informative about fundamentals.
   A second strand focuses on trading frictions and the mechanics of absorbing
new supply.  Ofek and Richardson (2003) show that short-selling increases before
unlocks but is constrained by borrowing costs and the limited supply of lendable
shares. Expected price declines cannot be fully arbitraged away. Field and Hanka
(2001) and related work document that, even though the float expands and long-
run liquidity improves, temporary price pressure persists as market makers absorb
concentrated sell orders. In this strand, abnormal returns around lockups are seen

                                    3
less as a pure violation of EMH and more as an outcome of limits to arbitrage and
slow-moving capital in the presence of frictions.
   More recent work adds an explicit contract and signaling view. Yung and Zen-
der (2010) model lockup length and structure as part of the optimal IPO contract
under asymmetric information and moral hazard. Lockups and their expirations are
natural points at which private information and changes in incentives can surface
in prices. Empirical papers in newer markets show that longer, stricter lockups are
associated with stronger signaling e↵ects and distinct post-IPO trading patterns,
especially when disclosure and enforcement are weaker. In these settings, abnormal
returns around unlocks are interpreted as rational responses to information that was
only imperfectly reflected in prices ex ante.
   Our study contributes to the literature in two main ways.
   First, we show that lockup expirations in modern major European markets do not
replicate the classic U.S. pattern. Instead of a large, sharply negative event-window
CAR, we find a strongly significant CAR in the pre-unlock window, only mild and
often insignificant abnormal returns around the unlock date, and a significant pos-
itive post-unlock reversal, combined with pronounced and persistent increases in
ATV.
   Second, we move beyond the limited existing European evidence, which mainly
focuses on single exchanges, by analysing IPOs from London, Frankfurt, Paris,
Stockholm, and Milan within a unified framework.  This multi-market setting al-
lows us to show that lockup reactions di↵er systematically across exchanges and
ownership structures, and to relate these di↵erences to variation in institutional
features, disclosure, and liquidity provision.

3.    Data, Methods, & Variables

A. The Sample
   Our sample of IPOs spans from January 2009 to December 2019 across five
major European equity markets:  London, Frankfurt, Paris, Stockholm, and Mi-
lano.  We begin by retrieving IPOs from the Security Data Corporation Platinum
(SDC) database with an IPO o↵er date within this period.   From SDC, we col-
lect fundamental IPO characteristics, including issuing market, IPO o↵ering date,
lockup expiration date, venture-capital or private-equity backing, and information
on locked-up shares and shares outstanding at the o↵er.  We retrieve daily stock
returns, trading volume, earnings announcement dates, and any potential seasoned
equity o↵ering dates over the sample period from the FactSet database in October
2025.
   Our initial dataset contains 744 IPOs recorded in SDC Platinum.  We require
IPOs to have an identifiable IPO o↵ering date within the sample period and a lockup
period with a specified unlock date, documented by SDC. With these restrictions,
our sample decreases to 431.   To ensure internal validity and comparability, we
exclude IPOs whose characteristics deviate materially from standard common-equity
o↵erings.   We also exclude IPOs whose unlock period overlaps with confounding

corporate events.
   Specifically, we exclude mutual-to-stock conversions and non-common-stock of-

ferings (ADRs, REITs, and unit o↵erings; 22 IPOs), penny stocks  and equity carve-
outs (53 IPOs), IPOs with earnings announcements within 3 calendar days of the
unlock date (19 IPOs), and IPOs with a seasoned equity o↵ering (SEOs) within 15
calendar days of the unlock date (23 IPOs).  To further ensure robustness, we re-
quire complete stock return and trading volume data for the 101-day event window
centered on the unlock date. In line with empirical practice in short-horizon event
studies, if an observation is missing on day t, we replace it with the most recent
available value (i.e., day t–1). This approach preserves observations with immate-
rial data gaps while avoiding unnecessary loss of sample firms.  Altogether, these
sample restrictions and data requirements reduce the initial set of 744 IPOs to a
final sample of 282 firms for the event-study and regression.
   To avoid excluding otherwise informative IPOs, we adopt two pragmatic as-
sumptions. For a subset of IPOs, we were unable to retrieve the original prospectus.
At issuance, regulators did not require long-term public archiving of prospectuses.
Many issuers and exchanges have since removed or reorganised online repositories.
To mitigate this, we conducted manual searches using sources such as FactSet and
London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) databases. This helped us recover relevant
share information.  When the number of locked shares was not directly disclosed,
we inferred it as shares outstanding at the o↵er minus primary shares, secondary
shares, and any exercised overallotment.  If lockup duration was not explicitly re-
ported, we calculated it as the number of days between the IPO o↵er date and the
lockup expiration date.
   For IPOs that disclose multiple lockup agreements with di↵erent expiration
dates, we select the lockup agreement that is most economically relevant for share
supply and insider selling incentives.  For most of our sample, this is the first un-
lock date.  It typically represents the initial and most significant expansion of the
tradable float.
   To address potential selection bias from restricting the analysis to IPOs with
complete return and volume data, we replicate our core tests on the broader sample
of 431 IPOs with identifiable lockups. The magnitude and direction of the results
remain materially unchanged. This confirms that the reduced sample of 282 IPOs
does not distort the main findings.

D. Research Design and Variables

D1. Cross-Sectional Multivariate Regressions
   We use cross-sectional multivariate OLS regressions to explain why abnormal
market reactions di↵er across IPOs at the time of lockup expiration.  Each IPO
contributes a single observation, and the dependent variable is measured over the
event window: either (i) the three-day cumulative abnormal return or (ii) log(1 +
three-day abnormal trading volume).   The design, therefore, exploits pure cross-
sectional variation in how strongly firms react to the same type of event, rather
than time-series dynamics.
   The regression structure is similar to Field and Hanka (2001) and is tailored to
key mechanisms in the lockup literature. The right-hand side groups firm and market
characteristics into three blocks: (i) ownership and lockup design, capturing insiders’
incentives and selling capacity; (ii) size, liquidity, and pre-unlock price behaviour,
capturing information environment and trading conditions; and (iii) market and
period indicators, capturing institutional di↵erences across exchanges and cycles.
We estimate ordinary least squares with heteroskedasticity-robust (HC1) standard
errors to allow for unequal error variances across firms.
   Combined with the event-study results, this framework allows us to move from
asking whether ”lockup expirations matter” to asking “for whom and under what
conditions” they matter the most.   The cross-sectional regressions show that dif-
ferences in ownership structure, information asymmetry, or market environment
reliably create stronger price pressure or larger volume surge.  This lets us test if
theoretical channels appear in the data.

D.2 Variables
    In the cross-sectional regressions, the dependent variable is either the three-day
cumulative abnormal return (CAR) or log(1 + three-day abnormal trading volume)
around the unlock date. These two measures summarize the price reaction and the
change in trading intensity at lockup expiration. The specification aims to explain
why some IPOs exhibit stronger returns or larger volume surges than others.
   To do so, we include several control variables at the firm, ownership, and market
levels.   The VCPE dummy identifies issuers backed by venture capital or private
equity sponsors. It captures the presence of concentrated, information-advantaged
shareholders with incentives to sell at lockup expiration. Market dummies for Lon-
don, Stockholm, Milan, Frankfurt, and Paris absorb persistent di↵erences in liquidity
provision, transparency regimes, and microstructure across exchanges.  Year dum-
mies (2012–2014, 2015–2017, 2018–2019) control for broader market and regulatory
conditions during the MiFID era, relative to the year 2009-2011.   Each dummy
variable equals 1 if the IPO belongs to the relevant category, and 0 otherwise.
   To account for scale e↵ects and skewed distributions, three regressors are entered
in logarithmic form: a Size measure that scales firm value, RunUp, which captures
pre-event valuation momentum and embedded expectations, and logAV, which prox-
ies for the intensity of order-flow pressure around the event. Unlock-specific supply
e↵ects are captured by FractionLocked, measuring the relative shock to the tradable
float at expiration. The inclusion of these variables and the structure of the regres-
sion are informed directly by the lockup-expiration literature, in particular by Field

                                    7
and Hanka (2001) and Brav and Gompers (2003), who demonstrate the relevance
of ownership structure and lockup design for explaining market reactions to unlock
events.   Additionally, drawing on the findings of Amihud (2002) and Spiegel and
Wang (2005), who establish that liquidity plays a crucial role in the cross-section
of stock returns, we include Pre-Turnover to represent baseline liquidity conditions.
This variable is designed to capture how trading capacity and ownership dispersion
influence the e↵ect of the unlock shock on abnormal returns and volume.  Formal
variable definition, construction and regression equations details are provided in
Appendix A.

F. Robustness Checks
    The robustness of our results is of severe value. Therefore, in this subsection, we
evaluate the reliability of our event study and our cross-sectional regressions.  We
also consider event-day robustness to ensure that abnormal returns are not driven by
announcement-day noise or timing conventions, as well as by potential distortions
arising from tax-motivated capital gains behavior. Notably, the diagnostics confirm
that the regression design is well-conditioned and that inference is robust to plausible
violations of OLS assumptions.

F.1 Is this a 180-day e↵ect?
   To verify that the documented abnormal return and volume patterns are driven
by the expiration of IPO lockups rather than by an unrelated event that happens
to occur 180 days after listing, we conduct a series of validity checks. There is no
observable regulatory-, tax-, reporting-, or trading-rule event in European markets
that systematically occurs 180 days after an IPO. Thus, there is no theoretical reason
to expect a general day-180 reaction unrelated to lockup expirations. If a “day-180
e↵ect” were driving the results, only firms with an exact 180-day lockup would
exhibit abnormal behavior around this point.  Table 3A further support that this
is not the case, showing significant pre- and post-unlock returns across all lockup
lengths. Lockups  >  180 days indicates a pre-unlock return of –5.76% (t = –3.87) for
lockups  >  180 days, and similarly significant post-unlock reversals for both  >  180
and  <  180 days. Moreover, as the sample is explicitly cleaned for all SEOs (follow-
on o↵erings) occurring within 15 trading days of the unlock date (transactions that
are well-documented to generate substantial abnormal returns and trading volume),
we eliminate the most credible alternative source of concentrated market reactions
around this period, and any remaining price or volume e↵ects must stem from the
lockup expiration itself.

F.2 Winsorization
   We winsorize to handle and dampen the influence of extreme outliers, likely
idiosyncratic observations (thin-trading days, data glitches, split/price spikes, un-
usually large volume bursts) that can dominate event-study averages and OLS es-
timates in our relatively small sample.   Specifically, for CAR, we trim the data
at the 5th and 95th percentiles, capping values below the 5th and above the 95th
percentile, to adjust the most extreme price moves while maintaining the integrity
of the remaining cross-section.  This approach stabilises event-window means and
regression residuals and is standard in event studies. For ATV, where volume ratios

                                    8
are far more right-skewed and susceptible to distortion when normal-period volume
is low, we winsorize at the 1st and 99th percentiles, excluding capping below the
1st and above the 99th percentile, to control for a small number of large-volume
spikes while preserving most of the economically relevant variation. We apply the
5/95 rule consistently to CAR and the 1/99 rule consistently to ATV in both the
event study and the cross-sectional regressions to ensure coherent treatment of each
variable and e↵ective control for outliers. Notably, our main results remain robust
to these specific winsorization thresholds.

F3. Multicollinearity, Heteroskedasticity, and Standard Error Structure
    To ensure robust cross-sectional regressions, we account for the following factors.
   We first assess multicollinearity using Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) for all
continuous regressors and dummy variables. VIFs measure how much the sampling
variance of a coe cient is inflated by linear dependence on other covariates.  Fol-
lowing Greene’s (2018) econometric guidance, VIF values above 10 are typically
considered indicative of problematic multicollinearity.   In our specifications, VIFs
range from 1.2 to 5.59, and the highest values occur for FractionLocked, reflecting
the mechanical link to tradable shares, but remain well below conventional concern
levels. One market dummy is omitted to avoid the dummy-variable trap. Full VIF
tables for all CAR and ATV regressions (Tables 4) are reported in Appendix B.
Overall, the diagnostics indicate that multicollinearity is moderate and does not
materially distort coe cient estimates.
   Cross-sectional regressions in abnormal returns and trading volume are likely
heteroskedastic because firms di↵er in size, float, liquidity, and volatility.  All re-
ported regressions use HC1 heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors, introduced
by White in 1980 and subsequently refined by MacKinnon and White in 1985, in
line with standard practice in asset-pricing and event-study research established by
Fama and MacBeth (1973).
   We also explore market-level clustering as a robustness check.   Clustering by
exchange allows arbitrary correlation of residuals across London, Frankfurt, Paris,
Stockholm, and Milan, and is more meaningful than clustering by year, whereas
clustering by firm is infeasible with one observation per IPO. However, the number
of clusters is very small (five), and prior work by Cameron and Miller (2015) shows
that cluster-robust standard errors with fewer than about 10 clusters are unstable
and often downward-biased.   HC1 robust standard errors are used for the main
inference. The clustered estimates yield identical coe cient signs and magnitudes,
reinforcing that our conclusions are not driven by the specific variance estimator.

4.    Empirical Analysis

A. Abnormal Market Reactions Around the unlock date
   In this section, we present a description of our empirical evidence on how IPO
lockup expirations a↵ect prices and trading activity in European equity markets in
three stages. First, we report event-study results for cumulative abnormal returns

                                   10
(CAR) and abnormal trading volume (ATV) around the unlock date. Second, we
examine how these patterns vary across subsamples defined by ownership structure,
lockup length, prior price performance, and listing market.   Third, we run cross-
sectional regressions to test whether firm, ownership, and market characteristics
help explain the heterogeneity in unlock-window CAR and ATV. These regressions
move beyond average e↵ects and show which factors are most strongly associated
with sharper price reactions or larger volume surges at expiration.

B. Cross-Sectional Multivariate Regression
   We proceed with cross-sectional regressions, following standard practice in Field
and Hanka (2001), to examine how firm, ownership, and market characteristics
explain variation in market reactions to lockup expirations. Specifically, we estimate
separate OLS models for our two dependent variables.  The three-day cumulative
abnormal return (CAR) and the three-day abnormal trading volume (ATV). For
each outcome, we run three specifications:  one for the full IPO sample, one for
VC/PE-backed firms, and one for non-VC/PE-backed firms.
    In line with standard event-study practice, the goal is to isolate systematic pat-
terns in returns and identify systematic drivers of trading intensity, not to predict
inherently noisy short-horizon outcomes.

significantly explain return variation. The strongest predictor is abnormal volume,

pre-turnover coe cient reflects the tiny scale of the underlying turnover measure,
and its insignificance indicates that baseline liquidity ahead of expiration does not
systematically shape the unlock- window price response for the full sample.
   Among VC/PE-backed firms, several determinants become economically and sta-
tistically more meaningful.  Market location plays a notable role.  Frankfurt-listed
IPOs exhibit significantly higher CARs of 2.65 (t = 2.28), while Milan shows a
strong and highly significant negative coe cient of –5.59% (t = –6.04), underscor-

is strongly positive at +3.84% (t = 3.84), and 2012–2014 is also significant at 3.05

VC/PE-backed issuers. Pre-unlock turnover is significantly negative at –3022.96 (t

5.    Interpretation of Empirical Evidence

In this section, we evaluate a set of hypotheses motivated by our empirical findings
on return and volume patterns around lockup expirations.   The analysis focuses
on explaining the observed dynamics, such as the pre-unlock drift, the reaction
at expiration, and the post-event adjustment through mechanisms proposed in the
theoretical and empirical literature.   For each hypothesis, we relate our evidence
to prior studies, highlighting where our results align with established findings and
where they diverge.
   We consider, first, whether the patterns we observe are consistent with anticipa-
tory repricing under asymmetric information, where investors progressively discount
the stock as insider selling becomes imminent. We then examine ownership structure
e↵ects, assessing whether VC/PE-backed firms whose insiders are often better in-
formed and more inclined to sell exhibit stronger market reactions than non-backed
IPOs.  We also discuss hypotheses related to liquidity and trading shocks, where
the unlock serves as a liquidity event that attracts arbitrage activity and order-flow
imbalances.  Further, we evaluate whether the short-term price movements reflect
temporary price pressure or a more persistent shift in demand. Finally, we consider
post-expiration reversals and whether return behavior after the unlock supports in-
terpretations based on temporary selling pressure or suggests broader underreaction.

A. Anticipatory Market Repricing Ahead of Lockup Expiration
    Here, we aim to evaluate our first hypothesis, which states that investors reprice
IPO firms before the lockup expires. This implies that prices should drift down in

                                   19
the pre-unlock window, while the narrow unlock window itself should show little
additional reaction. Primarily, this links information asymmetry with semi-strong
market e ciency, i.e., news about insider selling is anticipated and incorporated
gradually, not only on the unlock day.
   Theoretical work on asymmetric information gives a clear mechanism.  In sig-
naling models, Leland and Pyle (1977) and Myers and Majluf (1984) argue that
insiders who plan to sell soon reveal weaker private information about future cash
flows. Outside investors then discount the stock as the unlock approaches. Moral-
hazard models, as described by Holmstr¨om and Tirole (1993), add that insiders
may reduce e↵ort as their lockup constraint weakens. Hence, both channels imply a
gradual downward drift ahead of expiration. Therefore, in an e cient market, Fama
(1970) argues this drift should start well before the lockup date and leave only a
small residual move when the restriction is actually lifted.
   The IPO lockup literature documents similar patterns. Field and Hanka (2001)
find a negative three-day CAR around U.S. lockup expirations, but also report price
pressure building up in the month before.   Patatoukas and co-authors show that
U.S. prices often begin to decline about 10 trading days before expiry, in line with
increased short selling by informed traders.   Our European evidence, however, is
more mixed.
    Our pre-unlock results are consistent with the anticipatory repricing story.  Il-
lustrated in Figure II, and quantified in Table 2, we observe over days –50 to –6 a
clear and statistically significant negative CAR for the full sample, –2.79% (t=3.38).
Table 3A makes it evident that the decline is stronger for IPOs backed by VC/PE in-
vestors and for firms that already underperformed before the window. It is also more
pronounced for long lockups and for some large, liquid exchanges such as Frankfurt
and Paris. These patterns fit the idea that anticipatory discounting is most intense
when ex ante information asymmetry is high and when sophisticated investors have
the tools and incentives to trade on it.
   The behavior by ownership type is particularly informative. VC/PE-backed firms
show a steeper pre-unlock decline than other IPOs. This mirrors U.S. evidence, as
shown by Jordan et al. (2000) and Brav and Gompers (2003), that negative abnor-
mal returns around lockup events are concentrated in VC-backed issues.  Venture
investors are better informed about fundamentals and about their own exit plans.
Public investors, therefore, have stronger reasons to anticipate insider selling and
to demand a higher discount as the unlock date approaches.  Hence, our findings
suggest that this mechanism operates, to some extent, in European markets as well.
   Lockup length tells a similar story. IPOs with lockups longer than 180 days ex-
perience much larger pre-expiration declines than those with shorter commitments.
This, however, is anticipated and linked to theory.   Longer lockups are often ne-
gotiated when information asymmetry at the IPO is particularly severe or when
underwriters want a stronger commitment signal, as argued by Brav and Gompers
(2003) and Yung and Zender (2010). As expiration nears, the protection o↵ered by
these contracts erodes, and investors rationally expect insiders (who were locked pre-
cisely because of higher uncertainty) to sell once they are allowed to. The stronger
pre-unlock drift in long-lockup firms is therefore consistent with information-based
pricing rather than market overreaction.

                                   20
   The univariate test (Table 3A), which lists the market, also supports our first
hypothesis.   The most pronounced negative drift occurs on exchanges with deep
liquidity and high institutional participation. At these venues, informed investors,
hedge funds, and market makers can short-sell or gradually unwind positions as the
unlock date approaches. This matches the mechanism described by Brunnermeier
and Pedersen (2005), where funding and market liquidity interact to amplify the
e↵ect of predictable order imbalances.  In contrast, smaller or less liquid markets
exhibit weaker, less systematic pre-unlock patterns, consistent with greater frictions
in executing anticipatory trades.
   A crucial implication of this hypothesis is that the narrow unlock window should
not contain much new information. Our evidence is consistent with this. The three-
day CAR around the expiration date is close to zero and statistically insignificant
in the full sample and in all subsamples. This stands in contrast to the U.S. find-
ings by Field and Hanka (2001) and Brav and Gompers (2003), who report average
event-window drops of around –1.5% to –2%. One interpretation is that European
markets in our sample period process lockup information more gradually and possi-
bly more e ciently. Better disclosure rules, tighter short-selling constraints at the
time of earlier U.S. studies, and the growth of derivatives and lending markets in
Europe may all contribute to earlier incorporation of insider-selling expectations.
There is also additional contrasting evidence worth noting.   Brav and Gompers
(2003) interpret the negative event-window CAR in the U.S. as inconsistent with
fully rational expectations, arguing that investors are surprised by insider sales de-
spite public information about lockup terms.   Our results point in the opposite
direction.   Most of the adjustment happens before the contractual date, and the
event day itself is largely a non-event for prices.   This suggests that, at least in
our European setting, lockup expirations behave less like anomalies and more like
scheduled ownership changes that markets anticipate well in advance. Overall, our
first hypothesis is supported. We see a clear and economically meaningful negative
drift in the pre-unlock window, strongest precisely where information asymmetry
and insider incentives are likely to be most severe. At the same time, the unlock-
window CAR is close to zero, consistent with semi-strong e ciency. The evidence,
therefore, points to a systematic, information-driven repricing of IPOs well ahead of
the lockup date, rather than a sudden adjustment when the contractual restriction
actually expires.

B. Ownership Structure Shapes Market Reactions
   Next, our second hypothesis examines whether VC/PE-backed IPOs exhibit
stronger price and volume reactions around the lockup date. The idea is that con-
centrated, sophisticated blockholders create clearer selling incentives and sharper
information events than dispersed founders or small insiders.
   Theoretical and empirical work supports this view. Venture capital and private
equity investors are typically better informed, hold large stakes, and operate with
defined exit horizons, as shown by Gompers (1996) and Metrick and Yasuda (2010).
Their presence both certifies the issue at the IPO and creates a predictable “over-
hang” once lockups end.  Brav and Gompers (2003) and Bradley, Jordan, and Yi
(2003) show that U.S. lockup e↵ects are concentrated in VC-backed IPOs, where in-

                                   21
sider sales signal information and free up large blocks of stock. Investors, therefore,
monitor VC/PE lockups more closely and may start adjusting portfolios earlier and
more aggressively.  As demonstrated in section A, our pre-unlock results are con-
sistent with this mechanism. VC/PE-backed firms display a clearly more negative
return drift in the –50 to –6 window than non-backed IPOs. The exact magnitudes
are reported in Table 3A, but the pattern is robust.  Ownership by sophisticated
financial sponsors is associated with stronger anticipatory repricing. This is in line
with the notion that markets expect these investors to sell once allowed and that
their potential exit is more informative about firm quality than sales by less-informed
insiders.
   Ownership also shapes how the pre-unlock drift varies across other characteris-
tics.
   For VC/PE-backed IPOs, the decline is especially pronounced among issues that
already performed poorly before the window and among firms with longer lockups.
This fits information-asymmetry models in which adverse signals are amplified when
prior performance is weak or initial uncertainty is high. It also resonates with the
findings of Brav and Gompers (2003), who show that VC-backed firms with more
uncertain fundamentals exhibit larger post-IPO price adjustments.
   The event window returns, however, tell a more nuanced story. For both VC/PE-
backed and non-backed firms, CAR in the –1 to +1 window is small and statistically
insignificant. We do not observe the sharp negative unlock-day drops reported for
U.S. VC-backed IPOs in the 1990s, as documented by Field and Hanka (2001) and
Brav and Gompers (2003).   Instead, most of the adjustment for VC/PE-backed
firms appears to occur earlier, consistent with our first hypothesis, the anticipatory
repricing story, and with a more gradual, European style of price discovery. In this
sense, ownership structure a↵ects when prices react more than it a↵ects the raw
magnitude of the event-window CAR. The post-unlock window provides further
evidence on ownership.
   The post-unlock window provides further evidence on ownership. Table 3A shows
that non-VC/PE-backed IPOs experience a stronger positive reversal in the +11 to
+50 CAR than VC/PE-backed firms. This suggests that temporary price pressure
and subsequent correction are more pronounced when owners are less sophisticated
and perhaps slower to trade ahead of the event. VC/PE-backed stocks have already
moved down before expiration, leaving less room for a sharp bounce afterwards.
This pattern contrasts with some U.S. studies that find large, one-o↵  drops and
partial reversals precisely in VC issues. Our results indicate that European markets
with active institutional investors may spread this adjustment over a longer horizon.
   Ownership also matters for trading activity, though the evidence is mixed. Figure
3 shows that VC/PE-backed IPOs often exhibit very sharp volume spikes around the
unlock date and higher day-to-day volatility in abnormal volume. This is consistent
with large block trades, coordinated selling programs, and active participation by
hedge funds and arbitrageurs. However, the average abnormal volume in the narrow
–1 to +1 window is not significantly higher for VC/PE-backed firms than for other
IPOs, and in some specifications, the non-VC/PE group shows a slightly larger
mean ATV. Thus, our second hypothesis is only partly supported on the volume
side.   Ownership clearly shapes how and when trading responds, but it does not

                                   22
produce a uniformly stronger average volume response in the unlock window.
   Table 4 further deepens this picture. In the CAR regressions, the VC/PE-backed
subsample shows richer structure, exchange dummies, time dummies, and pre-unlock
turnover all matter. Returns for VC/PE-backed IPOs are higher on some exchanges
and lower on others, and they depend on how actively the stock was traded before
expiration. This indicates that VC/PE ownership interacts with market microstruc-
ture and liquidity, rather than acting as a stand-alone driver.   By contrast, the
non-VC/PE subsample is mainly explained by contemporaneous abnormal volume
and recent price run-up, with little role for listing venue or turnover.  In the vol-
ume regressions, Table 4, VC/PE status is associated with di↵erent sensitivities to
the fraction of locked shares and to the listing market, again pointing to a distinct
trading environment around these issues.
   Taken together, these pieces of evidence suggest that our second hypothesis is
broadly, but not fully, supported. Ownership by VC/PE investors clearly intensifies
pre-unlock repricing and creates a richer cross-sectional pattern in returns and trad-
ing around expiration. At the same time, we do not find uniformly stronger negative
CAR in the narrow event window, nor consistently higher average abnormal volume,
as some U.S. studies report. For European IPOs in our sample period, VC/PE back-
ing appears to shift the timing and structure of market reactions towards earlier and
more gradual adjustment rather than simply amplifying the short-run lockup e↵ect.

C. Unlock Events Trigger Liquidity and Trading Shocks
   Now, we will examine our third hypothesis, which states that lockup expirations
cause liquidity shocks.  When previously locked shares become tradable, investors
rebalance, and liquidity providers adjust. We should therefore see a sharp increase
in abnormal trading volume around the unlock date.
   The lockup literature documents exactly this pattern. Field and Hanka (2001)
find that U.S. IPOs experience a large volume spike in the week of expiration and
that average trading volume remains about 40% higher afterwards. Bradley et al.
(2001) and Brav and Gompers (2003) report similar jumps for large U.S. samples.
These studies interpret the spike as a direct consequence of insiders selling for the
first time and the market absorbing a larger free float. The broader microstructure
literature also links large order imbalances to temporary volume and price dislo-
cations, as shown by Chan and Lakonishok (1993) and Campbell, Grossman and
Wang (1993).
   Our time-series evidence strongly supports our third hypothesis. Figure 3 shows
that abnormal trading volume is close to zero and often slightly negative in the
distant pre-unlock period.  Roughly ten days before expiration, volume begins to
trend upward across all subsamples. This anticipatory rise suggests that informed
investors and liquidity providers start to reposition before the event, consistent with
our first hypothesis.   At the unlock date itself, average abnormal volume jumps
sharply for the full sample and remains elevated for several weeks. The exact mag-
nitudes by day and group are reported in Table 3B and are large in economic terms,
with mean abnormal volume in the –1 to +1 window clearly above zero for most
subsamples. The pattern is noisy at the daily level, but the shape is clear. Quiet
pre-period, sharp event spike, and a prolonged high-volume phase afterwards.

                                   23
   The cross-section of volume responses reveals more detail and makes it evident
that markets di↵er systematically.  Some exchanges, such as Frankfurt, show very
pronounced abnormal volume at unlock, while others exhibit more muted reactions.
This aligns with evidence from Jain (2005) and Chiyachantana et al.  (2004) that
trading institutions and market design a↵ect how order imbalances translate into
volume and price movements. Our volume regressions confirm that exchange dum-
mies explain a substantial share of the cross-sectional variation in log abnormal
volume.  Listing venues are therefore a key component of the liquidity shock, not
just the existence of a lockup.
   Ownership and share supply also matter, but in more nuanced ways. For VC/PE-
backed IPOs, the fraction of shares subject to lockup is positively associated with
abnormal volume during the unlock window. This suggests that when a larger block
of informed insider shares becomes tradable, more trading takes place as these in-
vestors rebalance and outside investors adjust their positions.  This result is con-
sistent with Field and Hanka’s (2001) interpretation of volume spikes as driven by
pre-IPO shareholders selling down large blocks. For non-VC/PE-backed firms, how-
ever, the relation is weaker and in some specifications even negative. Here, volume
shocks seem to be driven more by exchange characteristics and short-term trading
than by the mechanical release of large locked blocks.
   The interaction between volume and returns also supports the liquidity-shock
view.   In the CAR regressions, log abnormal volume is one of the strongest pre-
dictors of unlock-window returns. Stocks that trade more heavily in the –1 to +1
window also display larger price reactions. In the reverse direction, when we regress
log abnormal volume on three-day CAR (window -1 to +1), the price reaction en-
ters with a positive coe cient in the full sample, though the e↵ect is weaker in the
subsamples. This two-way relationship is consistent with models in which liquidity
provision responds to order flow, and prices adjust more when trading is intense, as
described by Campbell, Grossman and Wang (1993).  Volume and returns, there-
fore, appear jointly determined around unlock events, rather than one being purely
exogenous.
   Compared to U.S. evidence, our volume shocks are similar in shape but some-
what di↵erent in persistence.  Field and Hanka (2001) report a spike, followed by
a higher, but stable, new level of trading volume.  In our European sample, post-
unlock abnormal volume remains high but also quite volatile. This suggests a more
extended adjustment process, with trading intensity fading only gradually. One pos-
sible explanation is that European ownership structures are often more fragmented,
and that institutional investors unwind or accumulate positions over a longer horizon
rather than in a single burst of trading.
   However, it is important to acknowledge that there is also some contrasting evi-
dence in the European lockup literature. A few studies on individual markets report
weaker or insignificant volume responses, especially for smaller exchanges or earlier
sample periods. Our results indicate that once we pool several major European mar-
kets and focus on more recent IPO cohorts, the liquidity shock becomes clearer. This
underscores the importance of liquidity provision and institutional trading capacity
in transforming a contractual unlock into a measurable market event.
    Given this, the evidence for our third hypothesis is strong. We observe a clear and

                                   24
economically meaningful surge in abnormal trading volume around lockup expira-
tions. The increase starts shortly before the event, peaks in the immediate window,
and remains elevated afterwards.   Its size and persistence vary across ownership
types and exchanges, but the qualitative pattern is robust. These findings support
the view that lockup expirations act as liquidity and trading shocks to the market,
driven by the release of previously constrained insider shares and by investors’ and
liquidity providers’ responses to this predictable change in supply.

D. Temporary Price Pressure and the Downward-Sloping Demand Curve
   Subsequently, our fourth hypothesis evaluates whether this reflects a downward-
sloping demand curve for the stock or simply temporary price pressure. The theory
is simple.  When the lockup expires, a large block of insider shares becomes trad-
able.   Short-run supply increases.   If investor demand is not perfectly elastic, the
price must fall to convince marginal buyers to absorb the new supply, as shown by
Shleifer (1986) and Kaul, Mehrotra and Morck (2000). In that case, we expect a neg-
ative event-window CAR, and the decline should be stronger when more shares are
unlocked or when trading volume is larger. The event-study literature distinguishes
two related hypotheses:  (i) the price-pressure hypothesis, as described by Harris
and Gurel (1986), stating that order-flow imbalances cause a temporary price move
that later reverses, (ii) the downward-sloping demand curve hypothesis, stating that
the price impact is permanent because demand curves for individual stocks slope
down, as argued by Shleifer (1986). Harris and Gurel’s S&P 500 study is the classic
reference:  they find a jump in prices at index inclusion and a later reversal, and
interpret this as pure price pressure. Shleifer and, later, Kaul et al., show cases in
which price changes after index events do not reverse, and interpret this as evidence
of downward-sloping demand.
   Lockup studies have applied the same logic. Field and Hanka (2001) and Ofek
and Richardson (2003) document negative abnormal returns around U.S. lockup
expirations, especially when a large fraction of shares becomes tradable.  Because
these price drops are not fully reversed, they interpret part of the e↵ect as a demand-
curve phenomenon: more supply leads to a lower equilibrium price.
   Our data show a more moderate pattern.  The CAR path around the unlock
date has three steps. First, we see a clear negative drift in the pre-unlock window,
which we attribute to anticipatory repricing under our first hypothesis.   Second,
there is a small additional decline in the days immediately after the unlock. Third,
in Table 2 and Table 3A the +11 to +50 window, CAR turns positive and shows
a statistically significant reversal.   This shape is exactly what the price-pressure
hypothesis predicts, i.e. a short-run dip when selling pressure materialises, followed
by recovery once order flow normalises, as described by Harris and Gurel (1986).
   However, the e↵ect is mild. The three-day CAR in the –1 to +1 window is close to
zero and statistically insignificant for the full sample and for all subsamples. We do
not see the large, sharp drops of –1.5% to –2% reported for U.S. IPOs in the 1990s,
as documented by Field and Hanka (2001) and Brav and Gompers (2003).  This
suggests that, while some short-lived price pressure exists, the immediate unlock
e↵ect is weaker in our European setting.
   The cross-sectional evidence is even less supportive of a strong demand-curve

                                   25
channel.   If a downward-sloping demand curve were the main driver, we would
expect (i) more negative event-window CAR when abnormal volume is high, and
(ii) more negative event-window CAR when the fraction of locked shares is larger.
   We find the opposite. In the CAR regressions, log abnormal volume during the
unlock window is positively associated with CAR. Stocks that trade more heavily
around the event tend to have slightly higher, not lower, returns.   The fraction
locked is generally insignificant and does not explain variation in event-window
CAR. Graphical checks that mimic Field and Hanka’s volume–return plots show
a relationship that is essentially flat.   High-volume unlocks do not cluster in the
lower tail of the CAR distribution.
   The most plausible interpretation is therefore as follows.   There is mild, tem-
porary price pressure when insider selling and rebalancing flows actually hit the
market. This explains the small post-unlock dip we see in the graph. But the ef-
fect is quickly reversed and is not systematically stronger when the supply shock
is larger.  Using the framework of Harris and Gurel (1986), our data support the
price-pressure hypothesis but not the downward-sloping demand curve hypothesis.
   Modern European market structure helps explain this outcome. Major exchanges
now feature narrow spreads, deep order books, and fast, automated liquidity provi-
sion. Fragmented trading venues and MiFID-driven best-execution rules allow large
orders to be split and internalised with small price concessions. In such an environ-
ment, short-run demand for individual stocks is close to elastic at the event horizon.
Liquidity providers can absorb lockup-related selling without large or permanent
price changes.
   Therefore, our fourth hypothesis receives partial support. We observe a tempo-
rary post-unlock dip followed by a reversal, consistent with short-lived price pressure.
But we find no evidence that this e↵ect scales with the size of the supply shock or
that it leaves a permanent imprint on prices. Our results, therefore, do not support
a strong downward-sloping demand curve at lockup expiration and instead point to
small, transient liquidity e↵ects in otherwise highly liquid European markets.

E. Stock Return Reversal Following Lockup Expiration
   Lastly, our fifth hypothesis states that returns should rebound after the lockup
expiration.   As temporary selling pressure and order imbalances fade, prices are
expected to partially reverse, generating positive post-unlock CAR. This idea is
closely related to short-horizon return reversals in the asset pricing literature, as
described by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993).
   The mechanism is straightforward. Around the unlock date, insider selling and
investor rebalancing can temporarily push prices below fundamental value, especially
if liquidity is finite or risk-averse intermediaries demand compensation to absorb the
flow. Once this pressure disappears and the new ownership structure stabilizes, the
need for a discount vanishes.  Prices then move back up toward a more “normal”
level. In this view, negative pre- or near-event returns and positive post-event CAR
are two sides of the same adjustment process.
   Short-term reversals are a well-documented phenomenon.  Jegadeesh and Tit-
man (1993) show that U.S. stocks that underperform over short horizons tend to
outperform over the subsequent weeks, consistent with temporary price pressure, liq-

                                   26
uidity e↵ects, or investor overreaction that later corrects. Similarly, Kaul, Mehrotra,
and Morck (2000) find similar behavior around index events: supply shocks cause
short-lived dislocations followed by partial reversals.
   Our post-unlock results are consistent with our fifth hypothesis.   In the +11
to +50 window, we observe positive and statistically significant CAR for the full
sample. The exact values are reported in Table 3C, but the key point is that returns
change sign relative to the pre-unlock period.  Stocks that had declined ahead of
expiration show a clear rebound once the event is behind them. This pattern fits a
reversal story: some of the earlier discount is unwound as the market absorbs insider
sales, and uncertainty about the unlock outcome is resolved.
   The behavior across subsamples provides more structure.  Non-VC/PE-backed
IPOs display a particularly strong post-unlock rebound.   These firms often show
meaningful negative drift before the event but then outperform in the +11 to +50
window. This may reflect slower anticipatory trading by their insiders and investors.
If less sophisticated owners do not fully adjust before expiration, more of the sell-
ing pressure occurs around the event and is then undone afterward. The stronger
reversal for non-VC/PE-backed firms is consistent with this slower, more frictional
adjustment process.
   VC/PE-backed IPOs show a more muted reversal.  Their pre-unlock decline is
steeper, but post-unlock CAR is smaller and less precisely estimated, consistent with
our first- and second hypotheses. By the time the lockup actually expires, much of
the information and exit risk is already priced in.  As a result, there is less scope
for a sharp bounce after the event. In other words, where anticipatory repricing is
stronger, post-event reversal is weaker.
    The cross-sectional regressions are also in line with a reversal mechanism. Stocks
that experienced higher trading intensity and stronger short-run reactions around
the unlock date do not su↵er persistent losses. Instead, the main determinants of
unlock-window CAR are liquidity and prior performance, not firm fundamentals or
lockup features. This suggests that the pre- and near-event price moves are driven
more by temporary factors in trading and expectations than by permanent changes
in the firm’s value. The positive CAR in the post-unlock window is then a natural
correction once these temporary forces fade.
   Compared to earlier studies, our reversal is somewhat stronger relative to the
size of the event-window e↵ect.  According to Field and Hanka (2001), there is a
sizeable negative CAR around U.S. unlock dates and only partial reversal afterward.
In our European sample, the event-window CAR is close to zero, but the pre-unlock
drift is clearly negative, and the post-unlock CAR is clearly positive.
   This has an important implication.   The reversal we observe does not simply
mirror a classic anomaly in which markets “overreact” to the unlock and then correct
their mistake. Instead, it reflects the joint outcome of anticipatory repricing, mild
temporary price pressure, and the gradual resolution of uncertainty about insider
behavior. European investors appear to discount lockup risk early but still require
some compensation for absorbing selling pressure.   Once this process is complete
and ownership is rebalanced, prices rise again.

                                   27
6.    Conclusion

This study examines how European equity markets respond to IPO lockup expira-
tions, focusing on price dynamics, trading activity, information asymmetry, own-
ership structure, liquidity, and market microstructure. Using 282 IPOs across five
major exchanges between 2009 and 2019, we combine an event-study approach with
cross-sectional regressions to uncover the mechanisms behind market reactions to
these predictable changes in insider selling constraints.
   Our results reveal a clear pattern of anticipatory repricing, with prices drift-
ing downward well before expiration, most strongly for VC/PE-backed firms and
for IPOs with longer lockups, consistent with asymmetric information and insider-
incentive explanations.   The event-window CAR, by contrast, is near zero for all
subsamples, indicating that modern European markets incorporate lockup infor-
mation gradually rather than through abrupt unlock-day drops, as documented in
earlier U.S. studies.
   Trading activity exhibits a di↵erent but complementary dynamic.   Abnormal
trading volume begins to rise roughly ten days before expiration, surges in the
narrow event window, and remains elevated thereafter. This pattern aligns closely
with liquidity-shock interpretations, as constrained insider shares become tradable,
markets must absorb new supply and rebalance ownership. Yet, unlike earlier U.S.
evidence, these volume shocks do not translate into large negative event-day returns,
highlighting the depth and e ciency of contemporary European equity markets.
   Price adjustments around expiration are modest and temporary. A small decline
immediately after the unlock is followed by a statistically significant reversal in the
+11 to +50 day window.  This reversal is strongest for non-VC/PE-backed IPOs,
consistent with slower anticipatory trading and greater sensitivity to temporary
order-flow imbalances.  Together with the absence of strong relationships between
event-window CAR and the fraction of locked shares, the evidence supports a transi-
tory price-pressure mechanism rather than a downward-sloping demand curve with
permanent e↵ects.   Ownership structure further shapes these patterns.   VC/PE-
backed IPOs exhibit stronger anticipatory drift, more complex volume and return
dynamics, and greater sensitivity to market structure, yet they do not show the
sharp event-window drops found in earlier U.S. data. Their adjustment process is
earlier and more di↵use, consistent with the presence of sophisticated investors and
well-developed trading environments.
    Finally, our multi-market setting shows that exchange-level characteristics influ-
ence both price and volume reactions. Equity markets explain a substantial share of
abnormal trading volume and part of the cross-sectional return variation, underscor-
ing the importance of liquidity provision, transparency regimes, and market depth
in absorbing lockup-related supply shocks.
   Taken together, the evidence indicates that European lockup expirations no
longer generate abrupt anomalies.   Instead, they produce anticipatory repricing,
predictable liquidity shocks, and temporary price pressure followed by a reversal, all
of which are mediated by ownership structure and market microstructure.  These
patterns suggest that semi-strong e ciency is more closely observed in this setting:
markets incorporate publicly available lockup information gradually and systemati-

                                   28
cally, and any short-run disturbances are limited and temporary.

                                   29
7.    Limitations

We follow Field and Hanka (2001) and compute market-adjusted CARs using gross
returns. This approach is simple, transparent, and avoids the need for an estima-
tion window, which is important in IPO settings where firms have limited trading
histories. However, we acknowledge that this method is not without limitations. By
using the market index as the sole benchmark for expected returns, we implicitly as-
sume that firm-specific risk di↵erences and industry-level shocks are negligible over
the event window. Importantly, we do not adjust returns for beta or any risk-factor
exposure, such as those implied by the CAPM or Fama–French models. These as-
sumptions may not hold perfectly, particularly for young and volatile IPOs. Given
the very short three-day window examined here, we expect any resulting bias to be
small, but we recognise that the measure provides only an approximation of true
abnormal performance.

Appendix A: Definition of Variables

   Dependent Variables

    Cumulative Abnormal Return (CAR): The three-day cumulative abnormal
return for firm i over the event window (t = –1, 0, +1) relative to the lockup-
expiration date.   CAR is calculated as the compounded ratio of the firm’s gross
return to the corresponding home-market index return (FTSE, DAX, CAC, OMXS,
or FTSE MIB), minus one. The measure isolates the market-adjusted price reaction
attributable specifically to the unlock event, following MacKinlay (1997) and Field
and Hanka (2001). CAR is winsorised at the 5th and 95th percentiles and expressed
in decimal form.

   Abnormal Trading Volume (ATV):  The natural logarithm of one plus the
average abnormal trading volume over the same three-day window (t = –1 to +1).
Abnormal volume is defined as the ratio of event-period trading volume to the firm’s
average daily trading volume in the estimation window (t = –50 to –6), minus one.
This transformation reduces skewness and allows percentage-based interpretation.
Abnormal volume is winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles before logging.

   Independent Variables

   VCPE Dummy:  Indicator variable equal to 1 if the IPO issuer is backed by
venture capital or private equity investors at the time of listing, and 0 otherwise.
This variable captures the ownership structure relevant for information asymmetry
and potential insider selling intensity.

   Market Dummies:  Indicator variables for each exchange—London, Stockholm,
Milan, Frankfurt, and Paris—equal to 1 if the IPO is listed on the corresponding
market. One market serves as the omitted base category. These controls capture dif-
ferences in trading mechanisms, disclosure environments, and market microstructure
across European exchanges.

   Year-Group Dummies:  Indicator variables for the IPO year grouped into
2012–2014, 2015–2017, and 2018–2019, with 2009–2011 as the base period. These
dummies control for time-varying regulatory conditions, market liquidity, and cycle
e↵ects in European IPO activity.

   Size:  Natural logarithm of firm size at the time of the IPO, measured as post-
o↵er market capitalization (note that the size variable is computed based on the
o↵er price in EUR for each IPO regardless of where they are listed). This variable
captures cross-sectional di↵erences in firm scale, which may a↵ect trading behaviour
and sensitivity to unlock events.

                                   34
   Run-up:  Natural logarithm of the pre-unlock price run-up, measured as the
percentage change in the stock price from the o↵er date to day –6 relative to the
lockup expiration. Run-up proxies for pre-event valuation momentum and potential
overreaction that may reverse around the unlock date.

   Abnormal volume:  Natural logarithm of the average daily trading volume
during the estimation window (t = –50 to –6).   This measures baseline liquidity
and order-flow intensity.   In CAR regressions, logAV is included as a control for
di↵erences in underlying trading activity that may influence abnormal returns.

   FractionLocked:  The fraction of total post-IPO shares subject to lockup at the
time of the o↵ering, calculated as locked-up shares divided by total shares outstand-
ing after the IPO. This variable captures the potential supply shock at expiration
and reflects insiders’ retained ownership.

   Pre-Turnover:  Average daily turnover in the estimation window (t = –50 to
–6), measured as trading volume divided by shares outstanding.  This serves as a
liquidity proxy indicating how actively a stock trades prior to the unlock.  Higher
pre-unlock turnover may reduce price pressure e↵ects or signal informed trading
ahead of the event.

   CAR three day pct :  Measures the cumulative abnormal return over the
three-day unlock window (Day 1 to +1). The variable is winsorized at the 5th and
95th percentiles and expressed in percentage terms. When used as an independent
variable in the abnormal volume (AV) regression, it captures whether IPOs with
stronger short-window price reactions also experience di↵erential abnormal trading
volume, controlling for VC/PE backing, market and year fixed e↵ects, firm size,
pre-unlock price run-up, lockup fraction, and pre-event liquidity

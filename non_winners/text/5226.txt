Sustainable Investments and Stock Returns: A Study of the COVID-19 Pandemic

Abstract:

   Using cross-sectional regressions, we study the relationship between sustainability
   scores and stock returns during the COVID-19 pandemic. We find that EU firms that
   entered the COVID-19 stock market crash with a higher ESG score achieved higher
   stock returns during the stock market crash. The same relationship is shown to hold
   between a firm’s social score and stock returns. We find no evidence that the
   environmental or governance score impacted stock returns during this period. These
   findings are consistent with investor preferences having shifted towards more
   sustainable firms during the stock market crash. Thus, firms with higher
   sustainability scores within certain areas were in higher demand and could achieve
   higher stock prices and returns. However, this is likely not the full explanation for
   individual EU countries, as we find different effects for the respective countries and
   sustainability scores. When observing the COVID-19 recession, we find no
   relationship between sustainability scores and stock returns.

Keywords:

   ESG, CSR, sustainability, COVID-19, stock market crash, recession, investor
   preferences

             An increasing number of people are seeking to invest sustainably by
taking environmental, social and governance factors into consideration to mitigate risks
and achieve larger long-term returns (Sakuma-Keck, van Hoorn, Casarsa, Gallagher,
Lavayssière and Rathke, 2021; European Commission, n.d.). As of 2020, $35.4 trillion
were invested into sustainable assets, representing a 55% increase since 2016 (GSI
Alliance, 2021). Additionally, the European Union (EU) created the Non-Financial
Reporting Directive in 2014. The directive, which is legally binding for all EU
countries, requires listed firms with more than 500 employees to report about
environmental, social, and corporate governance matters in relation to the firm to ensure
transparency (European Union and Council of the European Union, 2014).
              However, prior literature has failed to reach a consensus on whether
investing in CSR increases shareholder wealth or not. For example, Lins, Servaes and
Tamayo (2017) find that US firms with a higher CSR rating prior to the financial crisis
in 2008-2009 achieved larger stock returns during the crisis compared to lower scoring
firms. On the other hand, Krüger (2015) observes a time outside the financial crisis and
finds that investors tend to react negatively to both positive and negative news about a
firm’s CSR measures. Further, Friedman (1970) famously questioned the responsibility
of firms to solve societal and environmental problems rather than maximise shareholder
value.
             Thus, it raises the question if CSR measures are purely implemented to
mitigate risks, satisfy stakeholders, and preserve the planet, or if addressing these
problems also increases shareholder value by being associated with higher returns. By
observing if there is a relationship between EU companies’ ESG scores and stock
returns during the COVID-19 stock market crash, we can contribute to a current gap in
the literature and provide guidance to policy makers and companies.
             During financials busts, investments into risky assets decrease compared
to what occurs in financial booms (Cohn, Engelmann, Fehr and Maréchal, 2015). This
increase in risk aversion is associated with higher risk premia and an overall drop in
stock prices (Cohn et al., 2015). However, other channels may worsen or improve this
change in stock prices. Mahmoud and Meyer (2021) find that investor preferences
shifted towards more sustainable firms during the COVID-19 stock market crash,
caused by an inverse relationship between risk and financial or nonpecuniary benefits.
Drawing upon these findings, we hypothesize that this heightened demand for firms
with higher ESG scores during the COVID-19 stock market crash is associated with
higher stock prices and higher stock returns for such firms. Thus, in times when stock
prices decline in general, we aim to find out if being more sustainable, measured as
having higher ESG scores, may be a channel through which firms can minimize their
stock price decline. In this paper, a company’s ESG scores refers their ESG Score,
Environmental Score, Social Score, and Governance Score.
            We define the COVID-19 stock market crash to have occurred between
February and March 2020, when a European market proxy, FTSEurofirst 300, declined
by -21.47% (Capital IQ, 2022). To test if there is a relationship between EU firms’ ESG
scores and stock returns, we employ the use of cross-sectional regressions. Raw and
abnormal returns calculated between February and March 2020 are used as dependent
variables in the regressions. The different types of ESG scores are then employed as
independent variables. First, regressions with the ESG Score are performed to detect if
there is a relationship between this common measure of sustainability and stock returns.
To observe a possible relationship between stock returns and the separate parts of the

                                   2
ESG Score, additional regressions with the Environmental Score, Social Score and
Governance Score are performed. To minimize omitted variable bias, control variables
used by Lins et al. (2017) are also included.
              We find that EU firms that entered the COVID-19 stock market crash with
a higher ESG Score achieved higher stock returns during the stock market crash. A one-
standard-deviation increase in the ESG Score is associated with a 1.68 percentage point
increase in Raw Returns and a 1.76 percentage point increase in Abnormal Returns.
These findings are, albeit in smaller magnitude, in line with Lins et al. (2017) who find
that a one-standard-deviation increase in a firm’s CSR rating is associated with a 1.83
and a 3.31 percentage point increase in raw and abnormal returns, respectively, during
the financial crisis. Further, we find that the Governance Score and the Social Score
positively affect stock returns when the other scores are not included in the regression.
No support is found for the Environmental Score being associated with an increase or
decrease in stock returns. Once all the individual scores are simultaneously included in
one regression, the Social Score is the only score to maintain its positive relationship
with stock returns. Thus, we find no support for the Environmental Score or the
Governance Score having an effect on stock returns during the stock market crash once
all components of the ESG Score are included. A one-standard-deviation increase in a
firm’s Social Score is now associated with a 1.52 and a 1.73 percentage point increase
in Raw Returns and Abnormal Returns, respectively.
             To test the robustness of this finding, we divide all firms into quartiles
based on their Social Score. This allows us to observe if the relationship between the
Social Score and stock returns is different for firms with a very high or very low Social
Score. Our findings continue to confirm that firms that entered the stock market crash
with a higher Social Score achieved significantly higher stock returns during the stock
market crash compared to firms with a lower Social Score. Firms belonging to the
fourth quartile achieve Raw Returns that are 4.3 percentage points higher than firms in
the first quartile. For Abnormal Returns, the difference between firms in the fourth and
first quartiles is 4.6 percentage points. These findings are consistent with investor
preferences shifting towards more sustainable firms within certain areas during the
stock market crash. However, it remains unclear why we find no relationship between
stock returns and a firm’s Environmental Score or Governance Score.
              In an extension, we perform the same cross-section regressions as for the
stock market crash, but we extend the time horizon to the COVID-19 recession. The
recession is defined as having occurred between March 2020 and December 2020.
Unlike for the stock market crash, we find no support for a firm’s ESG Score or Social
Score having a statistically significant impact on stock returns for firms headquartered
in the EU during the recession. Nor do we find support for a firm’s Environmental
Score or Governance Score impacting stock returns during this time. Thus, we conclude
that the results obtained for the stock market crash are not robust to a change in the time
horizon.
             Lastly, it is possible that some EU countries may exhibit different effects
than others. Therefore, we perform regressions on a country-by-country basis for firms
in Sweden, Italy, Germany, France, and the UK during the stock market crash. We find
no support for a firm’s ESG Score having a statistically significant impact on stock
returns in the different countries. However, once all the separate sustainability scores
are added into one regression, we find evidence of both positive and negative
relationships between the separate scores and stock returns for different countries. For

                                   3
example, French firms display a positive relationship between the Environmental Score
and returns, whilst the opposite is found for Swedish firms. The small sample sizes and
at times low significance levels do lead us to interpret these findings with caution.
However, it highlights the importance for companies and the EU to understand how
firms in each of the Member States may face different consequences when increasing
their investments in ESG.
            Altogether, we show that EU firms were collectively able to improve
shareholder wealth during the COVID-19 stock market crash by improving their ESG
Score or their Social Score. Our findings are in line with investor preferences shifting
towards more sustainable firms during the COVID-19 stock market crash, which may
have caused demand to rise for more sustainable firms. However, as our findings do not
hold for the COVID-19 recession, we do not find evidence that firms and shareholders
always benefit from improving their ESG scores when measuring stock returns outside
a stock market crash.

                                   I. Literature Review
A. Stock Prices during Booms and Busts
              As a starting point, our paper draws upon the findings of Cohn et al.
(2015) who highlight that higher risk aversion may be one channel through which share
prices decrease in general during financial busts. This is due to risk aversion being
countercyclical, causing higher risk premia to be required when investing into risky
assets during financial busts. One of the main channels contributing to risk aversion
during financial downturns is people’s increased fear, which may spur on further asset
price declines (Cohn et al., 2015). Huber, Huber and Kirchler (2021) confirm that these
findings hold during the COVID-19 stock market crash in March 2020. We contribute
to these findings by establishing if there are differences in stock returns during the
COVID-19 stock market crash between firms with higher ESG scores and firms with
lower ESG scores. Such that, even though overall share prices drop, some firms are hit
less hard than others by the decrease in share prices.

B. Stock Prices and ESG Scores
             Our paper is closely related to Lins et al. (2017) who find that US firms
with higher CSR ratings prior to the financial crisis achieved higher returns during the
crisis. We follow their lead by partly replicating their regression models. However, we
extend their work by instead looking at EU firms during the COVID-19 pandemic, and
by delving deeper into the components of a firm's ESG Score. Firstly, unlike for US
firms (Krüger, 2015), ESG reporting is mandatory for large firms across the EU
(European Union and Council of the European Union, 2014). Thus, there may be a
higher likelihood that US firms performing poorly within the ESG areas refrain from
reporting such information. Therefore, by using EU data we may limit survival bias in
the dataset, providing us with more reliable results. Secondly, by focusing on the
COVID-19 pandemic, we move away from the concept of low trust periods and instead
focus on investor preferences. This will allow us to observe if Lins et al.’s (2017)
findings continue to hold for a different period when other forces may be at play. Lastly,
we also contribute to Lins et al.’s (2017) findings by separating the ESG Score into an
Environmental Score, a Social Score, and a Governance Score for each firm. We draw

                                   4
inspiration from Krüger (2015) who finds that the different areas of ESG could provoke
different reactions from investors. For example, the author (Krüger, 2015) shows that
the environmental and community scores are associated with negative responses from
investors.

C. Preferences for Green Firms

            We argue that investor preferences may be one underlying mechanism as
to why ESG scores may impact stock returns. El Ghoul, Guedhami, Kwok and Mishra
(2011) find that the demand to invest in firms with better sustainability measures is
higher compared to firms who invest less in social responsibility.
             However, this demand may not be constant as social preferences may vary
over time when the investor’s environment changes due to an external shock. Mahmoud
and Meyer (2021) find an increase in socially responsible investing in the US financial
market during the initial stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, i.e., mid-March 2020. This
indicates that the preference for social responsibility is countercyclical rather than
constant. Their findings support the idea that risk had an inverse relationship with
nonpecuniary and financial benefits during the initial stages of the COVID-19
pandemic, as the high uncertainty levels caused by the pandemic may have caused the
more risk averse investors to focus on social responsibility (Mahmoud and Meyer,
2021). Although we do not measure investor preferences, we are able to draw upon their
findings and contribute to them by focusing on the relationship between stock returns
and the stated countercyclicality of investor preferences. If more investors preferred to
invest in firms with higher ESG scores during the COVID-19 pandemic, we would
expect such firms to have higher returns than lower scoring firms. This may be due to
the increased demand driving up stock prices during the stock market crash, all else
equal.

                                         II. Data
A. Sample Construction
             Our dataset consists of companies whose headquarters are in the EU,
Norway, and the UK. Norwegian companies are included as the Norwegian Accounting
Act directly relates to the Non-Financial Reporting Directive (Knudson, 2017).
Additionally, although the UK left the EU in January 2020, EU laws were still
applicable during the transition period which lasted until December 2020 (European
Commission, 2020c). As the transition period was not over when the stock market crash
occurred, UK firms are also included in our sample. Data for all 28 EU countries (pre-
UK’s withdrawal) are not available in Refinitiv Eikon. Therefore, as seen in Table I
Panel A of the appendix, our sample consists of firms from the 20 EU countries for
which financial data and ESG scores are available. The countries representing the
largest parts of the sample are the UK with 21.4% of firms, France with 17.9%, and
Germany with 9.9%. Obviously, this unequal representation of countries in our sample
is important to bear in mind when interpreting the findings of this paper. However, the
problem of sample construction to determine effects on the EU as a whole will always
be problematic, as it questions which countries should have more of a say than others.
Trying to fix this by employing an equal weighting of each country may, for example,

                                   5
not provide a good measure of potential economic effects. This outcome could then
defeat the purpose of the tests. As it is difficult for us to determine the weight each
country should have, we avoid attempting to weight the separate countries. Instead, in
an extension of the paper, the tests are also performed on a country-by-country basis to
increase the transparency of our results.
             Our sample of EU firms is obtained from the Refinitiv Eikon database
where stock returns, financial information, and ESG scores are supplied. The database
has collected information about firms’ ESG practices since 2002 and covers 9000
companies worldwide (Refinitiv, 2022a). The ESG scores are updated yearly,
coinciding with firms releasing their non-financial reports (Refinitiv, 2022a). Each
firm’s ESG Score is created by an industry-specific weighting of three other scores:
“Environmental Pillar Score”, “Social Pillar Score”, and “Governance Pillar Score”.
Henceforth, these scores continue to be referred to as the Environmental Score, Social
Score, and Governance Score. To obtain these three separate scores, information about
the following is collected from each firm: resource use, emissions, innovation,
workforce, human rights, community, product responsibility, management,
shareholders, and CSR strategy. Each company is then benchmarked against companies
within the same industry for the environmental and social categories, or within the same
country for the governance categories. Refinitiv Eikon rank all scores from 0 to 100,
with 0 being the lowest (Refinitiv, 2022a). To ensure that the independent variables in
the regressions are in the same magnitude, we divide all ESG scores by 100. Therefore,
the ESG Score, Environmental Score, Social Score, and Governance Score each range
from 0 to 1.
               A few firms have zero as their reported Environmental Score. It is still
reasonable to proceed with these firms as they have a total ESG Score reported by
Refinitiv Eikon and are obliged to report their environmental impact (European Union
and Council of the European Union, 2014). Thus, it is plausible that their scores are
zero due to them being the worst performers amongst their industry peers rather than a
lack of information.
             When collecting ESG data from the Refinitiv Eikon database, there are
several aspects that need to be considered. As the ESG scores are relative to industry or
country peers (Refinitiv, 2022a), our findings will only be valid for relative ESG scores.
On the one hand, Refinitiv Eikon’s scores make it easier for investors to differentiate
between companies. This is done by providing information about which firms are
leading or slacking in their ESG efforts relative to the industry or country standard. On
the other hand, it may produce skewed results as the only way a firm can achieve a
higher score is by outperforming some of its peers, not necessarily by only improving
their ESG practices.
             An additional problem is that the scores are not definitive until five years
have passed. Thus, if new information is released within five years, the scores will be
updated (Refinitiv, 2022a). As our event window requires the ESG scores to be
collected from December 2019, this may impact our results.
             Further, firms in sin industries are commonly removed from datasets. This
is due to them automatically being penalized for belonging to such an industry without
any way to improve their score (Lins et al., 2017). However, as the Environmental
Score and the Social Score are relative to peers in the same industry, Refinitiv Eikon
avoids this potential problem. Thus, a gambling firm could achieve a high score if it
incorporates better ESG practices than its peers. On the other hand, the highest-ranking

                                   6
company in a sin industry may receive the same score as the highest-ranking company
in a non-sin industry. To combat this problem, Refinitiv (2022a) employ different
industry-weights for each of the ESG subcategories to make comparisons across
industries more meaningful.
              Lastly, many other authors (e.g., Lins et al. (2017) and Krüger (2015)) use
the MSCI ESG Stats database to obtain ESG scores. Although the database has been
used in many prior research papers, Refinitiv Eikon’s database lends itself as a
robustness check. As Krüger (2015) states, each database collects and measures CSR in
different ways leading to different scores. However, even by using different databases,
the underlying problem of potential measurement errors may persist due to a lack of a
global benchmark to measure ESG scores against.

             The two different measures of returns used are raw buy-and-hold returns,
and cumulative abnormal returns (CARs). The first-mentioned is used to observe how
large the returns were during the stock market crash from the perspective of an investor
holding the stock (Lyon, Barber and Tsai, 1999). The latter is instead able to detect if
the firms in the sample were able to consistently earn abnormal returns during the same
period (Lyon et al., 1999). When it comes to measures of abnormal returns, some

                                   8
research points towards abnormal buy-and-hold returns being preferred as CARs can
prove to be a biased predictor of abnormal buy-and-hold returns (Barber and Lyon,
1997). On the other hand, Fama (1998) indicates that abnormal buy-and-hold returns
may lead to worsening of the “bad-model problem” that, for example, occurs through
spurious abnormal returns predicted by models like the CAPM. Thus, the CAPM may
not be able to correctly predict expected returns. This bad-model problem is then
worsened by compounding the returns over a longer period (Fama, 1998). Instead, Fama
(1998) recommends using CARs. We follow the lead of Lins et al. (2017) and the
recommendation of Fama (1998) by using CARs to measure abnormal returns.
However, we also use raw buy-and-hold returns, in line with Lins et al. (2017), in a bid
to omit some “bad-model problems” as the measure avoids the need to use models such
as the CAPM. Obviously, the downside is that it leads to these returns becoming raw
buy-and-hold returns rather than abnormal buy-and-hold returns, measuring slightly
different things. By using both buy-and-hold returns and CARs, we intend to control for
the potential problems raised above.

           What the significance level should be set to in order to reject the null
hypothesis is much debated. For example, Labovitz (1968) states that the commonly
used statistical significance level of 5% should not be mindlessly adopted. Instead,
some criteria to consider are sample size, the potential consequences of a type I error,
and the size of the true effect. As our sample size is relatively small compared to Lins et
al. (2017), the potential impact of our (potentially falsely claimed) findings is small, and
the expected findings are likely small we need to adopt a somewhat larger
predetermined significance level between 5% and 10% (Labovitz, 1968). However, we
are testing Lins et al.’s (2017) findings. To validate these, we need to avoid having a
significance level that is too high. In conclusion, the main measure of statistical
significance in this paper will be set to at least 5%, but findings at the 10% level will
also be considered.

B. Control Variables
             Since there were substantial differences in performance across sectors
during the COVID-19 pandemic (de Vet, Nigohosyan, Núñez Ferrer, Gross, Kuehl and
Flickenschild, 2021), industry dummies based on The Refinitiv Business Classifications
(TRBC) are included in all models. For example, many companies in the healthcare and
digital industries performed well, while companies in the aerospace industry
experienced considerable negative impact on their performance (de Vet et al., 2021). In
total, TRBC recognises 153 different industries (Refinitiv, 2022b), comparable to the 2-
digit SIC code representing 99 industries (OSHA, n.d.).
              Further, as the EU consists of multiple countries, country dummies are
also included to control for differences between Member States. The European
Commission (2020a) has alluded to Member States being highly dependent on each
other and facing similar problems during the pandemic. For example, a lack of recovery
in one country could create problems for other Member States. Further, almost all EU
countries had some type of containment measure in place by mid-March resulting in
similar impacts for many EU countries. Although this may be the case, it is still
important to control for other differences between countries. For example, there is
evidence suggesting that countries with less ability to implement policies to reduce the
impact of the pandemic were hit harder (European Commission, 2020a).
              Further, it needs to be considered that a firm’s performance during the
stock market crash may not only be related to the ESG scores themselves but may also
be affected by other variables. For example, in times of a severe economic downturn,
firms with better financial health (i.e., firms that are profitable, have overall low debt,
less short-term debt maturing during the severe economic downturn, and have large
cash holdings) are still able to invest and are more likely to survive (Duchin, Ozbas and
Sensoy, 2010). We therefore control for the financial health of firms in the year before
the onset of the pandemic by including the following control variables: Cash Holdings
(cash in proportion to total assets), Long-Term Debt (long-term debt divided by total
assets), Short-Term Debt (current liabilities over total assets), and Profitability
(operating income divided by total assets).

                                  14
             Moreover, through the Fama-French three-factor European model and the
additional momentum factor, we control for the firms’ factor loadings found to impact
                                                                       4
stock returns (Fama and French (1993) and Carhart (2012)) . Daniel and Titman (1997)
do not find full support for a firm’s factor loadings having an impact on returns in the
way Fama and French (1993) argue. Instead, they put forward that a firm’s
characteristics impact stock returns instead of it being due to how they covary with the
Fama-French factors. Such characteristics mentioned by Daniel and Titman (1997),
which are also controlled for in the regressions, are Book-to-market (book value of
equity divided by market capitalisation), and ln(Market Cap) (log of the market value of
equity in millions of dollars which controls for size).
              Further, it has been shown that an investor can achieve high abnormal
returns during a 3- to 12-month holding period by buying stocks that have previously
performed well and selling stocks that have historically not performed so well
(Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993). However, for longer holding periods such as 3- to 5-
years, this effect is reversed with prior losers instead experiencing higher abnormal
returns (De Bondt and Thaler, 1985). We control for the first case by employing a
variable called Momentum, which represents a firm’s historical buy-and-hold raw
returns between February 2019 and January 2020. To avoid a reduction in the sample
size, we refrain from controlling for longer periods in line with Lins et al. (2017).
            Fama and French (1992) acknowledge that there is a difference between
positive and negative book-to-market firms regarding, for example, stock price
performance. Since our dataset does not include negative book-to-market firms we do
not employ a dummy variable to control for this.
              Lastly, as Goyal and Santa-Clara (2003) have stated that a firm’s stock
price volatility might be an important factor when explaining stock returns, we control
for firms’ Idiosyncratic Risk. This variable is calculated by first performing a regression
with a firm’s monthly excess returns during the estimation window (see Figure 2)
against the excess market returns. The residual variance for each firm is its Idiosyncratic
Risk.
             The financial control variables and the ESG scores are from December
2019, whilst Idiosyncratic Risk and Momentum are measured 24 and 12 months before
the COVID-19 stock market crash, respectively.

             In Table II, the main results for the COVID-19 stock market crash are
presented. Panel A columns (1) and (2) indicate that EU firms that entered the stock

crash. These results continue to hold at a 5% significance level once the additional
control variables have been added in Panel B. A one-standard-deviation increase (0.187)
in the ESG Score is associated with a 1.68 percentage point increase in Raw Returns and
a slightly higher increase of 1.76 percentage points for Abnormal Returns. These effects
are somewhat lower than Lins et al. (2017) reported for the financial crisis, with the
corresponding numbers being an increase of 1.83 percentage points for raw returns and
an increase of 3.31 percentage points for abnormal returns. However, their measure of

stock market crash.

D. Robustness Check – Social Score
             As the Social Score is the only score to remain statistically significant in
Panel C, we re-estimate the regressions by dividing firms into quartiles based on their
Social Score. This is done to observe if the relationship between the Social Score and
stock returns are different for firms with a very high or very low Social Score. Further,
it will test the robustness of our previous findings that a higher Social Score is
associated with higher stock returns. We refrain from doing the same with the ESG
Score, as our main focus is on the separate parts of this score.
             In the regression, found in Table II Panel D in the appendix, Quartile 2 is
a dummy variable taking on the value of one if the Social Score belongs to the second
quartile and zero if it does not. Quartile 3 and Quartile 4 are constructed in a similar
way but correspond to the Social Score belonging to the third and fourth quartiles,
respectively. Firms whose Social Score are among the 25% worst in the sample are

                                  20
included in the first quartile, whilst firms with a Social Score among the top 25% in the
sample belong to Quartile 4.
             The results continue to confirm that if a firm had a higher Social Score
prior to the stock market crash, it is associated with larger stock returns during the stock
market crash compared to lower scoring firms. In the appendix, columns (1) and (2) of
Table II Panel D first represent the two regression models with additional control
variables omitted. It indicates that firms whose Social Score belong to the fourth
quartile achieve Raw Returns and Abnormal Returns that are 3.9 and 4.3 percentage
points higher, respectively, compared to firms whose Social Score belong to the first
quartile. These effects are represented by the coefficients of the Social Score in the
regressions. Once control variables are added in columns (3) and (4), the effects are
even larger, but the significance level drops to 10%. Firms belonging to Quartile 4 are
shown to achieve Raw Returns that are 4.3 percentage points higher than firms in the
first quartile. For Abnormal Returns this effect is slightly larger with the difference in
Abnormal Returns between firms in the fourth and first quartiles being 4.6 percentage
points. As our regressions only consider the Social Score, the results are not directly
comparable to those of Lins et al. (2017) who divide firms into quartiles based on a
firm’s “ES Score”. To avoid making a false comparison, we refrain from comparing our
findings.
             Thus, as the ESG Score is positive and statistically significant, it indicates
that firms were able to improve their stock returns during the stock market crash by
having a higher ESG Score before the stock market crash. These results are consistent
with investor preferences having shifted towards firms with a higher ESG Score during
the COVID-19 stock market crash (Mahmoud and Meyer, 2021), potentially creating
higher demand and stock prices. Once all ESG building blocks are taken into
consideration simultaneously, we only find support for the Social Score impacting stock
returns. No such relationship is found for the Environmental Score, or Governance
Score. This partly lends support to Krüger’s (2015) findings that different parts of the
ESG Score impact stock returns differently. However, these findings are not consistent
with investor preferences shifting towards favoring firms in all areas of sustainability.
Instead, it may point towards investors favoring firms with a high Social Score.
However, establishing if such a relationship does exist would require further research.
              Overall, our findings point towards it being valuable for firms to invest in
improving their Social Score or ESG Score to increase shareholder value. However, as
analyzing the potential costs of an increase of a firm’s ESG scores is beyond the scope
of this paper, it remains unclear if firms will find it valuable to increase their scores to
achieve higher stock returns in the future, or if the costs of the improvement potentially
decrease the gain in shareholder value. Additionally, it is important to note that firms
also impact other groups than shareholders. Therefore, even though improving certain
ESG scores may not be increase shareholder wealth, it may still prove valuable to other
stakeholders and society in general.

                                    IV. Extensions
A. COVID-19 Recession
             To observe if our findings are valid for other economic downturns during
the pandemic, we extend the time horizon to the COVID-19 recession. By looking at a
time period that is not characterized by extreme returns that are typical of a stock

                                  21
market crash, we may obtain different results. This is especially relevant as the Raw
Returns and Abnormal Returns are highly sensitive to the defined time period.
Additionally, any results found during the recession may prove more valuable to firms
as they provide insights that are applicable over a longer time period compared to a
stock market crash. Thus, firms may be more inclined to improve their ESG scores if we
are able to find that shareholder wealth increases in the long-run as well as the short-
run.
             In a report released by the European Commission (2020a) during spring
2020, it was stated that the EU was facing the deepest recession to date with record
levels of uncertainty. As infection rates began to grow, most EU countries had
containment measures in place by mid-March, causing output to fall during the second
quarter of 2020 (European Commission, 2020a). During this time, there were reports of
the Euro area operating at between 25-30% below capacity (European Commission,
2020b). GDP in EU countries was forecasted to drop by 7.5%, a far worse drop than
during the financial crisis between 2008 and 2009 (European Commission, 2020a).
Further, unemployment rates rose in the beginning of the second quarter of 2020 and
did not subside until the end of the third quarter the same year (Eurostat, 2021a).
             In hindsight, real GDP ended up dropping by a total of 5.9% in 2020
compared to 2019 (Eurostat, 2021b). By the first quarter of 2021, EU’s outlook on the
coming year was positive with new vaccines and fewer containment measures, resulting
in a forecasted GDP growth of over 3.7% throughout 2021 (European Commission,
2021a; European Commission, 2021b). By the end of the second quarter of 2021,
unemployment rates in the EU were back at pre-pandemic levels (Eurostat, 2021a).
             Considering this, we define the recession as having begun by March 2020
as COVID-19 was declared a pandemic, uncertainty was high, unemployment rates
grew, and output and GDP decreased. As the EU’s forecasts for late 2020 and the start
of 2021 indicated near future economic growth, in combination with European market
proxies recovering to pre-pandemic levels and unemployment levels beginning to
decrease, it is plausible that the EU’s economy was re-emerging from the recession by
December 2020, marking the end of the recession.
              As seen in Figure 1 of the appendix, the recession as we have defined it
had a considerably different stock market reaction compared to the stock market crash.
Between March and December 2020, the FTSEurofirst 300 achieved a positive return of
19.33%. By December 2020, the index value had risen above its pre-recession value.
             Seen in the appendix in Table I Panel C, the time horizon extension also
has a large effect on the summary statistics reported for the stock returns. Raw Returns
and Abnormal returns are both on average positive during the recession. However, the
median Raw Returns is still slightly negative at -4%, whilst Abnormal Returns turn
positive with a median of 6.3%. Just like for the stock market crash, we observe a large
                                           th                          th
difference in returns between the 25  percentile and the 75  percentile. Thus, it seems
reasonable to argue that some firms have fared better than others during this extended
period as well.
            Although Mahmoud and Meyer (2021) only measure a change in investor
preferences during the COVID-19 stock market crash, it is still possible that the inverse
relationship between risk, and financial and nonpecuniary benefits, continues to hold for
the recession. Thus, we would still observe a shift in investor preferences towards more
sustainable firms. This is due to the likelihood that higher risk aversion also continued

                                  22
to occur during the recession, in line with Cohn et al.’s (2015) findings that risk
aversion increases in financial busts.
              Table III, found in the appendix, contains the results from the cross-
sectional regressions performed for the COVID-19 induced recession. In Panel A,
unlike for the stock market crash, we find no support for a statistically significant
relationship between a firm’s ESG Score, with which it entered the recession, and a
firm’s stock returns during the recession. This result continues to hold when additional
firm characteristics are included as control variables in Panel B.
             When observing the separate components of the ESG Score in Panel A and
Panel B, we are still unable to find any relationship between stock returns and a firm’s
Environmental Score, Social Score, or Governance Score, respectively, during the
recession. Thus, unlike for the stock market crash, none of the ESG building blocks
seem to be associated with higher stock returns when they are considered in isolation.
             Further, as displayed in Panel C, this continues to be the case when
including the Environmental Score, Social Score and Governance Score in the same
regressions. Thus, the positive and significant relationship found between a firm’s
Social Score and stock returns during the stock market crash does not hold when the
time horizon is extended. It is possible that during the recession other factors than a
firm’s sustainability measures became increasingly important in explaining a firm’s
Raw Returns and Abnormal Returns.
            When looking at the control variables in Panel C of Table III in the
appendix, we find indications that this is the case. Firstly, opposite to what we find
during the stock market crash, Idiosyncratic Risk shows to be positively and statistically
significant at a 1% level for Abnormal Returns in column (4). This may be due to
investors holding under-diversified portfolios, causing them to demand a greater return
compensation (Merton (1987), and Malkiel and Xu (2004)). A one-standard-deviation
increase (0.006) in Idiosyncratic Risk is associated with a large 5.73 percentage point
increase in Abnormal Returns once all control variables are added. Not only is this
impact in the opposite direction of what occurs during the stock market crash, it is also
three-and-a-half times larger.
             Secondly, as seen in column (3) and (4) of Panel C, some measures of
firms' financial health become increasingly important in explaining returns during the
recession, whilst others lose their explanatory power compared to the stock market
crash. Short-term debt now shows a negative relationship with both Raw Returns and
Abnormal Returns, and the same holds for Long-term debt. A one-standard-deviation
increase in Short-term debt (0.146) and Long-term debt (0.136) is associated with a
-7.07 and a -2.94 percentage point decrease in Abnormal Returns, respectively. These
results seem reasonable in line with Duchin et al.’s (2010) findings.
              Concludingly, we do not find that the results obtained for the stock market
crash are robust to a change in the time horizon, namely by instead observing the
COVID-19 induced recession. Unlike for the stock market crash, a firm’s ESG Score, or
Social Score does not have a statistically significant impact on stock returns for firms
headquartered in the EU. In fact, none of the ESG scores are statistically significant
during this period. These results are consistent with investor preferences only shifting
towards more sustainable firms during the stock market crash (Mahmoud and Meyer,
2021) rather than also applying to the recession as we hypothesized. By also observing a
shift in which control variables help explain stock returns compared to the stock market
crash, it may indicate that different factors are at play in the two periods. Thus, during

                                  23
the COVID-19 recession, our findings indicate that firms were unable to increase stock
returns by entering the recession with higher ESG scores. In combination with our
findings for the stock market crash, it signals that entering the COVID-19 pandemic
with a high ESG Score or Social Score only benefitted shareholder value in the short-
run.

B. Country Specific Regressions
             Although we control for country specific factors through dummy
variables, it is still possible that the results found in Table II for the COVID-19 induced
stock market crash are more prominent in certain countries than others. If these
countries have enough observations in the data set, or countries have counteractive
effects, it may lead to skewed results for the EU as a whole. It is important to detect
such patterns to avoid the EU unknowingly establishing regulation that have different
effects in different countries. Further, it alleviates the concern that firms in different
countries are being led to believe that the positive relationship between stock returns
and an EU firm’s ESG Score, and Social Score automatically applies to them too.
              To extend our findings to individual countries, we run the same
regressions as in Table II during the stock market crash. Only countries with enough
observations to successfully run the regressions are included. These countries are
Sweden, Italy, Germany, France, and the UK. To minimize the decrease in the degrees
of freedom for each regression model, the Global Industry Classification Standard
(GICS) is used instead of the previously used TRBC. GICS covers fewer sectors which
implies fewer dummy variables.
             The results can be found in Table IV of the appendix. However, a few
caveats are in order. Overall, the sample sizes for the individual countries are too small
to make a confident statement about the population and the magnitude of the economic
effects. Therefore, we refrain from reporting the economic impact of a one-standard-
deviation increase in the scores. Instead, we focus on if the relationship is positive or
negative. In addition, due to some of the findings only being statistically significant at a
10% significance level, we are hesitant to conclude with confidence that they are valid
for most firms in each country.
             Interestingly, Panel A shows that the ESG Score has a negative
relationship with stock returns for UK firms, whilst the opposite effect is shown for
French firms. However, once additional control variables are included in Panel B, we
find no statistically significant impact of a firm’s ESG Score on stock returns for any of
the countries. This is unlike the result for the EU as a whole, where we find a positive
relationship between a firm’s ESG Score and stock returns.
             Turning to the separate scores making up a firm’s ESG Score, Panel C
shows that the Environmental Score is associated with lower stock returns during the
stock market crash for firms headquartered in both Germany and the UK. This is in line
with Krüger’s (2015) findings. Once additional control variables are added in Panel F,
the significance level increases from 10% to 5%, but the findings only continue to hold
for German firms.
              In Panel D, we find support for French firms displaying a positive
relationship between a firm’s Social Score and stock returns, which is in line with the
findings for the EU. However, once additional control variables are employed in Panel
G, the positive relationship only remains statistically significant between a firm’s Social

                                  24
Score and Raw Returns. As this finding is only at a 10% significance level, some
caution needs to be taken in interpreting it as representative of the whole population.
             Like for the EU, we find support for French and UK firms that the
Governance Score has a statistically significant relationship with stock returns when
additional control variables are omitted in Panel E. Much like the ESG Score, French
firms show a positive relationship between the Governance Score and stock returns,
whilst the opposite effect is found for UK firms. However, once additional control
variables are added in Panel H, the Governance Score is only shown to impact stock
returns positively for Swedish firms.
            Once all the separate scores are included in one regression in Panel I, we
find no support for any of the countries exhibiting a positive relationship between the
Social Score and stock returns, unlike the findings for the EU. Instead, the Social Score
showcases a negative relationship with stock returns for German firms, which continues
to hold once additional control variables are included in Panel J. The Environmental
Score is associated with a decrease in returns for Swedish firms, in line with Krüger’s
(2015) findings, whilst being associated with an increase in returns for French firms.
These findings also continue to hold once additional control variables are added in
Panel J. Finally, the Governance Score is found to have a negative relationship with
stock returns for UK firms when additional control variables are omitted, with the effect
disappearing once additional control variables are added.
             Concludingly, unlike for the EU, a firm’s ESG Score is not found to have
an impact on stock returns during the stock market crash for any of the countries once
control variables are included. However, when observing the separate scores all run
simultaneously, our findings point towards firms in different countries being faced with
different consequences depending on which score they increase. Thus, these findings
are only partly consistent, for some countries, with investor preferences shifting towards
more sustainable firms during the COVID-19 stock market crash. Once again, as the
sample sizes are small, we are unable to make a confident statement that are valid for all
firms in each of the countries. However, as we do observe some differences between
countries, it warrants further research with larger sample sizes to see if the findings in
this paper line up. Further research is also needed to determine the mechanism behind
why different countries may experience different effects when entering the COVID-19
stock market crash with higher ESG scores.
             Further, our results highlight the need for the EU to consider what effects
potential future legislation, such as requiring firms to invest in ESG, may have on
different countries. This in turn leads us back to the important question of sample
construction. For example, the EU may want to weigh benefits and consequences for
each country based on their population or overall economic impact, or on each country
being equally represented in the sample. This in turn will provide better guidance on the
consequences of potential legislation that the EU implements.

                         V. Conclusion
             In this paper, we find that EU firms that were considered more sustainable
before the COVID-19 stock market crash, characterized as having a higher ESG Score,
achieved higher returns during the stock market crash. Further, when the building
blocks of ESG are considered in isolation, we also find a positive relationship between a
firm’s Social Score and stock returns, and between a firm’s Governance Score and stock

                                  25
returns. However, once all the scores are taken into account simultaneously, only a
firm’s Social Score is found to impact stock returns. Thus, a firm’s Environmental
Score, nor a firm’s Governance Score impact stock returns during the stock market
crash. Although the regressions we perform do not establish the mechanism behind
these results, it is consistent with investor preferences shifting towards more sustainable
firms when an external shock occurs (Mahmoud and Meyer, 2021). This preference
shift is associated with a higher demand, which in turn may impact share prices and
through that, stock returns. Overall, our results are consistent with Lins et al. (2017)
who find that a firm’s CSR rating, with which it entered the financial crisis, positively
impacted stock returns during the financial crisis.
             However, once we consider a longer time horizon by observing the
COVID-19 recession, we find no relationship between an EU firm’s ESG scores and
stock returns. As investor preferences are only observed to change during the COVID-
19 stock market crash, our results are not inconsistent with these findings. It may be that
investor preferences shifted in the beginning of the recession and then reverted back, or
that, as we find support for when observing the firm characteristics as control variables,
other factors are more important in explaining returns during a longer time horizon.
Further, it needs to be considered that the stock market crash led to an -21.47% decrease
in a market proxy FTSEurofirst 300, whilst the recession resulted in a 19.33% increase
in the same market proxy. Thus, is it plausible that this may also have explanatory value
when comparing the two different periods.
               When observing the relationship between ESG scores and stock returns for
some of the countries in our sample, our results are more nuanced compared to the
findings for the entire EU. For the separate countries, shareholder value does not seem
to increase for firms with a higher ESG Score prior to the stock market crash. However,
compared to the entire EU, once the separate building blocks of ESG are considered we
find different effects for each country and for each of the scores. For example, Swedish
firms display a negative relationship between the Environmental Score and stock
returns, whilst the opposite is found for French firms. Thus, some firms may not benefit
from an increase in shareholder value from higher ESG scores when the stock market
declines, whilst the opposite it valid for others. These mixed findings highlight the
importance for the EU to consider how firms may be impacted differently by potential
further legislation within ESG depending on where the firm is headquartered. Further,
more research is needed with larger sample sizes to obtain reliable results for each
country and to explore other severe economic times.

quartile are absorbed by the intercept. Firms whose Social Score are among the 25% worst in the
sample are included in the first quartile, whilst firms with a Social Score among the top 25% in the
sample belong to Quartile 4. As the Social Score is the only ESG score-building block to show

to ensure the robustness of our previous findings. The Social Score and financial data are from

      Why do hedged mutual funds
       underperform  hedge funds?*

     A study of liquidity risk and  IPO first-day  returns

                 ♣                                                                  ♦
Jakob Pettersson                                                       Victor Kihlgren

                                 Abstract

Following the  financial crisis,  investors have increasingly started to look towards hedged
mutual funds  providing  downside protection in  a regulated framework.  This is of particular
interest to retail investors since the hedged strategies  previously  have  been unavailable to
them.  Although the hedged mutual funds claim to  be running the same strategies as
unregulated hedge funds,  they  deliver inferior  returns.  The purpose of our essay is to
provide explanations for the difference in returns. To do this we  run regressions on the
returns of both hedge funds and hedged mutual funds using different  explanatory  variables.
Our hypothesizes are that the outperformance can be explained by less liquid holdings of
hedge  funds  and  their  access  to  underpriced  IPOs.  We  find  that  the  mutual  funds
underperform since  they fail to benefit from the liquidity premium and,  unlike the hedge
funds,  they do not gain from IPO first-day returns. When controlling for these two factors
the two types of funds  exhibit  no significant difference in performance.

1. Introduction

Over the last 10-15 years, hedged mutual funds (HMFs) have  become more common in the

mutual fund industry. HMFs are similar to hedge funds (HFs) that they employ similar

trading strategies; they are able to profit from both under-  and overvaluations, while

traditional mutual funds  are  net  long and  only  occasionally  hedge  their  positions  (Ross

2007).  The most common HF  strategy used by  HMFs is  long/short equity  and its

subcategories.  What separates HMFs from  HFs is that  the former category  falls  under the

same regulations as mutual funds,  meaning  that they have to provide daily liquidity, limit

borrowings to only one-third of total assets and hold a maximum of 15% of total assets in

                                                         1
illiquid securities  (Agarwal, Boyson and Naik 2009). Another difference  is the larger size

and longer history of the HF industry, HFs are currently managing $2 trillion compared to

$50 billion of  HMFs  (Strasberg and  Eder 2011 and Lawson 2010).  They have been an

important source of income for investment banks during the last decade.  In 2006,  they

could have accounted for as much as 30% of the brokers’ equity commission (Teague

2006).  Moreover, HF managers have a different incentive structure than  managers of

HMFs. HFs charge both an annual fee and a performance based fee, while HMFs often only

charge investors with the annual fee (Ross 2007).

The combination of a hedge fund like strategy and a mutual fund structure is  attractive to

investors. The lower fees and more frequent liquidity are both reasons for this.  “We're at

the very early stages of a multitrillion-dollar wave that's going to wash over the long-only

asset-management industry", said Charles Krusen of Krusen  Capital Management LLC

(Barr and Mamudi 2010). From the perspective of a HF manager, setting up a HMF can be

dollars  in  five years.

Agarwal  et al.  (2009) find that HFs outperform HMFs between 1994 and 2004. They

attribute this performance to incentives and regulations. To conduct the analysis they run

the Carhart  (1997) four-factor model and the Fung and Hsieh (2004) seven-factor model.

This is today’s most widely used model for evaluating hedge fund performance. Fung and

Hsieh (2004) argue that a large part of the variation in hedge fund returns can be explained

by market  factors, meaning that HFs are taking on systematic risk rather than exploiting

arbitrage opportunities. They construct a seven-factor asset-based-style model consisting of

two equity-oriented risk factors, two bond-oriented risk factors and three  trend-following

                                        2
risk   factors.   The   trend-following   factors   are   option-based   in   order   to   control   for

nonlinearities in returns. The authors show that the two equity factors can explain a large

part of the risk exposures of most funds in the hedge fund industry. They also show that the

seven factors are found in 57% of the hedge funds in the TASS database and in 37% of the

hedge  funds in the HFR database.  Agarwal et al. (2009)  also show that HMF managers who

have previous HF experience, or are concurrently managing a HF, significantly outperform

the  HMF managers  without HF experience. When comparing the returns of HMFs managed

by someone with HF experience  to HF returns, the HMFs  still underperform.  Further they

find that the performance of those HFs that are  also running a HMF do not underperform

the HFs not offering HMFs.  They therefore show that it is not only poor performing HF

managers who offer  HMFs.  This provides some evidence against the  explanation  that

manager  skill would be the main factor to why the  HFs outperform the HMFs.

We  expand this analysis by providing  two  additional  explanations for the  outperformance.

Due to stronger regulation we want to investigate whether  HMFs  are  able to  benefit from

the illiquidity premium  (1), and if  the HFs  profitable relationship with investments banks

enable them to gain access to larger shares of  Initial Public Offering (IPO)  allocations (2).

Our first  hypothesis  is to investigate if the regulations imposed on the HMFs by the SEC

prevent them from exploiting the illiquidity premium.  To do this we test if HMF returns

can be explained by a measure of market liquidity.  We  predict  that, due to the  regulations

HMFs are not applying the same strategies as  HFs. To our knowledge, this paper is the first

one explaining discrepancies in HF and HMF returns in terms of illiquid holdings. Since

                                        3
the HMFs promise their investors a hedge fund like strategy it is important to investigate if

they succeed in doing so. It has been shown that long/short equity HFs take advantage  of

liquidity risk  (Brandon and Wang 2010 and Sadka 2010), if the HMFs fail to do so they are

not completely applying the same strategy.

Our second objective is to examine the possibility that hedge fund managers are being

allocated bigger shares of IPOs  due to more profitable relationships with underwriters.

Previous research has shown that profitable business relations with underwriters increase

investor access to underpriced IPOs (Reuter 2006).  We hypothesize that the returns of the

HFs in our sample can be explained by IPO first-day returns.  This is interesting since HF

managers are highly rewarded for delivering excess returns. If they consistently are able to

gain access to underpriced IPOs  with almost no downside, this is something that  should be

rewarded  (for further discussion,  see  section  5).

Looking  at  illiquidity  exposures  of  HFs,  Brandon  and  Wang  (2010)  show  that  the

managerial   skill   (alpha)   of   long/short   equity   managers   to   a   large   extent   reflects

compensation for bearing systematic liquidity  risk (beta).  Their results are robust  using

several liquidity measures. A similar study done by  Sadka (2010) reaches  the  same

conclusion when studying  HFs during 1994 to 2008.  This further confirms  the regulation

and incentives  hypothesis of  Agarwal et al.  (2009),  since part of the alpha generated by HF

managers is attributable to illiquid holdings, this might be a reason for the outperformance

of HFs as  the HMFs are not  able to take the same positions.

                                        4
The studies done by  Brandon  and  Wang  (2010)  and  Sadka  (2010)  are of importance  given

the incentive and fee structure in the hedge fund industry, which largely compensates for

manager skill based on an alpha calculated without contemplating liquidity risk. The two

papers   reach   similar   conclusions   using   different   methods.   Since   liquidity   is   an

unobservable variable, prior  research uses  proxies  to control for this risk factor.  Brandon

and Wang (2010)  apply  the liquidity measures of Pastor and Stambaugh (2003) and

Acharya and Pedersen (2005)  with  the Fung and Hsieh  model  and  the Hasanhodzic and Lo

(2007) six-factor  model.  Pastor and Stambaugh (2003)  find that stock returns are related to

sensitivities in fluctuations of liquidity. Their measure of liquidity is based on  the principle

that order flow induces  greater return reversals when liquidity is lower  (for further

description,  see section  3.1).  They report that from 1969 to  1999 the average return on U.S.

equities that showed a high sensitivity to liquidity outperformed those with  low sensitivity

by  7.5%  annually.  Sadka (2010) constructs  his own measure  based on price changes due to

new information and market-making costs  (for further description,  see section 3.1).  He  uses

this with a slightly altered version of the  Fung and Hsieh  (2004)  model.  Concluding  that

funds   with   significant   load   on   liquidity   risk   outperform   low-loading   funds   by

approximately 6%  annually during the period 1994 to  2008.

Regarding the process of IPO allocations, Sherman and Titman (2002) state that since IPO

shares are underpriced on  average, book building in IPOs enable underwriters to reward

their favorite clients.  Aggarwal, Prabhala, and Puri (2002)  state  that  underwriters tend to

allocate more shares to institutions when the shares are expected to appreciate in the

aftermarket; likewise they show that the same allocations are significantly lower when

shares are less likely to appreciate. They prove that institutional allocations in underpriced

                                        5
IPOs are higher than what can be explained by book building principles, but provide no

further evidence explaining why this is the case.  The practice when  investors  try to profit

from  underpricing by selling shares in the  first or second day of trading after an IPO  is

commonly referred to as flipping.  In Aggarwal’s (2003) paper on  this phenomenon, he

finds that institutions are more likely than retail investors to sell shares in the following two

days of an IPO. Even though  underwriters  try to limit flipping since it depresses the share

price in the aftermarket;  Aggarwal finds that 15% of the shares offered in an IPO are

flipped.  Looking further into the process of IPO allocations,  Reuter (2006) questions

whether U.S. underwriters use IPO allocations to reward clients who  generate  large

brokerage business. The author finds evidence of a strong link between the reported IPO

holdings of mutual funds and the level of brokerage commission payments. Further, he

shows that IPO allocations depend on the level of brokerage commission earned by the

underwriter. To  the best of  our knowledge, no research on the connection between IPO

allocations and long/short equity hedge fund returns has been done. Since hedge funds are

the most important clients for the major equity brokerage houses (Teague 2006), it is

reasonable to believe that they receive a preferential treatment when it comes to IPO

allocations.

We build on the previous  literature by studying HMFs over  a longer time period, measuring

their potential exposures  to  two liquidity factors  and an IPO  factor (reflecting  IPO first-day

returns) in our regressions.  The analysis is done using two HF indices and a self-

constructed HMF index  (for details on the datasets,  see section  2).  The most important

finding in this paper is that after controlling for the IPO factor the alphas of the HFs are

                                        6
reduced to the same level as the alphas of the HMFs. This implies that the long/short equity

hedge fund managers add value to their investors through their access to underpriced IPOs.

Furthermore, our results show that  some of the  monthly alpha generated by HF managers

can be explained by added liquidity risk, whilst the illiquidity exposures of the HMF

managers are insignificant.  Like  Agarwal  et al. (2009)  we  find that the HFs load  more

heavily on the size factor, meaning that they buy small stocks and short large stocks. This

means that the HMF managers are unable  to,  or fail to completely mimic the strategies of

the HFs.  For an illustration  on how the added factors affect the alphas of the indices see

Figure 2.

2. Data

2.1  HMF Index

To select our sample of HMFs we use the CRSP  Survivor-Bias-Free U.S. Mutual Fund

Database  and sort out  the  funds categorized by Lipper  Inc.  as Long/Short and  Equity

Market Neutral. By studying individual fund names we detect that some of the funds

categorized as Long/Short actually follow a Long Bias  strategy. We choose to keep this

strategy in our sample  (as they use hedging strategies)  and classify the funds in our index as

following a Long/Short, Long Bias  or an  Equity Market Neutral strategy. Long/Short  funds

typically hold long and short positions in equities,  with  net exposure between 0  and 100%.

Long Bias  also hold long and short positions but maintain a  100% net long exposure. The

most common strategy among these funds is the 130/30 strategy, which holds up to 130%

in long positions and 30% in short. Equity Market Neutral  funds divide their exposures

evenly between long and short positions, meaning that they have a low correlation with the

market.

There is a major issue with only using the Lipper data to compile the sample. Since the

Long/Short   and   Equity   Market   Neutral   categorizations   are   quite   new   (they   were

implemented in early 2006), funds running these strategies that went out of business prior

to 2006  are not included in the list.  This causes an upward performance bias in the sample.

We address this issue by searching the CRSP mutual fund database using the following

search terms: “long/short”, “long-short”, “market neutral”, “arbitrage”, “hybrid”, “hedged”

and “hedge”. To locate the defunct funds following the same strategy as the rest of our

sample we keep all funds going out of  business before 2006 and exclude all that have a

                                        8
                                                                            3
non-equity  strategy. Further, we remove passive management styles.  Following this step

we review the prospectuses and annual reports from the SEC, when this final step is

completed  we include  another seven  defunct funds in  our sample.  We believe the measures

taken generate the best possible sample of the HMF industry. Our total sample  consists of

149 funds (out of which 25 are defunct) with a mean business life of 6.69 years.

We use the monthly return series (net-of-fees) of the funds to construct an equally weighted

monthly HMF index over the period January 1997 to December 2010.  Since mutual funds

have to report to the SEC our HMF index is free from the common biases in HF indices

(see section 2.5). Since CRSP only provides data on U.S. funds, the index may  be biased if

the HMFs in the CRSP database are not representable for the  entire  hedged mutual fund

industry.  Finally, if the Lipper classifications are not correct  or  we fail to capture relevant

funds in our extended search, the index  will not truly reflect the composition of the HMF

industry.

2.3  HFRI Equity Hedge Index

As an additional hedge fund index we use the HFRI Equity Hedge Index, which  consist of

funds that maintain  long and short positions in equity and equity derivative securities. The

strategies employed by the funds in the index can differ in terms of diversification, level of

net exposure, leverage, holding period, etc. The managers of the funds in the index

typically have at least 50% of the assets under management invested.

HFRI mitigates the survivorship bias  (see section 2.5)  problem by exhausting all possible

efforts to receive fund performance until final liquidation. It is also free from the instant

history bias  (see section 2.5)  since they choose not to include historical performance of the

new funds that start reporting to the index (Hedge Fund Research 2011).

2.4  Risk Characteristics

When comparing the statistics of the different return  distributions’ (see Table 1) we note

that the HMFs display a similar level of kurtosis as the HF indices but that they differ in

skewness. This implies that the HMFs take on more risk  than the HFs since negative

skewness implies left-tail risk; extreme negative events increase in probability.  Agarwal et

al. (2009) attribute this to career concerns and managerial co-investments  in HFs. Another

significant difference is the Sharpe ratio. Since the HMFs have the same standard deviation

as the HFs but  exhibit  lower returns, the Sharpe ratio is substantially lower.

2.5  Biases in Hedge Fund Indices

When using data from hedge fund databases it is important to note that that they suffer

from several biases (Fung and Hsieh  2004  and  Agarwal, Boyson  and Naik 2006). Fung  and

Hsieh (2004) discuss three types of biases; the selection bias, the survivorship bias and the

instant history bias. The selection bias is present since hedge funds, unlike mutual funds,

are not required to report and explain their  investment strategies, disclose results etc. Hedge

fund data is typically collected by private organizations that in turn sell the information. If

the funds in the  database  are  not  representative for the hedge fund industry or subsector a

selection bias occurs. This is possible since the hedge funds voluntarily chose whether or

not to report to one of the databases, this occurs if some hedge fund strategies are more

secretive than others.  The hedge fund databases can also suffer from a survivorship bias,

caused by funds generating large losses that may decide to stop reporting. The performance

of the disappearing funds is then likely to be worse than that of the survivors. The third

type of bias in many hedge fund indices is the instant history bias. This occurs when a new

                                       11
fund’s past performance is appended into the database when it enters. It is likely that, only

funds with good past performance chose to enter a database leading to an upward bias in

the average return.

All of our  indices  are equal weighted  (net-of-fees), which is favorable since it gives  a more

general picture of the respective industries. Any bias towards larger funds that would be

present with a value  weighting is removed. A problem with this weighting approach is that

it does not capture the distribution of assets among managers. It is a well  known  HF

industry phenomenon that assets are skewed towards the top twenty five percent of

managers,  who oversees about seventy five percent of the capital (Fung and  Hsieh 2004).

On the  other hand there are other  issues with using a value  weighted index such as an

overemphasis of the performance of leveraged funds.

3.1.3  Robustness

                                                           !
For robustness we run all of the above regressions switching  ℒ! for an additional  liquidity

           !                            10
measure  ℒ! defined by Sadka (2006).  He distinguishes between permanent and transitory

price changes by classifying transaction  price changes into those whose effects  that  persist

in the following transaction (permanent) compared to those that vanish (transitory). A

permanent effect on the stock price is associated with a change in the security’s intrinsic

value (due to new information), and a transitory price effect is associated with market-

making costs. Sadka (2006) estimates the components of price impact by using volume and

the trade’s relative value to the midpoint of the quoted bid-ask spread.  The latter is used to

estimate the sign of the trade. A value below the midpoint is considered to be seller

initiated (-1) and those above, buyer initiated (+1). To determine if the effect is transitory or

permanent a variable for public information is included. Price changes induced by private

information (volume and direction of trade) are considered transitory and those caused by

public  information  are  considered  permanent.  We  believe  that  Sadka’s  measure  for

transitory price changes is the better of the  two, we use this  for robustness.

As a second robustness test, we divide our sample into three time periods: pre-crisis

(January 1997 to June 2007), crisis (July 2007 to July 2009) and post-crisis (August 2009

to December 2010). This is to see if the HFs  and HMFs behave differently during periods

of high market turbulence.

Lastly, to see if the IPO factor might be correlated with an unknown factor affecting HF

returns in general, we run our two main regressions on a Fixed Income Hedge Fund Index.

use  the  Prais-Winston  GLS  regression  that  mitigates  the  problem  with  first  order

autocorrelation in the error terms of a  linear model. We augment this model with robust

standard errors to correct for heteroescadicity. Further, we run all regressions with year

dummies to correct for fixed year effects.

5. Implications and conclusions

In this article, we study the  explanatory factors of HF and HMF returns. To  the best of  our

knowledge, this is the first study investigating  how differences in  illiquidity exposure  and

access to IPO first-day returns can explain discrepancies in performance between HFs and

HMFs.  All funds in our sample apply equity-based strategies. Like  Agarwal  et al. 2009 we

find that the HFs outperform the HMFs when running the Fung and Hsieh (2004) seven-

factor model and the Fama and French (1993) three-factor model. We further  confirm their

findings by showing  that HFs load more heavily on the size factor. When taking into

account the effect of systematic liquidity risk we find that it do  not explain any of the HMF

return. As  Brandon  and Wang (2010) and Sadka (2010) we find that the liquidity factor  can

explain parts of the HF returns.  Although the support for this result is weak when using the

Pastor Stambaugh  (2003)  measure, we believe that our  results obtained with the Sadka

(2006)  measure together with  the findings of previous research,  show  that  long/short equity

hedge funds do carry liquidity risks.  However, even after  adjusting HFs’ returns for this

risk factor  they  still outperform HMFs. After controlling for the IPO factor, the HFs no

longer outperform  the HMFs. For the long/short equity hedge funds the alphas merely

mirror compensation for first-day returns from IPOs. These results are robust to: (1) an

alternative liquidity measure and (2) different time periods.

The results provide strong evidence  that, due to SEC regulations, the HMFs cannot benefit

from the illiquidity premium. They also fail to take the same level of exposure to the size

factor as  HFs, which  also might  be an effect of the regulations since large positions in small

stocks can be  considered illiquid. These are important findings, since investors need to

know that they are not getting the same risk exposures investing in HMFs compared to

                                       25
HFs.   Interestingly,   the   illiquid   exposures   are   not   the   main   explanation   for   the

outperformance of HFs over HMFs. This can instead be attributed to the fact that the HFs

seem to have access to underpriced IPOs. Assuming that the HFs then sell their allocation

shorty after receiving the stocks; they can benefit from the first-day return of the IPO. We

believe this provides  evidence that the long/short equity HF managers add value to their

investors through their relations with IPO underwriters. It is important to note that, unlike

the other factors used in our regressions, the IPO factor cannot be seen  as a direct risk

exposure. If the practice of underpricing were to stop, the returns of funds benefiting from

this would certainly drop but it is not likely that they would be negative. Those engaged in

flipping are essentially facing an asymmetrical risk  with almost no downside and a

                         13
potentially big upside.   Despite the  underwriters’  efforts to allocate IPOs to long term

institutional investors,  Aggarwal (2003) finds  that  institutional clients do the majority of

flipping. We believe  HFs belong to the group  of institutional investors  receiving IPO

allocations  that is the most active in this,  since they are  not typically long term owners.  An

explanation for the absence of attribution from IPO first-day returns to the HMFs could be

that they receive similar IPO allocations as the HFs, but instead of selling the stocks within

a few days chose to hold their positions. Thus, they would not gain from first-day returns.

However, this is not likely since there are no regulations dictating the holding period of

mutual funds. Since the HMFs aim to pursue the same strategies as HFs they would also

take advantage of the IPO premium if they had the possibility.  We therefore conclude that

HFs receive bigger IPO allocations than HMFs.  In order to  validate this conclusion,  one

As mentioned above, HF indices are subject to certain biases. Given these biases there

might be an overrepresentation of hedge funds with good performance. Despite this, our

results show that HFs have significant exposure to liquidity risk which suggests that this

risk not is an important  source to the biases. However, the impact of these biases might

allocations. Both issues would be resolved if hedge fund return data were publicly

available.

Suggested further research could be  to apply our methodology to the  individual hedge fund

level,  as  there might be evidence that some funds are better connected than others when it

comes to IPO allocations. To see if it is exclusively long/short equity hedge funds that

benefit from IPO first-day returns the same tests should be done for long only mutual

funds.

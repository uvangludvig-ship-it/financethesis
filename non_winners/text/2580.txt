     The Development of Institutional Investors’ Effect on Structure and Level of Executive

                     Victor Cedervall (22892) and Alexander Marell (22702)

Abstract
This study aims to examine the effect institutional investors have on compensation schemes within their
holdings. The model constructed assumes both the structure and level of compensation to be dependent
on institutional investor ownership. By first using a Tobit model and verifying the results with an
instrumental variables regression, the study demonstrates with a high degree of certainty that institutional
investors prefer and drive up both levels of incentivized structures and incentive compensation. The size
of the effect has increased during the 21st century with a drastic regime shift between 2005 and 2006. The
influence of institutional investors is subsequently fairly steady, showing no signs of changes during the
financial crisis. It is clear that institutional investors drive up both levels of incentive compensation and
the fraction of total compensation that is incentivized. Further research into the identified regime shift
and drivers within institutional investment firms are suggested.

Keywords: executive compensation, incentive compensation, institutional investors, financial crisis, Tobit

1. INTRODUCTION

1.1. Background
Executive compensation has during the last half century undergone great changes (The Guardian,
2005). Incentive programs as well as executive compensation levels have increased dramatically,
the determinants of which are still largely unknown. Possible explanations from a multitude of
fields have been provided. It has been hypothesized that it is due to shifting relationships of
power, increased competition or as an attempt to battle agency conflicts.

       In the after-math of the global financial crisis, when the United States (U.S.) housing
bubble burst resulting in decline in credit availability and investor confidence, criticism of
corporate governance mounted. Though the primary focus of reform and criticism has been on
the complex interplay of policies causing the housing bubble, increased attention was also
directed at companies not operating in financial markets but in physical consumer markets,
physical business markets and non-physical markets. Together, the popular outrage, perceived
excessive risk-taking and changing macroeconomic environment conspire to alter the principles
by which firms act (United Nations, Corporate governance in the wake of the financial crisis,
2010; Pascual, 2008; Walker, 2009).

       This could conceivably translate into changed corporate governance policies with regard
to executive compensation as investors endeavor to rein in managers in an effort to adapt. This
change in institutional investor driven corporate governance strategy could conceivably be
observed both directly and indirectly. It could be observed directly by observing changes in
compensation, shifting usage and intensity of bonuses, long-term incentive plans, employee
benefits, etc. Indirectly it would be observed by changing investments patterns, in which holdings
are chosen based on a common understanding of corporate governance and executive
compensation schemes.

        An institutional investor is generally an organization which pools money to invest in for
example securities and real property, but could also be an operating company setting aside funds
to invest outside of its core business. Typical investors include banks, insurance companies and
investment funds. Institutional investors have due to their holdings voting rights enabling them
to potentially shape corporate governance issues and trends.

        Institutional investors are therefore likely to be in the fore-front of this endeavor of
pressuring firms to adapt due to their desire and ability to swiftly exert considerable influence.
The roots of this comparably significant influence are multifaceted. Primarily it is a matter of

                                                                                           4
market capitalization; since 1950 public equity owned by institutional investors in the US has
increased precipitously from low levels of 7% to approximately 67% in 2010. Institutional
investor ownership is even more significant when looking at the 1.000 largest firms in the US,
reaching approximately 73%. It is thereby likely that institutional investors now have a larger
capacity to influence issues of corporate governance. Furthermore, an institutional investor is by
design more focused in its actions compared to a dispersed host of individual investors as it acts
as a pooled-investment vehicle, entrusted with representing and acting in the name of its owners
and customers. There is also the issue of immediacy and timing of impact. Trends do not
immediately permeate the populace, especially not with regard to their investing strategies, as
many perhaps do not look over their holding as regularly and routinely as professional and
institutional investors (Aguilar, 2013).

       This thesis will therefore try to answer the question if decisive changes in the
macroeconomic environment, i.e. the global financial crisis of 2007-2008, caused institutional
investors to change their supported incentives schemes.

1.2. Purpose and Relevance
The determinants of executive compensation remain largely unknown though there seems to be a
trend of both increasing compensation and focus on incentives programs. By studying the subject
the hope is to illuminate the influence exerted by institutional investors on executive
compensation with regard to the level of influence and changes observed in a turbulent and
transformative period in history. Identifying both the timing and the drivers of change, be they
either trend breaking or trend intensifying changes, could help further academia’s attempts, both
normative and descriptive, to map corporate governance issues.

1.4. Expected Results
With the advent of institutional investor ownership and its rise to significance, monitoring and
incentivizing as a means of aligning disparate interest also became significant. This study begins
by hypothesizing that there is a continuing and increasing need to monitor and consequently
correctly incentivize managers. Thanks to technological and theoretical advancements,
monitoring has become more frequent, effective and cheap. However there needs to be a
concerted effort to execute strategies aimed at aligning interests through executive compensation.

       With increased competition in globalized financial markets and the means necessary to
enact change and influence strategy present, it is plausible to hypothesize an increasing trend
where institutional investors have an ever increasing effect on the mechanisms of incentive
compensation. It therefore follows that there should be an increasing trend with regard to the
influence wielded by institutional investors in response these.

                                                                                           6
       Furthermore, changing perceptions in the public as to the perceived operation and goal
of institutional investors might change. Trends relating to activism and fads might exacerbate the
changed relationships further. The financial crisis of 2008 is also of interest in this regard. It is
possible that the crunch lead to a prioritization of core business over executive compensation.
Thereby we could expect institutional investors to target more pressing concerns during this
period leading to a weaker relationship throughout the time-period. Alternatively, changed
incentives might have been concluded to be a remedy and efforts to align interests intensified due
to the crisis.

       The determinants of compensation and incentivizing schemes have been theorized upon
with a host of explanations being identified (Chung & Pruitt, 1996). The explanations put
forward include the over-arching concept of operating environment. This concept contains
factors such as operating risk, firm value and ownership structure. Macro-economic changes and
shocks do in this framework invariably alter the determinants of executive compensation. This
leads to the hypothesis of institutional investors changing their proposed compensation schemes
in the light of a changing business environment, i.e. the financial crisis of 2008.

       The hypothesis can thereby be summarized in the following way. (1) Institutional
investors influence executive compensation, (2) this influence is increasing over time, (3)
institutional investors pushed for changed compensation schemes as a result of the financial
crisis. Evidence of such a shift is expected to be found within the studied time period.

2. THEORY

A modern corporation is comprised of diverse units, primarily shareholders, managers and
workforce i.e. stakeholders, which are compelled to work in concert. Stakeholders are in essence
the groups necessary for the continuation of operations but also the groups which stand to gain
or lose from such operations (Brealey & Myers, 2003). The conflict between these groups can
largely be summarized as a conflict of interest, where interest and actions of groups are not
clearly aligned. Thereby, expectations, gain and losses are not commonly shared among the
dispersed factions.

       Jensen and Meckling (1976) examined the conflict between managers and shareholders
and subsequently argued for means of solving what is known as the agency problem. It is defined
as “a contract under which one or more persons [principal(s)] engage another person [the agent]
to perform some service on their behalf, which involves delegating some decision making
authority to the agent”. They conclude that the conflict arises due to differences in how
maximization of wellbeing is achieved: Shareholders strive to increase company value while
managers aim to increase their own wealth and influence while minimizing effort. Several theories
have surfaced, both normative and descriptive in form, to explain the conflict and its resolution.
These include tournament theory (Lazear & Rosen, 1981), equity theory (Gerhart, Minkoff &
Olsen, 2005) and agency theory (Jensen & Meckling, 1976).

        The apparent solution in many of these theories revolves around aligning the interest of
the two groups, where much of the debate is centered on compensation as a means of achieving
this alignment. Compensation is theorized to accomplish this by creating incentive schemes for
managers which to some extent mirror company performance and thereby shareholder wellbeing,
leading to actions which are consequently profitable for both interested parties. The relevance of
the subject continues to be high, as both economic theory and empirical studies continuously
shows that incentive pay contributes to firm performance. Studies which find such a positive
relationship are for example Murphy (2002).

       However, in both practice and theory the determinants of executive compensation are
still debated, this has led to both practitioners and theoreticians suggesting relationships between
executive compensation and other factors. One such factor is institutional investor ownership.
This factor is of special interest partly due to the large influence wielded by institutional investors
but also due to its relatively clear-cut definability and measurability, as shown by the forthcoming
methodology and data analysis.

                                                                                           8
       The weight of compensation and more specifically incentive schemes has through
academia and practitioners been heralded as one of the tools with which to solve the agency
conflict. The work of incentivizing and thereby closing the gap in disparate interest has fielded
exciting and occasionally self-evident results.

       Incentive effects have been shown, as one would expect, to differ across different
compensation schemes (Holmstrom, 1979; Shavell, 1979), some academics even argue that it is
not a matter of how much but of how a company pays its executive which relates to increased firm
performance (Jensen & Murphy 1990). Furthermore, fund managers have publically pushed for
more active incentives programs linked to corporate performance (Smith, 1996; Gillian & Starks,
2000). These two seemingly connected phenomenon, incentive effects and institutional investor
owners as proponents of incentivizing compensation structures, thereby becomes an engaging
topic of study.

       A previous study was conducted on the subject of institutional investor ownership and
executive compensation in 2003 (Hartzell & Starks). The study sets out to determine the
relationship between executive compensation and institutional investor ownership by examining
the top five executives in each holding. The data gathered is consequently regressed on the
institutional investor ownership in said company. A host of control variables are used as to
disentangle the effect. They find that executive compensation and institutional investor
ownership are indeed related. Their study concludes that pay-for-performance is positively
correlated and the level of compensation is negatively correlated to a measure of institutional
investor ownership. The alternate explanation that both effects arise simultaneously and
endogenously is discarded after examining the timing of the effects and conducting an
instrumental variables analysis. It is thereby decisively concluded that a relationship exist where
changes in institutional investor ownership leads to subsequent changes in executive
compensation, as was hypothesized.

       Executive compensation has undergone great changes during the last four decades. From
the 1970s and onward the compensation and its diverse elements have increased. In
chronological order these included seemingly precipitous increases to perquisites, parachutes,
option awards and awards of restricted stock (Murphy, 2011). Furthermore, the expansion of
executive compensation was considerably larger than the increases to real wages of the average
production worker. The ratio of pay increased from 2,5 in 1970 to nearly 90 in 2000, and to a
ratio of 500 if stock options are included (Bureau of Labor Statistics n.d.). Simultaneously there

                                                                                           9
was a great increase in institutional investor ownership where the percentage of institutionally
controlled equity soared.

       These facts conspire to constantly change the landscape in which investors and firms
conduct corporate governance, and set executive compensation schemes, as changes in the macro
undoubtedly pressures individual firms to adapt. The relationship between institutional investors
and executive compensation has in this regard not been studied since the beginning of the
century when Hartzell and Starks conducted their study, more than ten years ago using data from
more than 20 years ago. During this time we have seen globalization pick up its pace,
extraordinary growth in the developing world as well as an unprecedented financial crisis. The
prevailing theories and data are outdated though a theoretical framework exists on which the
forth-coming analysis will start and conceivably generate thought-provoking conclusions and
become a fruitful part of academia's current understanding of the subject.

3. DATA AND METHODOLOGY

3.1. Datasets and variables
The data used in this study consist of 62.215 observations of executive compensation spanning
13 years (2001-2013) in the U.S. There is consequently data for circa 1500 companies per year
during the period. The sample was selected by virtue of its extensiveness; a byproduct of this
selection came to be an apparent compatibility of variables necessary for analysis. Additional
efforts into data collection would only marginally increase significance of results due to the
already sizable sample. The sample was deemed sufficient and such efforts were consequently
disregarded. America as a base for the study was chosen considering the size and regulations of
its financial market, assuring both extensiveness and availability of data.

    This paper will throughout focus on two different measures of incentive compensation:

    - Incentive compensation level
    - Compensation structure

Incentive compensation level is the absolute amount of incentive compensation paid out to an
executive during a year. Compensation structure is the fraction of total compensation that is
incentivized and will henceforth be referenced to as simply compensation structure. It is
calculated as incentive compensation level divided by the total compensation paid out during a
period.

COMPUSTAT database. The dataset contains information on several measures of compensation
in U.S. companies quoted by S&P. For each company, compensation data is available for up to
five top executives. Where data on five executives are available they are also utilized, where fewer
are reported, as many as possible are used. Figure 1 presents the distribution of number of
reported executive per company.

3.1.2. Institutional investor ownership
Data on size and value of holdings are based on institutional investors’ 13-f filings and are
collected from the Thomson Reuters database where these are collapsed into one dataset. 13-f
filings is a form that every investor in the U.S. that exercises investment discretion over $100
million or more in securities uses to report on its holdings to the SEC. As a consequence, the
dataset only consists of investors that are likely to be sophisticated and powerful enough to have
actual influence over their holdings. The filings include, for example, number of shares held,
value of the shares held, and a Manager ID identifying the manager responsible for the particular
holding. In essence, the 13-f filings provide vast possibilities to calculate institutional investors’
wealth and potential influence over their holdings.

       For each company in the sample, two groups of institutional investors are analyzed: the
top five largest institutional investor owners and total institutional investor owners. The rationale
is that owners controlling a non-negligible fraction of a company, compared to smaller owners,
will be more prone to affect and influence said company and hence its compensation structure,
be they institutional investors or not.

3.1.3. Additional data
Additional data consist of stock information such as share prices, monthly returns as well as
shares outstanding and common shares turnover. This data is collected from S&P’s
COMPUSTAT where monthly information on all stocks in the S&P indices is available. The data
is collapsed from monthly to annual data to make it compatible and functional in the subsequent
regressions.

        Shareholder wealth (or shareholder value) is a crucial driver for incentive compensation.
It is defined as the sum of all management decisions during a year, i.e. its ability to increase
earnings, dividends and cash-flow. It can include all types of strategic decisions, both long-term
and short-term as well as official decisions and informal ones. Subsequently, it is in fact a
qualitative measure; it can only be approximated with absolute quantitative values. In a perfect,
frictionless market, shareholder wealth equals the share price plus paid out dividends. This would
be the effect of all available information regarding the company and management decisions being
reflected in valuation. However, due to inefficiencies in the market share price can only
approximate shareholder wealth in a quantitative model. In this study it is defined as the return
during a year on the market value on the outstanding equity in the beginning of the year.

3.3. Methodology

3.3.1. Tobit model explained
To analyze the relationship between institutional investor ownership and executive
compensation, a Tobit model is employed. The model builds on the assumption that an

                                                              ∗
observable variable 𝑦𝑖 is equal to an unobservable variable 𝑦𝑖 in this way:

                                                ∗      ∗
                                                                    𝑦𝑖   𝑖𝑓 𝑦𝑖  > 0
                                           𝑦𝑖 = {           ∗
                                               0 𝑖𝑓 𝑦𝑖 ≤ 0

The latent variable is linearly dependent on an independent variable or vector 𝑥𝑖 via a parameter
β. The model also includes an error term that is normally distributed, with the intention of
capturing random influences on the relationship. The model can be written as follows:

                                  ∗                           2
                                            𝑦𝑖  = 𝛽𝑥𝑖 + 𝑢𝑖, 𝑢𝑖 ~ 𝑁(0, 𝜎  )

Tobit models allows for the possibility of censoring the dependent variable. Variations of the
Tobit model can be produced by changing how input data is censored. These different methods
of censoring values are named Type I, II, etc. The procedure to use censoring and Tobit models
is fairly common in econometrics where Type I Tobit and Type II Tobit are the most frequently
used.

3.3.2. The model used
The model used in this study is a Type I Tobit with a lower censoring limit at the standard value
of zero. Using zero as the censoring level is done for two primary reasons:

    - Some companies do not employ incentive compensation programs at all, making the data
        incomplete at points with e.g. missing values. By having a lower censoring level at zero,
        all missing values will be treated as zeroes.
    - The model is based on pay-per-performance with change in shareholder wealth and
       institutional investors’ ownership as the main variables. While change in shareholder
        value can be both negative and positive, such is not the case with incentive compensation
       – or any type of compensation. Therefore, the Tobit model, specifically with zero as the
        censoring level, is used to ensure that the practical relationship is not underestimated if
        there are negative values in independent variables.

The model is constructed as follows:

The model will examine both level of incentive compensation and compensation structure, i.e.
incentive compensation as a fraction of total compensation. Both measures will be tested to
check for institutional investor influence on the size of incentive compensation and potential
biases with regard to incentive compensation.

        Institutional investor ownership as a percentage of the total amount of shares outstanding
is the key factor with which a statistically significant relationship is sought, but more factors are
needed for a complete analysis. Change in shareholder value is the most intuitive driver of
incentive compensation levels as the common expression pay-for-performance is derived from
this relationship. Therefore, both the present and last year’s actual firm performance are included
in the model. The reason for using the previous year’s ownership statistics is that the goal is to
see how ownership affects the compensation by setting up rules and practices for the coming
year, rather than introducing them in, for example, the middle of the year. Control variables are
market capitalization and Tobin’s Q due to their effect on firm, and management, performance.
In likeness to the ownership variable, control variables are lagged since incentive compensation is
awarded based on previous performance, not on ongoing or expected performance. The model
initially assumes homoskedasticity but subsequently test the strength of the assumption through
statistical analysis.

       In previous studies (Hartzell & Starks 2003) a similar model was employed to evaluate
institutional influence on executive compensation. They utilized an option sensitivity measure as
the dependent compensation variable instead of a more complete analysis utilizing absolute levels
of compensation and incentives as a fraction of total compensation as in this study. Not only
option awards but several types of compensation are being considered, which is why a broader
definition of incentive compensation is used. Also, Hartzell and Starks used data from the 1990’s
when incentive compensation was neither as widely used nor as diverse a concept. Furthermore,
they used a CEO dummy variable as a control variable which in this study is omitted. This is
done as it falls outside the scope of the study. Executives’ positions are not relevant as the

                                                                                          21
objective is to look at executive compensation and incentive compensation, separating and
controlling for different corporate positions is redundant.

3.3.3. Interval regression
An interval regression is a type of regression where the actual values of the data to be regressed is
not observed. Instead, intervals are observed where the actual values are not, which is not
completely unlike the dataset used in this study. A Tobit regression calculates betas fitted to
actual values when only values within one interval are observed, an interval regression does the
same but assumes all data is split up in several intervals. Technically, an interval regression can be
done on a single interval, which is the intended use in this study. In this case, the two intervals are
below and above zero. An interval regression is mainly used to mimic the Tobit regression with
the aim to check for consistent results regardless of whether the dataset is homo- or
heteroskedastic.

3.3.4. Instrumental variables regression and causality analysis
The instrumental variables method is used to estimate causal relationships when controlled
experiments are not feasible or when a treatment is not successfully delivered to every unit in a
research study. When having a model as the one presented above in section 3.3.1., an
instrumental variable is one that is (1) independent of the error term 𝑢𝑖, (2) not independent of

                          ∗
𝑥𝑖 and (3) not affecting 𝑦𝑖 or 𝑦𝑖 if 𝑥𝑖 is held constant. In this study, share turnover is used as an
instrumental variable for institutional investor ownership. The intuition behind this is that studies
(Bennett, J., Sias, R. & Starks L., 2003) show that there is a causal relationship between turnover
and institutional investor ownership, while there is no reason to believe turnover has any effect
on incentive compensation levels or structures.

       This study uses a two-stage least-squares (2SLS) instrumental variables approach. The
method is based on the following two stages:

    1. Regress 𝑋 on the instrument 𝑍 to obtain 𝑋̂.
    2. Regress 𝑌 on 𝑋̂ with an error term which is a constant consisting of either omitted
        variables or unquantifiable factors which could be considered “noise”.

To check for abnormal or unexpected casual relationships, institutional investor ownership is
regressed on lagged incentive compensation levels and on lagged compensation structure.

4.3.2. Causality
To check for the direction of causality, two more simplified regressions are made. These are
simple models where a reverse relationship, compared to the actual analysis, is examined. In these
regressions institutional investor ownership is regressed on lagged incentive compensation levels
in the first and on lagged compensation structure in the second. The regressions are made in
order to assure that institutional investor ownership does in fact cause the incentive
compensation levels and structures and not the reverse. The lagged regressors assure causality
since it would be technically impossible for incentive compensation levels one year to affect the

                                                                                          29
institutional investor ownership level of the previous year. These tests are made to check for a
completely reversed relationship (lagged compensation and institutional investor ownership)
rather than in order to verify the validity of the previous models and regressions.

        The results show that there is no statistically significant relationship of lagged incentive
compensation levels on institutional investor ownership but there is one between lagged structure
and institutional investor ownership. The results are shown in Appendix 23 and Appendix 25
with complementary regressions that prove a relationship the other way around in Appendix 22
and Appendix 24.

                                                                                                                                          2
       These models are evaluated with simplistic standard OLS-regressions and the low R
show that they are insufficient as models to explain the regressed variables. They do however
contribute to the aggregate proficiency of the study as they do not find evidence of an outright
reversed relationship

4.4. Development during the 2000s
After concluding that institutional investors have a positive influence on incentive compensation
levels and the structure of compensation, the next step is to evaluate how this phenomenon has
developed over the course of the studied period. The point is to test the hypothesis of an
increasing trend and changes occurring due to the financial crisis. By performing a Tobit
regression with year dummies for each year and their interaction with the institutional investor
ownership that year, the effect institutional investors have on incentive compensation levels and
structure in firms per year is obtained. With this, a revised model is used in the following analysis,

The influence of institutional investor ownership on incentive compensation has been established
in the first Tobit regression (Table 5) and it has been confirmed that the relationship has
increased during the last ten years. There seems to be a drastic regime shift between 2005 and
2007 after which the relationship is fairly steady. This shows with a high degree of certainty that

the result of this preference in practice is yet to be determined.

4.5. Summary of results
It is apparent that institutional investors do have a significant influence on executive
compensation levels and structure. All regressions show significant, positive relationships
between the magnitudes of institutional investor ownership and incentive compensation,
considering both compensation levels and the structure.

                                                                                          31
        The level of incentive compensation is the strongest correlated with institutional investor
ownership. This signifies that a company that is to a large extent owned by institutional investors
has larger incentive compensation payouts. However, it does not signify a potential bias towards
incentive compensation over other forms of compensation. In theory it is conceivable that
institutional investors are correlated with higher compensation levels over all, e.g. higher
incentives but also higher direct compensation. So this finding is not relevant unaccompanied.

        By also including the findings of a positive relationship between the fractions of total
compensation made up of incentive compensation, and institutional investor ownership, a more
interesting and holistic result is achieved. It demonstrates that institutional investors prefer
incentive compensation over other forms of compensation. Together, the two results paint a
statistically significant picture of the relationship between incentive compensation and
institutional investors.

       The results are complicated by the supporting analysis due to its ambiguity. The
instrumental variables regression shows that, with annual share turnover as an instrumental
variable, institutional investors are causal drivers of both compensation levels and structures.
However, the simple regressions show that a reversed relationship likely exists where
compensation structure also affects institutional investor ownership.

        Furthermore, the developments of the relationships are in line with each other and they
are statistically significant at a 1% level. It has been established that a regime shift occurred right
before the financial crisis which will be discussed further in the conclusion and implications
section (5.2. and 5.3.).

5. IMPLICATIONS AND CONCLUSION

5.1. Institutional investors influence executive compensation
The statistical analysis has hitherto demonstrated with a high degree of certainty that there exists
a relationship between institutional investors and executive compensation. The mechanism under
which this influence operates in not yet wholly identified however and is not expanded upon in a
meaningful way in this study. A mix of factors, both direct and indirect through clientele effects,
seems reasonable explanations.

        While it is clear from the results that a large institutional investor ownership percentage is
statistically indicative of high future levels of incentive compensation and a more incentive based
structure, it also suggest that institutional investors prefer to own companies with incentive based
compensation structures. This effect is identified through a non-negligible positive effect on
institutional investor ownership caused by compensation structure. Interesting to notice is that
no such causal relationship exists for compensation levels yet both are significantly influence by
institutional investors.

        This lends weight to the provided hypothesis of institutional investors’ influence, both
directly through ownership and reversely, as shown by the causality regressions through clientele
effects. If the notion of incentive compensation proficiency has permeated institutional investors’
strategies and calculations it is reasonable to find the prevailing relationships. Institutional
investors value both existent incentive compensation schemes and the possibility of enacting
them themselves. Instead of making the results less important, it alters and expands the
implications of the results while demonstrating the veracity of the hypothesis.

       Consequently, it conforms to the lack of evidence found for the claim that compensation
levels attracts institutional investors. As the regressions showed, the statistical significance of the
effect of changes in shareholder wealth, i.e. firm performance, is higher for incentive
compensation levels than for structure. This implies that institutional investors prefer
investments in firms with certain compensation structures, while levels are no such driver. The
intuition is simple: institutional investors do not necessarily favor short term performance of
which high incentive compensation levels is a likely indicator.

5.2. The influence has increased over time
The paper failed to identify a trend in the manner hypothesized. A long-term persistent increase
possibly driven by increased efficiency and effectiveness of monitoring and capability of correctly
align interests with incentive compensation as a tool failed to materialize. Adjusting the scope to

                                                                                          33
encompass a longer period of time could possibly more appropriately test the hypothesis as the
current time-period proved insufficient to identify any long-term trends. The identified
development is instead a regime shift in promoted executive compensation schemes by
institutional investors in the year 2006. This dominating change becomes a natural focal point for
discussion of results and implications.

        While earlier papers (e.g. Hartzell & Starks 2003) identified a positive effect on incentive
compensation brought on by institutional investors, they have not taken the development over
time into consideration. As the results show, the coefficients, most notably the impact from
institutional investors on incentive compensation, range from negative to positive with the
mentioned regime shift in 2006. Examining the total influence during the whole period, the
results could have a low predictor value as negative coefficients may partially cancel out positive
ones. The total effect of institutional investors on executive compensation is thereby exposed to
a risk of underestimation if time is not taken into consideration. Without regressing correctly on
the entire time-period, this effect is overlooked and the result may be downwardly skewed.

        Correcting for time and investigating trends and trend-breaking tendencies is reasonable
as institutional investors are unlikely to be static in their proposed stratagems with regard to
executive compensation. Instead the effect is during some periods positive and in others
negative. Therefore, to predict the influence and effect institutional investor ownership has on
future compensation schemes, e.g. the coming five to ten years, the results from the simple Tobit
regression (Table 5) is possibly a lacking foundation. Attempting a prediction utilizing Figure 6 is
preferable as coefficients before 2006 are fairly insignificant when predicting a short future
beyond 2013.

        The fact that the coefficient is increasing dramatically finds a reasonable basis in the
descriptive statistics. Institutional investor ownership has been somewhat stable throughout the
whole period studied, while incentive compensation has increased both in level and in relative
size compared to direct and other compensation. The descriptive statistics do not assure an
increased relationship between the two, but they do provide robust information about low levels
of incentive compensation before 2006.

        Incentive compensation has increased only during the period when institutional investors
had a positive impact on incentives. As can be seen in the descriptive statistics, the level of
incentive compensation has increased since 2006 and the compensation structure has become
more incentive based. This further strengthens the hypothesis that institutional investors favor
incentives since the positive impact on incentives combined with the rising levels strongly suggest

                                                                                          34
a continuing up-going tend. Since the effect institutional investors have on incentives remains the
same, this could possibly be interpreted as a derivative of the development of incentive based
structures and levels.

       The main type of incentive compensation during the past years, and the one increasing
fastest, is stock awards. By combining this with the main findings from the regressions, it is
implied that institutional investors have an increasing bias with respect to stock. This was not
explicitly tested in the thesis but is a likely implications and an interesting subject for further
study.

5.3. The financial crisis lacks explanatory power
The hypothesis with regard to changes in relation to the financial crisis proved faulty. Mounting
political and social pressure during and in the aftermath of the financial crisis led to no
discernable changes in executive compensation driven by institutional investors. Other factors
than those hypothesized are possibly at work. Conceivably they are both tangible and intangible
in nature e.g. due to macroeconomic factors, theoretical findings and/or herd-like behavior. The
paper does not encompass the necessary investigative study to expose the inner workings of
institutional investors shaping compensation policy. Neither explanations for changing
coefficients, i.e. influence, nor lack thereof are attempted.

        The regime shift of 2006 is also visible in the descriptive statistics section of the paper,
Figure 2, where incentive compensation increases drastically from 2006 and onward. It is
conceivable that the increase of the median by $600.000 is connected to the relationship
previously discerned. The subsequent increases, 2007 to 2013, where the incentive compensation
median increased by approximately an additional $600.000 are maybe linked to other ownership
blocks later pursuing and advocating similar executive compensation schemes. The interactions
and influence diverse owners have with regard to issues of corporate governance would be an
interesting topic of study: Do owners typically acts as counter-weights or do they influence each
other to draw the same conclusions exacerbating already prevalent sentiments?

5.4. Generalization and implications

                                                                         th
This thesis was conducted on U.S. companies during the 21  century. For a long time, the U.S.
has been the corporate big brother both with regard the financial market and economic theory
development, which makes it a good fit for financial research. However, the sample does pose
problems with respect to generalization.

                                                                                          35
       Firstly, the sample companies are all quoted companies. This limits the certainty with
which the results are transferrable to unquoted companies, but does not assert that it is not
possible. The results support the hypothesis that institutional investors prefer incentive
compensation schemes over direct compensation schemes coupled with the main goal of all
investors is firm performance of their holdings leads to interesting inferences. It is reasonable to
claim that institutional investors assume a connection between incentive compensation schemes
and firm performance, i.e. portfolio growth. The results are therefore likely transferrable to
unquoted companies despite the lack of statistical backing.

       Secondly, due to globalization, with American institutional investors being active in
markets world-wide, it is reasonable to claim that the conclusions should be applicable outside of
the U.S.. Furthermore, since the 13-f filings apply to all large investors active in the U.S., they
include foreign investment companies, further strengthening the argument in favor of geographic
generalization.

       Finally, this study has examined institutional investors’ attitudes towards incentive
compensation, with the intention of using institutional investors as a proxy for all investors.
Institutional investors are used since they are strong owners with the ability to implement their
preferences. Also, they are likely in the forefront of potential changes in attitude, e.g. the regime
shift in 2006. Therefore, the results could with fairly high probability be applied to other
investors. As previously mentioned, further research focusing on the relationship, influence and
interactions of diverse groups would undoubtedly prove intriguing.

5.5. Final words
The study demonstrates that there is a positive effect from institutional investors on both levels
of incentive compensation and compensation structure. The size of the effect has increased

                  st
during the 21  century with a drastic regime shift between 2005 and 2006 with the relationship
shifting from negative to positive. Since then, the influence of institutional investors has been
fairly steady, showing no signs of changing during the financial crisis. The study also indicates
that institutional investors prefer to purchase firm that have already implemented incentivized
compensation structures, but leaves it to future studies to further examine the subject.

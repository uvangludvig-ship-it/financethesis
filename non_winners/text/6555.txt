Debt Refinancing and Cross-Market Spillovers  into Credit Spreads

Abstract:

   This paper examines whether the timing of corporate debt maturities influences
   credit spread pricing and the transmission of refinancing stress across markets. Using
   U.S. firm-level data from 2002 to 2024, we study how refinancing intensity (RI), the
   share  of debt maturing within three years, affects credit default swap (CDS) and
   corporate bond spreads. RI commands a significant premium in CDS markets once
   firm characteristics are controlled for, yet its influence on bond spreads is negligible.
   RI also fails to predict future changes in credit spreads, indicating that rollover
   exposure is priced only when conditions deteriorate. Residual refinancing risk factors
   that remove size, value, and bond-market liquidity show that refinancing stress
   originates in CDS  markets and later reaches bonds, while equity signals are short-
   lived. Our results identify refinancing stress as a credit-market state variable, most
   clearly reflected in CDS spreads and only weakly visible in bond spreads or equity
   returns.

Keywords:

   Refinancing risk,  Debt maturity  structure,  Credit default swaps,  Corporate bond
   spreads, Cross-market spillovers

I    Introduction

The onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in March 2020 revealed how corporate debt structures
expose firms to sudden funding disruptions. As global credit markets froze and liquidity
conditions deteriorated, many firms faced surging credit risk premia despite stable operating
performance. Figure 1 illustrates that average CDS spreads and average corporate bond
spreads rose sharply during this period and co-moved in earlier episodes of financial
stress such as the global financial crisis and the eurozone debt crisis. This co-movement
suggests that shifts in funding conditions a!ect multiple credit instruments simultaneously,
indicating that refinancing pressures are not confined to a single segment of the credit
market.

From a corporate finance perspective, issuing short-term debt can reduce borrowing costs
and create flexibility in capital structure decisions. It exposes firms to refinancing risk
because liabilities must be rolled over more frequently.   When a substantial share of
outstanding debt nears maturity, firms become particularly sensitive to changes in credit
conditions.   Investors may require compensation for bearing the risk that refinancing
occurs when liquidity weakens or credit spreads widen.  Friewald, Nagler and Wagner
(2022) formalise this mechanism and show that firms with higher refinancing intensity

                                      2
(RI), defined as the share of total debt maturing within three years, earn higher equity
returns. Their results suggest that RI captures a dimension of risk that is distinct from
overall leverage and not fully reflected in traditional measures of financial distress.
   In this paper, we extend the work of Friewald et al. (2022) in two ways. First, we
broaden the empirical setting by focusing on corporate credit markets rather than equities,
thereby shifting the lens from realised compensation to the forward-looking pricing of
RI. Credit spreads incorporate refinancing risk at the point of funding, making them
the relevant ex ante measure for identifying whether rollover exposure is priced before
conditions deteriorate. Second, we examine whether RI behaves as a distinct component of
credit risk premia that remains after controlling for standard firm attributes, and whether
this refinancing-related component persists across instruments. We address three central
research questions:

(i) Do firms with higher RI exhibit wider credit spreads once observable firm characteristics
are controlled for?

(ii) Does RI predict subsequent changes in credit spreads, indicating that credit markets
anticipate rollover risk?

(iii) Does RI function as an independent state variable whose innovations transmit across
markets?

Recent research addresses parts of these questions but leaves two critical issues unresolved.
Li, Wang, Wu, and Zhang (2023) show that firms with high RI earn higher realised bond
returns, demonstrating that rollover pressure is compensated ex post once refinancing
constraints materialise. Liu, Qiu, and Wang (2021) document that firms with significant
near-term debt relative to cash experienced pronounced increases in CDS spreads during the
COVID-19 crisis, indicating that RI can be priced ex ante under stressed credit conditions.
These studies establish that RI and related rollover measures can a!ect credit premia
when refinancing needs become material, but they do not show that RI is systematically
embedded in credit spreads under non-stressed conditions or that refinancing-related
pricing operates as an independent and persistent component of credit premia across
instruments.
   These distinctions have material implications. If RI raises credit risk premia today
rather than only when refinancing costs spike, firms face incentives to maintain smoother
maturity structures. They also have reasons to avoid concentrated rollover profiles and to
preserve liquidity bu!ers that mitigate refinancing shocks. If RI behaves as an independent
state variable, refinancing stress, defined as the component of credit spreads associated
with RI after removing characteristic-driven variation, is not only a firm-specific attribute.
It also functions as a systematic pricing force that shapes funding costs across credit
markets. Determining whether RI carries such characteristics is therefore essential for
understanding how maturity structure influences the cost of capital and how refinancing

                                      3
shocks propagate.
   To address these questions, we construct a comprehensive dataset of U.S. non-financial
firms with traded 5-year CDS contracts, measured by par-mid spreads (henceforth "CDS
spreads"), and matched corporate bonds, where bond spreads are defined relative to
maturity-matched U.S. Treasury yields. First, we analyse how refinancing intensity relates
to both the levels and the changes in CDS and bond spreads. Second, we isolate refinancing-
related pricing components by removing variation attributable to firm size, leverage, value
characteristics and bond-market illiquidity. The resulting residual refinancing risk (RRI)
factors allow us to assess whether refinancing exposure contains independent pricing
information and whether related shocks propagate across markets.
   Our findings show that RI is priced in CDS spreads only after controlling for observable
firm characteristics, while its e!ect on bond spreads is small and statistically insignificant.
RI does not predict short horizon changes in credit spreads, indicating that rollover
exposure is incorporated contemporaneously rather than forecasted in advance. Finally,
innovations in RRI factors originate primarily in CDS markets and transmit to bond
markets, consistent with the view that refinancing stress behaves as an independent state
variable within credit markets.
   The paper proceeds as follows: Section II outlines the theoretical foundations, reviews
related literature and positions our contribution.  Section III details the data sources,
variable construction and sample filtering.  Section IV presents the empirical analysis,
including the pricing tests for RI and the construction of residual risk factors, and the
subsequent spillover analysis. Section V reports robustness checks. Section VI concludes
and discusses implications for credit pricing and corporate financing decisions.
II    Theoretical Background and Related Lit-
    erature

The maturity structure of corporate debt determines when firms must confront credit
market conditions. Rather than holding a single long-dated liability, firms issue debt with
varying maturities, which creates refinancing needs at particular points in time. If a large
share of this debt falls due when credit spreads widen or liquidity tightens, refinancing
costs can rise sharply despite stable fundamentals. This exposure reflects the timing of
debt rollover, not the overall level of indebtedness.
   Traditional capital structure theory, beginning with Modigliani and Miller (1958), treats
leverage as a static aggregate and consequently overlooks di!erences in when firms must
refinance. Two firms may have identical leverage but face distinct risks if one relies on
short maturities while the other does not. Maturity composition therefore introduces an
intertemporal element that leverage alone cannot capture.

                                      4
   This distinction aligns with structural credit models. In Merton’s (1974) framework, debt
values depend on asset volatility and the schedule of liabilities. Firms with concentrated
short-term maturities are more sensitive to adverse market shocks because they must
re-enter funding markets more frequently. Debt maturity therefore acts as a separate state
variable in the analysis of credit risk.
   Friewald, Nagler, and Wagner (2022) provide one of the most comprehensive treatments
of this idea. They decompose total leverage into short-term and long-term components and
introduce refinancing intensity (RI), defined as the share of total debt maturing within three
years. Firms with high RI must roll over debt more often, exposing them to funding shocks
when market conditions deteriorate. Because firms actively choose their maturity profiles,
RI may also reflect managerial expectations about future financing conditions rather
than a purely exogenous risk exposure. Friewald et al. (2022) suggest two counteracting
channels. Short-term debt o!ers flexibility and can discipline management, yet it increases
vulnerability to sudden tightening in credit conditions. Their empirical results show that
the risk component dominates. Firms with high RI earn higher expected stock returns.
This suggests that equity investors require compensation for bearing refinancing risk, and
it positions RI as a distinct priced factor in equity markets.
   He and Xiong (2012) provide a complementary theoretical channel that places refinancing
conditions at the centre of credit risk. In their framework, creditors may decline to roll
over maturing debt when funding markets tighten, even if the firm remains economically
solvent. Default risk therefore arises not solely from deteriorating firm value but from
the interaction between short-term liabilities and market liquidity. RI thus becomes a
direct determinant of credit risk premia, and the pricing of debt instruments reflects both
solvency considerations and the prospect of future rollover constraints.
   These observations align with established empirical patterns in credit spreads. Elton,
Gruber, Agrawal and Mann (2001) show that corporate bond yields contain sizeable
components that are not explained by expected default losses. Collin Dufresne, Goldstein
and Martin (2001) find that most time series variation in spreads is driven by a single
latent factor that standard credit risk fundamentals cannot account for. Refinancing needs
concentrate debt maturities at precisely the moments when liquidity conditions tighten,
and they provide a credible channel through which this residual variation may arise. Short
maturity structures compress refinancing decisions into narrow intervals. They leave firms
exposed to adverse movements in credit conditions regardless of their ongoing profitability.
Understanding where this refinancing channel is reflected, and whether it is priced in
advance or only upon realisation, requires separating the mechanisms embedded in spreads
from those observed in returns.
   The di!erence between ex ante pricing and ex post realisation clarifies why RI appears
di!erently across measures. Credit spreads represent the premium investors require upfront
for bearing rollover exposure. Realised returns compensate investors only when refinancing

                                      5
costs actually materialise. Li, Wang, Wu, and Zhang (2023) show that firms with high
RI earn higher bond returns. Whether a similar premium is embedded directly in credit
spreads remains less explored. Liu, Qiu, and Wang (2021) examine the COVID-19 crisis
and show that firms with a large share of near-term debt relative to cash experienced
larger increases in credit default swap (CDS) spreads when funding markets tightened.
Rollover pressure can therefore be priced immediately under stress. These results highlight
that refinancing risk may a!ect credit premia when refinancing needs become material,
but they do not establish that RI systematically predicts the evolution of credit spreads in
normal conditions.
  Market structure further shapes how RI is incorporated across instruments.  CDS
contracts trade in liquid markets and o!er direct protection against credit events. They
therefore adjust quickly when perceptions of refinancing pressure change.   Corporate
bond spreads embed both credit and liquidity premia, which depend on trading frictions.
Longsta!, Mithal, and Neis (2005) show that non-default components form a substantial
part of bond spreads. Information unrelated to fundamental default risk can therefore
a!ect bond pricing and obscure signals that pass through CDS markets more clearly.
Norden and Weber (2009) show that equity markets adjust first, CDS follows shortly
after, and bond markets react with a lag. This sequence implies that refinancing-related
information should appear most strongly in CDS spreads and only later in bonds. These
dynamics clarify why RI is more visible in some instruments than others, but they do not
show whether the refinancing channel can be detected within spreads themselves.
   Identifying this channel is challenging. Credit spreads combine several priced compo-
nents that can mask refinancing e!ects. Larger and more profitable firms typically have
lower spreads because they are perceived as safer borrowers, regardless of when their debt
matures. Expected returns reflect realised compensation for risk and are less influenced by
these additional components. This makes refinancing e!ects more visible in return based
tests. Kozak, Nagel and Santosh (2020) argue that asset pricing tests should first remove
characteristic driven variation before assessing whether an additional variable contains
independent pricing information. Applying this logic to credit markets requires stripping
away the firm level characteristics and pricing components that systematically influence
spreads. Only then can RI be examined as a distinct source of variation. The resulting
residual is an empirical proxy rather than a perfectly isolated refinancing factor, but it
sharpens identification.
   Although RI is recognised as a priced component in equity markets, its role in credit
markets remains largely unexplored.   Existing evidence shows that rollover exposure
a!ects realised bond returns and that credit spreads embed variation unrelated to default
fundamentals.  Prior work has not shown whether RI is priced in the cross section of
credit spreads, whether it influences their short horizon dynamics outside crisis periods, or
whether refinancing related information transmits across financial markets. To address this

                                      6
gap, we examine whether RI is reflected in credit spreads once standard firm characteristics
are controlled for.   We also study whether RI predicts changes in credit premia and
whether a refinancing related pricing component persists and propagates across markets
once characteristic driven variation has been removed. This approach allows us to assess
whether refinancing risk operates as an independent mechanism in credit pricing rather
than a feature confined to equity returns or crisis periods.
III    Data

We construct two firm-level datasets that merge equity data with credit default swap (CDS)
spreads and with corporate bond spreads at the monthly frequency. Accounting variables
come from Compustat North America. Stock returns, prices, and shares outstanding are
obtained from CRSP. Corporate bond trades and bond characteristics, including coupon
rate, maturity date, issue size, and TRACE credit grades, are drawn from FINRA TRACE.
Single-name CDS spreads come from Refinitiv via LSEG Workspace. Constant-maturity
Treasury yields used to construct bond spreads are sourced from the Federal Reserve H.15
release. Monthly risk-free rates and Fama-French factor returns are retrieved from the
Kenneth French Data Library. The CDS-equity sample spans December 2004 to December
2024, and the bond-equity sample spans July 2002 to December 2024.
   Both datasets are aligned at the monthly frequency. Following Friewald, Nagler, and
Wagner (2022), accounting figures are matched to monthly stock returns using a six-month
reporting lag. Financial statements for the fiscal year ending in December 2020 therefore
enter the sample from July 2021. This lag ensures that the accounting information reflects
data available to investors.
   The sample contains common stocks listed on NYSE, NASDAQ, and AMEX, identified
using CRSP share codes 10 or 11 and exchange codes 1, 2, or 3. Financial firms (SIC
6000-6999) are excluded.  Observations with non-positive total assets, book equity, or
market equity are removed, and firms must have positive leverage. The construction of
the debt-maturity variables follows Friewald et al. (2022). Missing short-term debt due
within one year (DD1) and long-term debt (DLTT) are set to zero. For the intermediate
maturity buckets DD2 to DD5, which record debt maturing in years two to five, missing
entries are set to zero if at least one value in the sequence is reported. Observations with
negative debt values are removed. Total debt defined as DD1 + DLTT must not exceed
total assets. Long-term debt must be at least as large as the sum of debt maturing in years
two to five (DD2 + DD3 + DD4 + DD5). Refinancing intensity (RI) follows Friewald et
al. (2022) and measures the share of total debt maturing within three years:

steps ensure that bond prices reflect actual trading behaviour rather than microstructure
noise.

reflected in wider spreads. Firm size and profitability, by contrast, are negatively correlated
with credit spreads. Larger and more profitable firms are perceived as safer borrowers and
typically refinance under more favourable conditions, consistent with their lower spread
levels.   These relationships follow the idea that stronger fundamentals ease financing
constraints and reduce the compensation required for bearing credit risk.
   CDS and bond spreads exhibit a high correlation of 0.832, indicating that both in-
struments reflect similar assessments of underlying credit risk. The correlation between
their monthly changes is lower at 0.538, suggesting that the two markets do not adjust
contemporaneously to new information. Periods with rising credit premia coincide with
weaker equity performance, as shown by the negative correlations between excess equity
returns and changes in credit spreads (-0.466 for CDS and -0.396 for bonds). This pattern
indicates that equity and credit markets react asymmetrically to shifts in perceived cor-
porate risk. When investors revise a firm’s outlook downward, equity prices fall because
shareholders absorb residual downside risk, whereas credit spreads widen as lenders require
greater compensation for elevated default risk.
   Additional descriptive statistics for the merged CDS-bond-equity sample are presented
in Table A.I. This integrated panel contains all firm-month observations common to both
markets and forms the basis for the spillover analysis. The distributional properties resemble
those in Table I, confirming that the merged dataset maintains the key characteristics of
the underlying samples.
IV   Empirical analysis

The empirical analysis proceeds in four steps. First, we examine whether firms with higher
refinancing intensity (RI) are associated with wider credit spreads once standard firm
characteristics such as leverage, size, and profitability are controlled for. Second, we test
whether RI contains predictive power for subsequent changes in credit spreads, which
allows us to assess whether RI is reflected contemporaneously or anticipated by credit
markets.  Third, we isolate the refinancing risk component of pricing by constructing
residual refinancing risk (RRI) factors. Finally, we examine whether shocks to the RRI
factor originate in one market and transmit to others.

once di!erences in fundamentals have been accounted for. This reversal follows from the

insure against credit deterioration, so protection costs rise when short-dated liabilities
increase the likelihood that refinancing needs coincide with adverse market conditions.
Firm characteristics associated with strong funding capacity, such as size or profitability,
can mitigate these concerns and suppress the unconditional relation between RI and CDS
spreads. Once these mitigating influences are controlled for, RI captures rollover exposure
that is no longer tempered by firm strength and therefore enters as a priced component of
credit protection.
   The bond spread regressions yield a di!erent outcome.  RI loads negatively in the
univariate specification, but the e!ect disappears once firm characteristics are added, and
the coe"cients turn positive, small, and insignificant. Leverage, by contrast, remains large
and stable across all specifications. This result di!ers from the findings of Friewald, Nagler,
and Wagner (2022) and Li, Wang, Wu, and Zhang (2023), who do not document leverage
as a priced factor in equity or bond returns. In the context of spread determination, this
pattern indicates that the cross-section of yields is shaped by the total scale of outstanding
liabilities rather than by the timing at which these liabilities fall due.   The level of
indebtedness influences the perceived likelihood that a firm may be unable to honour its
fixed commitments, whereas the maturity structure determines when such pressures may
surface. The interaction terms with illiquidity and maturity are also economically small
and insignificant, so neither trading frictions nor the refinancing horizon strengthens the
pricing of RI in bonds.
   The contrast with Li et al. (2023) concerns the nature of the pricing channel. Their study
shows that firms with higher RI earn higher realised bond returns. The di!erence arises
because returns capture compensation that materialises over the holding period, whereas
spreads reflect the cost of debt at issuance or secondary-market trade. A characteristic
may therefore yield a return premium ex post without commanding an ex-ante spread
premium if markets adjust to refinancing pressures only once they occur. The two findings
are thus complementary: RI generates compensation when rollover costs emerge, but bond
spreads do not incorporate this risk ex ante because the market recognises it only when
conditions deteriorate.

pricing intuition. Larger and more profitable firms exhibit narrower spreads, reflecting a

Securities of weaker credit quality or inferior liquidity command higher premia, as investors
require compensation for holding instruments that are more di"cult to trade or more

similar relations in their study of Israeli corporate bonds. Although market structures di!er,
the directional agreement across settings suggests that spreads incorporate information
about firms’ solvency and the frictions associated with trading their debt, even if magnitudes

  C. Isolating refinancing stress from firm characteristics

The previous results show that RI is priced in CDS spreads once firm characteristics
are controlled for, whereas the corresponding e!ect in corporate bond spreads remains
economically small and statistically weak. Because credit spreads combine several priced
components related to firm size, value, liquidity, and other dimensions of credit risk,
the refinancing signal may be obscured even when RI is economically relevant.  This
creates the need to separate characteristic driven components from spreads and returns to
isolate a residual refinancing metric that can reveal whether refinancing stress operates
as a distinct state variable. Refinancing stress denotes the component of credit spreads
attributable to refinancing pressure after characteristic driven variation has been removed.
Establishing such a measure is essential for the subsequent spillover analysis, since cross-

                                      21
market transmission can only be identified once this common co-movement has been
filtered out.
   To address this, we adopt the characteristics-first framework of Kozak, Nagel, and
Santosh (2020), combined with the view of RI as a separate channel of fragility proposed
by He and Xiong (2012). The objective is not to construct a tradable factor but to extract
a residual refinancing metric that captures the component of pricing that aligns with
refinancing pressure once unrelated pricing influences have been accounted for. However,
the residual may still reflect latent credit-quality variation, so it should be viewed as
consistent with refinancing stress rather than a definitive measure of it.
   The rationale for this construction can be illustrated by considering two firms that
are similar along priced characteristics such as size, value, and, for bonds, liquidity, yet
di!er in the share of debt maturing in the near–term. If refinancing stress operates as an
independent source of pricing variation, the firm with greater rollover exposure should
exhibit distinctive residual pricing outcomes after adjusting for these characteristics. We
therefore construct residual refinancing risk (RRI) factors for CDS spreads, bond spreads,
and equity excess returns. These factors isolate the pricing component associated with
RI after removing systematic variation attributable to firm characteristics, providing a
clearer view of how refinancing conditions are reflected in market valuations.

  D. The cross-market dynamics of refinancing stress

The previous section constructed residual refinancing risk (RRI) factors for equities,
CDS, and bonds after removing variation linked to firm size, value, and bond-market
liquidity. The results revealed a clear divergence. The CDS-based RRI displays pronounced
persistence, the bond-based RRI moves in the same direction but with lower intensity, and
the equity RRI is e!ectively serially uncorrelated and behaves like noise. Only the credit
market measures therefore exhibit the temporal structure required of a state variable
capable of transmitting shocks.   Equity RRI is nevertheless retained to test whether
refinancing related information first appears in equity markets before it is reflected in
credit spreads, which aligns with documented information hierarchies across asset classes
(Norden and Weber, 2009).
   Having established that refinancing stress persists in credit markets but not in equities,
we next examine whether refinancing pressures in one market a!ect the others over time.
This matters because pricing in levels does not show whether refinancing shocks remain
isolated or transmit across markets. If RI functions as a state variable, shocks should
spread from the markets where they first appear. Studying this transmission allows us to
link the cross-sectional pricing results to how refinancing conditions influence the evolution
of credit premia.
   Accordingly, we estimate a vector-autoregression (VAR) in which each RRI series
depends on its own lags and on the lagged values of the others. Unit-root tests confirm
that the series do not contain stochastic trends. Augmented-Dickey-Fuller (1979) tests
reject the presence of unit roots, while KPSS (1992) statistics reflect the high persistence of
the CDS- and bond-based measures rather than evidence of explosive behaviour. The equity
RRI is strictly stationary. Although the credit market RRIs are highly persistent, their
autoregressive coe"cients remain below unity (Table A.IV), which makes them suitable for
estimation in levels. Because the factors di!er in scale, all series are standardised to mean

                                      27
zero and unit variance. Lag length is selected using the Bayesian Information Criterion
(Schwarz, 1978), which favours a VAR(1) specification (Table A.V). Orthogonal shocks are
identified using a recursive Cholesky ordering Equity  →  CDS  →  Bond, which aligns with
evidence that information is incorporated first in equities, then in CDS markets, and only
subsequently in bonds (Norden and Weber, 2009).

bonds, while equities exert a direct but more limited influence on bond market refinancing
spreads. This directional structure matters because it shows that refinancing stress arises
where refinancing risk is priced most explicitly and only later a!ects borrowing costs
through bond spreads.   It therefore links the cross-sectional pricing evidence to how
refinancing conditions propagate across credit markets rather than remaining confined to
one instrument.

VI   Conclusion

This paper set out to examine whether refinancing intensity (RI), defined as the share of
debt maturing within three years, is reflected in credit default swap (CDS) spreads and
corporate bond spreads, and whether refinancing stress propagate across markets. The
motivation comes from the observation that funding disruptions often coincide with sharp
increases in both CDS spreads and bond spreads, as seen during the COVID-19 shock
and earlier episodes of market stress. These periods show that the timing of debt rollover,
rather than aggregate leverage alone, can amplify firms’ exposure to adverse financing
conditions and shape the cost at which they access credit.
   Our empirical evidence shows that RI is priced in CDS markets once firm characteristics
are controlled for, yet it carries no robust pricing e!ect in corporate bond spreads. CDS
spreads increase for firms with more concentrated near-term maturities even after adjusting
for leverage, size and profitability.  Bond spreads, by contrast, remain shaped by the
scale of total indebtedness. The economic magnitudes confirm that RI is a secondary
determinant of CDS spreads. Its e!ect is modest relative to leverage and book-to-market,
but su"ciently persistent to merit attention. RI does not predict short horizon changes
in either CDS spreads or bond spreads. This suggests that credit markets incorporate
rollover exposure contemporaneously rather than anticipating it. RI therefore behaves like
a static characteristic in cross-sectional pricing rather than a forward-looking indicator of
spread dynamics.
   The construction of residual refinancing risk (RRI) factors sharpens this distinction.
Once size, value and, for bonds, liquidity premia are removed, firms with high RI display
consistently higher residual CDS spreads but only weakly higher residual bond spreads.
The equity based residual factor contains no persistent refinancing signal.  The credit
market residuals allow us to examine how refinancing stress evolves across instruments.
Shocks originate in CDS markets, where refinancing conditions are priced most directly,
and later transmit to bond markets, where trading frictions slow adjustment.  Equity
markets contribute little to the propagation of refinancing related shocks once characteristic
driven variation has been removed. This hierarchy indicates that refinancing stress behaves
as a pricing force specific to credit markets rather than a broad risk factor visible across

                                      34
asset classes.
   Future work could explore whether RI becomes more salient in credit markets with
di!erent liquidity structures or regulatory environments, and whether stronger interaction
with firms’ liquidity bu!ers alters the pricing of RI. Such extensions would clarify whether
the patterns observed here are general or specific to the U.S. credit market setting and
the period studied.
   These findings give rise to concrete implications for firms and investors. The structure
of debt maturities determines how exposed firms become when funding conditions shift,
and refinancing pressure materialises only when credit markets reflect higher protection
premia, rather than in anticipation of such revisions.  Since neither CDS spreads nor
bond spreads embed refinancing risk ahead of stress, firms cannot rely on price signals
that support orderly adjustment of maturity profiles. The sequence in which refinancing
stress appears across credit instruments reinforces this constraint. CDS markets register
refinancing strain first, and this pressure later di!uses into bond valuations. As a result,
the information set available to managers compresses precisely when refinancing becomes
more expensive. Firms that rely on short-term liabilities must therefore smooth maturities
and maintain liquidity bu!ers well before conditions deteriorate, because once stress
propagates through credit markets, refinancing premia rise quickly and the opportunity to
reshape debt structures diminishes at the moment flexibility is most valuable.

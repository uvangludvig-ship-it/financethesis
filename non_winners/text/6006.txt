Outperforming S&P 500: The power of using earnings quality in fundamental
stock selection

Abstract:

   The purpose of this paper is to examine the relationship between quality of earnings
   and stock returns, by measuring whether higher (lower) earnings quality corresponds
   with a higher (lower) stock return, focusing specifically on the constituents of S&P
   500. To assess the potential of each individual stock, we construct a Quality-of-
   Earnings (QoE) Score composed of five variables. Through portfolio regressions
   based on 20 years of historical data, this non-experimental, observational study finds
   abnormal returns ranging between 4.3-5.2% annually depending on the update
   frequency (annual, three-year, or five-year) of the QoE Score portfolios. We also find
   that one of the variables itself, the Margin Score portfolio, finds alpha of 12.6-14.6%
   annually, depending on the update frequency. Finally, we conclude that all QoE
   Score portfolios, regardless of the update frequency, outperform the S&P 500 in
   terms of both absolute and risk-adjusted returns.

                                    1. Introduction

With the help of earnings management, firms can apply accounting techniques to mislead
investors, by exhibiting earnings figures that are manipulated and inflated to different
extents. Typically, this is where earnings quality comes in – the degree to which the
earnings have been created by increasing revenues or decreasing costs, as opposed to the
mere use of accounting tricks. Bhojraj, Hribar, Picconi & McInnis (2009) prove that firms
with low quality of earnings, that intentionally resort to earnings management to beat or
meet analyst forecasts, attain a stronger stock performance in the short term, than those
missing the forecasts, however, this is not a persistent effect. After merely 1.5 years, the
authors find that earnings quality becomes more important for long-term stock
performance than whether the forecasts were met or missed. This is consistent with the
research on earnings quality in firms involved in initial public offerings. It was found with
statistical significance that IPO firms with a conservative use of earnings management
and accounting practices, and thus with a high quality of earnings, outperform their
counterparts engaging in aggressive use of earnings management over the long run (Teoh,
Welch & Wong, 1998).
       But how does all this affect stock performance and stock selection from a portfolio
manager’s perspective? In this paper, we aim to provide guidance on how the previous
findings in this field can be used in a stock-picking model to find better value investments.
Through our constructed Quality-of-Earnings score we grade every stock listed on S&P
500 in terms of their underlying earnings quality and subsequently test different portfolios
constructed with the QoE Score as our anchor. This yields the following research
questions that we attempt to answer in this paper:

   1. Are stocks with higher earnings quality outperforming the S&P 500 and can
       abnormal returns be observed?
    2. Can quality of earnings be used as a stock selection metric to systematically
       obtain a higher return than that of S&P 500?

As suggested by the discounted cash flow valuation technique, future cash flows are
strong determinants for today’s value of a security. We believe that earnings quality
therefore could be a good indication of the magnitude of future cash flows. The higher
the earnings quality, the more certain the current earnings are to persist or increase. Also,
due to the pureness aspect of earnings quality, that is, how close the earnings are to the
actual cash flows derived from the business activities, a high earnings quality indicates
that reported earnings correspond well with the cash flowing into the entity. Thus, it
seems that high earnings quality indicates future cash flows, with less risk of not
occurring, as opposed to low earnings quality, which rather indicates that even though
future cash flows are coming, there is much uncertainty with regards to their timing and
size. Our hypothesis is therefore that firms displaying a high QoE Score should be better
long-term investments than firms with low QoE Scores, which corresponds with the
empirical results of the aforementioned papers that this paper builds on.
       This paper starts by outlining the existing research and findings on this rather
broad topic through a breakdown of seven papers pertinent to earnings quality and the

variables included in the QoE Score. The following section dives deeper into the variables
themselves, their construction, as well as their relevance for this setting. We also present
our intended methodology including regression equations and summary statistics for our
data set. Thereafter follows a presentation of 13 tables exhibiting the outputs from our
tests, from which we subsequently draw our conclusions.
       We found that a portfolio created by our QoE Score finds alpha of 5.2%, 4.3%
and 4.6% annually for an annual, three-year, and five-year update frequency respectively,
all at the same significance level, corresponding to p<1%. Surprisingly, we found the
greatest alpha across all variable portfolios, including the QoE Score portfolio itself, to
be in the five-year updated Margin Score portfolio of 14.4% annually at p<0.1%
significance level. Once lagging effects are considered, there is an even greater alpha in
the Margin Score portfolio of 14.6% annually (p<1%) for year two and three in the three-
year updated portfolio, adjusted for the effects from the first year. When we tested the
performance of our QoE score on our dataset, we found that non-investment quintile
portfolios created with our score outperforms the market portfolio of the S&P 500; the
QoE portfolios, regardless of whether one uses the annual, three- or five-year updates,
yield a higher return than the S&P 500 portfolio with a greater Sharpe ratio.
       As a final remark, this study intends to contribute to the existing research by
combining findings from current papers, mainly about earnings management, into one
common measure, namely the QoE Score, which is supposed to capture as broad a part
as possible of the earnings quality aspects in firm fundamentals. Many variables in our
QoE Score, e.g. Revenue Consistency, Cash Flow Matching and Non-Recurring Items,
are constructed with close links to existing papers, as is our methodology from which we
measure the final results. Hence, with this study, we take a wide stance on the earnings-
quality perspective through a combination of existing knowledge mixed with our own
knowledge and experience within this domain.

                                 2. Literature Review

Since a generally accepted formula or definition for quality of earnings does not exist, we
have considered a multitude of papers with links to the topic, to see different approaches
one can take in the construction of a quality-of-earnings score. Furthermore, due to the
apparently close relationship between earnings quality and earnings management, we
have considered multiple papers about the latter too, which have been important to get an
idea of which variables to include in the quality-of-earnings score. Lastly, we have singled
out the variables that were the most frequently occurring and the most significant and
read research papers only examining those variables in isolation, to get more expertise on
the micro level. A majority of the papers included in the literature review section link
quality of earnings, earnings management, or the variable(s) with the market activity of
the firms’ shares, which is relevant to our topic and hence has provided us with useful
insights that will be built upon in this paper.
             In their research, Bhojraj et al. (2009) provides empirical evidence that
firms using earnings management to beat analyst forecasts have a strong short-term stock
performance in comparison with firms missing the forecast targets. However, this effect
subsides and after 1.5 years, such firms yield on average the same return as firms missing
the forecast targets but have high earnings quality. Thereafter, a strong divergence
between the two types of firms is observed, such that the firms with high earnings quality
perform significantly better than those with low earnings quality, even if the latter beats
the forecast targets in the first place. Other firm characteristics were also noted with a
connection to earnings quality. Firstly, the paper concludes that poor earnings quality is
synonymous with a higher likelihood of issuing equity in the future, regardless of whether
one compares with high earnings-quality firms that either miss or beat analysts’ forecasts.
Hence, earnings quality - not whether one beats or misses the forecasts - seem to be the
determinant for future equity issuance according to the authors. Furthermore, they also
find that the same holds for insider sales so that poor earnings quality increases the
likelihood of insiders selling their shares. We think that this is an interesting and useful
finding to consider in our thesis. As insiders in a firm know the most about the company
due to their access to both private and public information, they should have the best
possibility among all stakeholders to predict the firm’s future. A clear relationship
between poor earnings quality and insider sales infers that earnings quality might
correlate with the future stock value, which is exactly the research purpose of this thesis.
              Abdelghany (2006) acknowledges that an agreed-upon definition of quality
of earnings does not exist, however, due to the strong link between earnings management
and earnings quality, there are three basic pillars that may outline the degree of earnings
management and hence earnings quality in a firm. Firstly, the variability of earnings,
where a high variability in operating income is synonymous with low earnings quality.
Secondly, the model used in the paper considers a so-called “earning surprise indicator”
followed by the last parameter, namely the ratio of cash flow from operations to net
income. High variability in earnings quality was found across different industries, with
firms in the mining, gas and oil industry having the highest share of firms with high
earnings quality, while banks, insurance and investment companies had the highest share
of low-quality firms. It was concluded that the three parameters might be inapplicable to
some industries and that the latter industries may require other earnings-quality
measurements.

                                   4
       Non-recurring items exemplify business accounts that may inflate or deflate sales
or costs temporarily, and hence might contribute to low earnings quality. It was found
that analysts tend to revise their earnings forecast of a firm upwards as a result of non-
recurring gains but leave forecasts unchanged for non-recurring losses. However, the
misuse of non-recurring gains to inflate the earnings numbers was a reason for analysts
to revise forecasts downwards; the rationale is that experienced analysts see through such
manipulation and suspect that bad times lie ahead, incentivizing them to revise the
forecasted earnings downwards. The most interesting and crucial non-recurring items for
analysts were found to be those that imply changes in the underlying business scope, such
as profits or losses from restructurings (Li, Su, Dong & Shu, 2018).
            By constructing the ESCORE measuring the degree of earnings
management in a firm, Nguyen, Iqbal & Shiwakoti (2022) examined whether a zero-
investment portfolio of long positions in the firms displaying the lowest ESCORE,
financed by shorting the firms with the highest ESCORE, exhibits abnormal returns. After
adjustments, such a portfolio displayed 1.37% abnormal returns per month, resulting in
an annual abnormal return of 17.74%. Hence, it turns out that a conservative (aggressive)
earnings management practice is correlated with a strong (poor) stock performance. Even
tendencies of the firm fundamentals were discovered with regards to the degree of
earnings management. Firstly, firms with a low ESCORE were much more profitable than
firms with high ESCORES, shown by their return on assets as well as change in return on
assets. The low ESCORE firms further exhibited a lower Z-Score than their counterparts
indicating that they were less financially distressed than the high ESCORE firms. Lastly,
the authors found that firms with low ESCORE were less likely than firms with high
ESCORE to issue new equity and/or debt, which might be connected to the observation
about the higher Z-Score and thus greater financial distress such firms experience.
            Ali and Bansal (2021) go one step further in examining the impact of
earnings management on stock returns, by considering the directions of earnings
management. This dichotomy consists of positive earnings management, that is, using
earnings management to inflate earnings and put the company in a better light, and
negative earnings management, that is, applying earnings-management techniques to
smoothen out the earnings over time (e.g. cookie-jar accounting or life-cycle accounting).
They found that firms using discretionary accruals to inflate their earnings (positive
earnings management) had lower excess returns compared to firms using primarily
negative discretionary accruals (negative earnings management). The authors conclude
that the discount rate is the underlying reason for this; negatively managed earnings are
perceived by investors as a riskful element, so such firms are discounted at a higher
discount rate than firms using positive earnings management, which should be perceived
as a less riskful element in comparison. In particular, overvalued stocks were engaged in
upwards earnings management. The authors suggest that the overvaluation makes the firm
sensitive to price fluctuations, so such firms must signal stable and high levels of
profitability to sustain their high value, which explains the phenomenon. In conclusion,
the study suggests that the lower (higher) the degree of earnings management in a firm,
the higher (lower) its stock returns. In addition, the direction of earnings management
turned out to hold significance in all tests, with firms engaging in negative earnings
management receiving a premium.
       The importance of meeting analyst expectations with regard to the direct effect on
the share price development has also been studied. Significantly greater abnormal returns
were observed for firms managing to meet their earnings expectations while controlling

                                   5
for the current year’s earnings information (Kasznik & McNichols, 2002). Consistency
turned out to be paramount in this context too, as firms meeting expectations only in the
recent 1-2 years only receive a valuation close to what their fundamentals would suggest.
From the fundamental point of view, the study found that firms meeting their earnings
expectations had on average higher earnings during the subsequent three years compared
to the firms failing to meet their earnings expectations. The market seems to reward this,
as a market premium could be observed in firms meeting earnings expectations. The
premium increased the higher the frequency of meeting the earnings expectations during
the past three fiscal years; meeting expectations for all three preceding years resulted in
a greater market premium than if expectations were met during two of the three preceding
years et cetera. This market premium was of a persistent character and was not reversed
within the coming 12-36 months, ruling out the possibility of the premium being a
temporary overvaluation. The final finding that we want to highlight from Kasznik &
McNichols’ study is the negative market effect observed for firms that previously met
expectations, but underperformed the expectations for the current fiscal year. Kasznik &
McNichols conclude that this finding infers riskiness with earnings manipulation and
misleading investors and that such practices become costly over time; manipulating
today’s accruals to make earnings appear in a better light will postpone the
underperformance until when such accruals must be reversed, which makes it even harder
to meet the expectations for the year of reversal.
        Low earnings quality entails risk for investors and debtors, which is shown in the
relation between accruals and the cost of equity and cost of debt (Francis, LaFond, Olsson
& Schipper, 2005). The study divides accruals into innate accruals and discretionary
accruals, where the former are accruals that arise inevitably from the business model and
the latter are accruals that managers have more freedom and control over. Thus, the
discretionary accruals are those that would be used for earnings management and hence
contribute to an intentionally lowered earnings quality. However, the study finds that the
quality of the discretionary component of accruals has a smaller pricing effect than the
innate part. The reason is that the discretionary accruals decrease the information
uncertainty as they give a hint about where the company is heading based on how
managers use them, while the innate accruals inevitably appear due to the operational
nature, which does not communicate as much to investors. This means that the quality of
innate accruals also has a greater impact on the cost of capital than the quality of
discretionary accruals. The study concludes that firms with a high aggregate quality of
accruals, that is, both discretionary and innate, have lower capital costs for both equity
and debt. In a comparison between the highest and the lowest accruals-quality firms, the
high-quality firms enjoyed on average a 126 basis points lower cost of debt and a 210
basis points lower cost of equity than the firms with poor accruals quality. In addition,
the top quintile firms had on average a 0.35 lower equity beta than the bottom quintile.
The authors then comment that the economic fundamentals, meaning the business model
and the operating environment, has a significant influence on the total accruals quality.
Some firms will therefore naturally have less accruals than others and hence a lower
information risk. The risk itself comes from the unrealized nature of accruals; they are
business transactions that are recorded as income or costs without any cash inflows or
outflows, making them uncertain. The judgement and estimating part of accruals is also
contributing to the risk element, as opposed to the non-accruals transactions that are
realized instantly through a cash inflow or outflow. For those reasons, the findings in the
study make sense – firms with low accruals quality increases the risk for stakeholders,

                                   6
resulting in a higher required return from the firm to compensate them sufficiently, which
explains the higher cost of equity and debt capital such firms have.
        The current papers on the topics will all be useful in our research about the impact
of quality of earnings on stock returns. Bhojraj et al. (2009) helps provide an idea of the
time horizon on which the earnings quality and stock return correlation becomes
noticeable. In addition, their contribution of the equity-issuance behavior and insider
share management suggest that poor earnings quality is linked to future poor
fundamentals, which we will use in our QoE Score model when selecting appropriate
variables to test. Abdelghany (2006) shows more specifically which variables that might
be included in such a model and offers inspiration to the construction of it. Some of our
variables, namely the Revenue Consistency, Non-Recurring Items and Cash Flow
Matching variables, are very similar to Abdelghany’s variables and have only been
slightly adjusted – something that Abdelghany encourages if the goal is to capture a
broader range of industries. Li et al. (2018) helped us understand the role of non-recurring
items and their direct impact on earnings quality as well as analyst forecasts, which laid
the foundation for the non-recurring items variable used in our QoE Score. Their
discussion of the underlying reasons for their observations will be considered in our own
interpretation of the non-recurring item variable output. We use the findings of Nguyen
et al. (2022) to obtain an understanding of the mere practice of earnings management and
its effect on the share price. In addition, we value their findings on aggressive earnings
management correlating with financial distress. Their research has been valuable in
constructing the Margin Score and Investment variables in our model. From Ali et al.
(2021), we learn that earnings management should not be generalized into one practice,
but rather that it is a dichotomy with negative earnings management being worse and
riskier perceived by investors than is positive earnings management. This adds a new
perspective in the interpretation of our empirical results. So does Kasznik & McNichols
(2002) which gets more focused around the market and its reaction to meeting or missing
expectations, which increases our understanding of the importance of meeting analyst
forecasts from a market point of view, and why earnings management is only a short-term
and very risky solution to that – also vital in the subsequent interpretations of our
empirical results. Finally, we expand our knowledge of the risk perspective with the
insights from Francis et al. (2005) which complements the former paper very well, while
simultaneously emphasizing the perspective of debtors. In addition, their findings about
accruals, their quality and their influence on the share price, added insights to the
construction of the cash matching variable and the interpretation of its outcome. All in
all, the seven papers together provide useful insights about the quality of earnings
relationship with share prices, both on a macro level, e.g. Bhojraj et al. (2009) and on a
micro level, e.g. Li et al. (2018).

   A. Model and Components of Quality of Earnings

To measure the earnings quality for a firm listed on the S&P 500, this paper applies a
model that builds on models from other papers, specified in the Literature Review section.
We have subsequently altered the model in some respects to make it, what we believe,
more precise and less directed towards earnings management and more towards earnings
quality. For example, Bhojraj et al. (2009) measure accruals by dividing earnings by cash
flow from operations and since we agree that it is the most efficient way of doing it, we
decided to use the same variable to capture accruals. However, their model grades three
earnings-management indicators on a scale 0-3 – total accruals, discretionary accruals,
and discretionary expenditure cuts – but this paper uses a weighted percentage score for
a more precise assessment of each individual firm. The weights are equal for all variables,
i.e. 20% weight on each individual variable, spread across five components.

A positive revenue change between the fiscal years, with little volatility, infers a business
with stability and relatively low operational risk. In the Revenue Consistency variable,
we create a measurement for this. The numerator consists of the average year-on-year
revenue change over the past five years and the denominator is the standard deviation of
the revenue change. Hence, firms with steadily increasing revenue are the most rewarded,
while the opposite holds for firms with a negative revenue trend with high volatility, as
suggested by Abdelghany’s (2006) research. This captures parts of the earnings-quality
concept; essentially, high earnings quality comes from (1) operationally increased
revenue, (2) cost reductions or (3) both of the aforementioned. Therefore, this variable
becomes essential as it assesses the first of the three means. However, the drawback of
the component is that increased revenue is not equivalent to high earnings quality per se,
after all, the revenue can be of a non-recurring nature, accruals, or inflated through
earnings management techniques, which the subsequent variables in our QoE Score deal
with.

The purpose of the Cash Flow Matching component is to explain the relationship between
earnings reported and the actual cash flows generated from operating activities. Cash
Flow Matching helps identify whether the earnings reported by a company are supported

                                   8
by cash, which is a more concrete measure of financial health than earnings alone. In
order to be awarded a high Cash Flow Matching score, earnings should be closely
followed by corresponding operating cash flows. If a company report increasing earnings
but does not show comparable increases in cash flow from operations, it might indicate
accounting practices or earnings management, thus lowering the earnings quality, as
supported by Abdelghany (2006).
       The formula for Cash Flow Matching involves the correlation between EBIT and
Operating Cash Flow, as well as Revenue and Operating Cash Flow, over a five-year
period. By taking the average of these correlations, the model assesses how well the cash
flow movements match the reported earnings and revenue figures. A high correlation
suggests that the reported earnings are well supported by actual cash flows, indicating
high earnings quality. This component thus acts as a safeguard against the manipulation
of earnings through accruals or other accounting techniques, providing a more nuanced
view of a company's financial stability and operational efficiency.

Increasing margins is another indication of increasing earnings quality - at least if it
occurs in a continuous fashion and without significant deviations over time. An increasing

margin in the numerator. This means that the variable rewards firms with a high current
EBIT margin of a non-volatile character. Hence, firms with only temporary high margins
will not reach as high margin scores as the firms that manage to sustain a high EBIT
margin over time.

Non-recurring items are essential in the assessment of earnings quality. If a firm has high
income, but the underlying business transactions show that this income is made up of
temporary items (e.g. capital gains, provision reversals or currency exchange gains),
earnings quality is low, as this type of income is highly unlikely to be continuous. This is
confirmed in the paper by Li et al. (2018) finding that experienced financial analysts see
through obvious attempts to use non-recurring items for earnings management purposes,
which incentivizes them to lower their forecasts accordingly.
       On the contrary, if the operational costs (here defined as gross profit minus EBIT)
are high, but a significant share of them consist of costs that are non-recurring (e.g.
lawsuit costs, write-downs, and provisions), this should be positive for the earnings

                                   9
quality, as it indicates that the operating costs are normally lower when the non-recurring
costs are absent. Therefore, we have constructed a formula that computes the mean of (1)
the inverse of the share of non-recurring income and (2) the share of non-recurring
operating costs. For a detailed explanation of the data included in non-recurring income
and non-recurring costs, please refer to the data section of this paper.

The investment aspect in a firm would be relevant to consider for two reasons from an
earnings-quality perspective: Successful investments into the operating infrastructure will
facilitate cost cutting for the firm (e.g. through economies of scale or scope, or through
improved resource efficiency), and in some ways facilitate increasing the revenue too
(e.g. enabling a higher production output to meet high demand), which in turn increases
the earnings quality. Furthermore, we ask ourselves: Do earnings matter if the cash flow
they generate result in inefficient use of earnings? Say a firm has high earnings quality
and generates a cash flow from operations near its earnings amount, but that cash flow is
wasted on poor investment decisions. In that case, the cash flow does not generate any
value to the firm nor to its shareholders, and the share price is likely going to reflect this
inefficiency. Therefore, we include the investment variable in our Quality-of-Earnings
Score to assess whether the part of the operating cash flow that is not attributed to debtors
or equity owners is used efficiently. The score is constructed in the following way: Firstly,
the numerator considers the change in gross property, plant and equipment which is
equivalent to the investment during the year. In the denominator, we put together all the
cash outstanding, meaning the opening cash balance, the cash flow generated from
operations and the cash flow from debtors or equity holders. After seeing how much of
the cash that is allocated to investments, we take that ratio and multiply it with the firm’s
return on assets to get an idea of how much the investment will contribute to the next
year’s EBIT. To summarize the formula, we derive the share of the current year’s cash
holdings that will, on average, be contributing to the next year’s EBIT through
investments - the higher the better.

   B. Data

With the aforementioned model in mind, firm fundamental data needed to be acquired in
order to test the hypothesis. As the paper intended to test said hypothesis on the S&P 500,
the initial data needed was the S&P 500 constituents for the last 20 years (2004-2023).
This data was acquired through S&P 500 CapitalIQ, where the latest index constituents
and recent changes to the index could be found (S&P CapitalIQ, 2024). To find the index
constituents for prior years, a code in R was constructed to add/remove tickers based on
recent changes. This allowed us to create lists of tickers that built up the S&P 500 for the
last 20 years.
        Then, financial data for said firms in the S&P 500 was collected by creating a list
of all unique tickers in the 20-year index constituents’ data and uploading it to Compustat.
In Compustat, several data points for each stock ticker were collected (in the given time
frame) and downloaded. The collected data points included revenues, earnings, and more,
which can be seen in Appendix 1 (Compustat, 2024).

                                  10
        With the index constituents and all financial data downloaded for the tickers, the
only data remaining was the S&P 500 returns for the last 20 years, Small-minus-Big
excess return, High-minus-Low, and the risk-free rate (3-month T-bill rate). Said data
was collected from Fama & French Data Library (Fama & French, 2024) and was used
as the benchmark return which the QoE Score portfolios were subsequently regressed
against.

   C. QoE Component Calculations and Portfolio Construction

The majority of the portfolio construction and the regressions were made in R Studio.
Firstly, the data collected, as described in 3.2, was imported into R. The code used to
acquire the index constituents of the S&P 500 for each year between 2004 and 2023 (as
discussed in 3.2) was the first step in building later portfolios. To be able to create
portfolios based on the firm's quality of earnings, a function calculating each component
of quality of earnings was created. The function’s inputs are the company tickers, the
financial data acquired in 3.2, and a list of the years that are sought after (2004-2023).
The function calculates the Revenue Consistency, Cash Flow Matching, Margin Score,
Non-Recurring Items, Investment, and Quality of Earnings Score for each ticker and each
year. Each component is calculated in accordance with the formulas written out in 3.1.
The quality of earnings function then returns scores of said six components in a data
frame, for all the company tickers and all the years that are tested. Furthermore, each
component of the quality of earnings score cannot be lower than -200, or higher than 200.
As most components are in the range between -50, and 100, this constriction was added
to avoid outliers in the components that could skew the results. The data frame also has
a column of the stock return for said ticker and year, which is important for later steps.
       With all components calculated and stored in a data frame, sub-data frames for
each year can be created by filtering the original data frame by the index list for a given
year, and the return for another given year. This means that a data frame consisting of all
QoE Score components and returns for all tickers in the S&P 500 index for e.g. year 2012
can be created. This also means that the returns of the said data frame are not restricted
to the returns of 2012, but one can create a data frame based on the S&P 500 2012 index
but with returns for 2015 and so on. By not limiting the different sub-data frames to the
index constituents, one can create portfolios that are updated every 3 or 5 years too,
instead of being limited to annual changes. From this, 15 data frames are created, one for
each year between 2009 and 2023 using said filter function with the two inputs, the index,
and the year. The reason that the first year that is used is 2009 and not 2004, is because
some of the components of the QoE, such as Revenue Consistency, require at least 5 years
of data to return an output value. This limits the portfolios to companies that have been
traded publicly for at least 5 years. The analysis will be based on 3 different portfolio
strategies; one where the portfolio is updated every year, one that is updated every third
year, and one that is updated every fifth year.
       Regardless of which strategy is used, each portfolio consists of five different
components, which will be used as sub-portfolios. To arrive at each sub-portfolio, taking
Revenue Consistency as an example, each data frame for each year is sorted based on the
chosen component score, in this case, Revenue Consistency. Then, the function goes long
in the 20% of companies with the highest Revenue Consistency scores, and short in the
lowest 20% of Revenue Consistency scores. The function does this for all years using the

                                  11
15 data frames previously discussed. This method is based on the Fama French 3-factor
model. The process is repeated for all five components, meaning that 5 sub-portfolios are
created. These five sub-portfolios can be used to invest in each of the five components.
Using the five sub-portfolios, an ultimate QoE Score portfolio consisting of all sub-
portfolios is created based on equal weights, i.e. 20% assigned to each component, further
explained in 3.4.
       Conclusively, a QoE Score portfolio consisting of the five sub-portfolios based
on the different criteria that make up high-quality earnings is created, and its returns are
recorded in a final data frame that is regressed against the market benchmark S&P 500.
Furthermore, as three major portfolio strategies are tested, four QoE Score portfolios have
been created in total, and 20 sub-portfolios in total.

   D. Ordinary Least Squares Regressions

Starting with the univariate regressions, each sub-portfolio and QoE Score portfolio was
regressed using Ordinary Least Squares Regression against the market return (S&P 500)
in excess of the risk-free rate. The linear regressions were based on the following
equation:

                 𝑌𝑖 = 𝛽0 + 𝛽1 × 𝑋1 + 𝛽2 × 𝑋2 + ⋯ + 𝛽𝑛 × 𝑋𝑛 + 𝜀𝑖 (7)

With the general formula in mind, the univariate formulas were simply derived by only
using one beta in each formula, meaning that the six formulas (for each strategy, i.e. 1-
year, 3-year, and 5-year) were made. These formulas are in accordance with CAPM. The
formulas are:
                     𝑌𝑅𝑒𝑣𝑒𝑛𝑢𝑒 𝐶𝑜𝑛𝑠𝑖𝑠𝑡𝑒𝑛𝑐𝑦 = 𝛼𝐶 + 𝛽𝑀𝐾𝑇 × 𝑋𝑀𝐾𝑇 + 𝜀𝐶 (8)

                   𝑌𝐶𝑎𝑠ℎ 𝐹𝑙𝑜𝑤 𝑀𝑎𝑡𝑐ℎ𝑖𝑛𝑔 = 𝛼𝐶𝐹𝑀 + 𝛽𝑀𝐾𝑇 × 𝑋𝑀𝐾𝑇 + 𝜀𝐶𝐹𝑀 (9)

                      𝑌𝑀𝑎𝑟𝑔𝑖𝑛 𝑆𝑐𝑜𝑟𝑒 = 𝛼𝑀𝑆 + 𝛽𝑀𝐾𝑇 × 𝑋𝑀𝐾𝑇 + 𝜀𝑀𝑆 (10)

                         𝑌𝐼𝑛𝑣𝑒𝑠𝑡𝑚𝑒𝑛𝑡 = 𝛼𝐼 + 𝛽𝑀𝐾𝑇 × 𝑋𝑀𝐾𝑇 + 𝜀𝐼 (12)

To also regress the components using Fama French 3-Factor model, the following

       𝑌𝑅𝑒𝑣𝑒𝑛𝑢𝑒 𝐶𝑜𝑛𝑠𝑖𝑠𝑡𝑒𝑛𝑐𝑦 = 𝛼𝐶 + 𝛽𝑀𝐾𝑇 × 𝑋𝑀𝐾𝑇 + 𝛽𝑆𝑀𝐵 × 𝑋𝑆𝑀𝐵 + 𝛽𝐻𝑀𝐿 × 𝑋𝐻𝑀𝐿 + 𝜀𝐶 (14)

All regressions were like the portfolios, made in R. For example, the sub-portfolio
Revenue Consistency with the updated annual strategy’s returns was regressed as the
independent variable, in excess of the risk-free rate, against the market return as the
dependent variable, also in excess of the risk-free rate. This was similarly done for all
sub-portfolios, and the ultimate QoE Score portfolios. Now, as mentioned in section 3.3,
the variables in the QoE Score portfolios are equally weighted between all the five
components, meaning that each component is assigned a weight of 20%. These weights
could of course be anything, and 20% was arbitrarily chosen for the sake of simplicity
and fairness between the scores.
       The multivariate regressions that were based on equation 20, which regressed each
sub-portfolio in the given strategy as independent variables against the market as the
dependent variable, proved interesting to test, however, does not implicitly say anything
at all, as it regresses the portfolio’s return against all the components of the quality of
earnings score, which it is made up of. Therefore, these regressions do not serve that much
purpose, but are interesting to test, nevertheless. The dual approach of univariate and
multivariate regressions serves multiple purposes: it validates the significance of
individual sub-portfolios, explores the combined effect of different earnings quality
indicators on market returns, and informs portfolio construction and optimization
strategies. It also broadens the regressions to not solely rely on CAPM, but also the Fama
French 3-Factor model to see how market size, and the relation between book and market
value affects returns.

    E. Further Regressions

Based on the univariate and multivariate regressions that are made, with the optimal goal
of finding alpha, further regressions are made for the components of quality of earnings,
where the alpha (or constant) is positive with significant results.
             As is further discussed in 4. Empirical Results, alpha was found for the
component Margin Score. To further examine exactly where this alpha was found, instead
of solely going long in the top 20% and short in the bottom 20%, a regression for each
decile (10%) was made against the market excess return, SMB, and HML. The point of
said regression is to see where the alpha can be found, and if one should invest in the
whole Margin Score portfolio or just some specific deciles.

    F. Portfolio Construction and Real-World Application

To test the real-world applicability of the QoE Score, further portfolios were built in MS
Excel to test if the QoE Score could be used as a means of beating the market. To test

                                  13
this, similar portfolios were built based on the same weights specified in 3.3, then, to
concentrate the results on companies with the highest QoE Scores, only the top 50
companies were included. Four portfolios were built, each equally weighted. One updated
every year, one updated every third year, and one updated every fifth year. The main
difference between these portfolios and the ones previously discussed is that the previous
ones are better suited for regressions, as these regress the financial data of specific firms
against the returns for a given year. However, in reality, it is impossible to predict
fundamental financial data for a given company and year, before the return for that year
has been established. If one could know the financial data of all companies in the S&P
500, and trade according to this theory, one would be able to reap large returns from said
QoE Score portfolio. However, as only public knowledge and data are available, these
portfolios are built with a one-year lag, meaning that the companies with the top 50 QoE
Scores are combined into a portfolio that is traded the following year. This allows us to
test the real-world application of the QoE Score, to see if it can actually be used as a valid
investing strategy. To test this, said portfolio return and standard deviation are captured
over the 14-year time frame, as QoE data is available for 2023, but not the stock returns
of 2024. Then, the returns and standard deviation of the three portfolios are combined
into a Sharpe ratio for each portfolio and then compared to the Sharpe ratio of the S&P
500 in the same time frame.

   H. Discussion of data sources

This paper’s main data sources were as mentioned Compustat, S&P Capital IQ, and Fama
& French Data Library. Starting with Compustat, which we used for acquiring financial
data with high levels of accuracy and reliability. S&P Capital IQ provided us with the
index constituents of the S&P 500; However, its interface made it difficult to find the
index constituents of the S&P 500 for years before 2023, and a code in R “reverse-
engineering” it for years before 2023 had to be made.
       For Compustat and Capital IQ, some data biases and errors may be present,
particularly in less transparent markets or for smaller companies that are not as well
scrutinized. Therefore, limiting the data to the S&P 500 was done to ensure a relatively
high accuracy in the data.
       Lastly the Fama-French Data Library provided us with data specifically designed
to test asset pricing models, particularly the three-factor model (market risk, size, and
value factors). However, the data primarily focuses on factors relevant to the Fama-
French three-factor and later models. While this is extensive, it may not cover other
variables or factors that could be relevant in different financial research contexts, such as
a momentum factor.

portfolios based on the Cash Flow Matching portfolio, we attain the outputs exhibited in
Table 5. Although beta is statistically significant for all different update frequencies,
including the isolated years 2+3 and 4+5, we find no significance for any alpha, which
would have been of our interest in this context. Thus, there is no indication that the use
of Cash Flow Matching for stock selection would yield statistically significant abnormal
returns. Jansen (2021) finds that cash flow itself is just a value conservator of a firm’s
stock; the actual increase (decrease) of stock value comes from growth (decline) in the
underlying cash flows of the entity. In this case, we constructed the Cash Flow Matching
variable to observe correlation between sales and operating income with the operating
cash flow, meaning that there is only relative, not absolute, growth being examined. To
exemplify, imagine a firm increasing (decreasing) its Cash Flow Matching score through
a reduction (increase) of income from accruals. In such a case, the Cash Flow Matching

                                  21
score is higher (lower) than before, as the correlation between EBIT and cash flow from
operation is increased due to the cash flow from operations remaining fixed, while the
EBIT is lowered (increased) due to the reduction (gain) of income from accruals. Hence,
there is a possibility that the operating cash flow remains unchanged while the Cash Flow
Matching variable score changes, resulting in a potentially unchanged stock price because
of the absence of cash flow growth which is the main driver of stock price movement
according to referenced literature.

                                  24
idea that most investments are directly involved in the business operations and hence, a

a metric of operating performance. Consequently, this means that firms with a high
Investment variable score should not have a strong stock performance per se; a high
invested share of the total cash outstanding might cause an increase in the Investment
variable score itself, however, without a sufficiently strong operating performance (ROA)
the stock performance might be poor despite a relatively high score in this variable.

                                    5. Conclusion

This study has found that our constructed equally-weighted QoE Score is able to yield
statistically significant abnormal returns on the S&P 500 when used as a stock selection
guide for fundamental analysis. We further establish that an annual update frequency, to
ensure that the 80th percentile is bought and the 20th percentile is sold, yields abnormal
returns amounting to 5.2% per year with a p<1% statistical significance level. Statistically
significant abnormal returns were also found for the three and five-year update
frequencies averaging 4.3% and 4.6% annually respectively. From our tests, we can also
conclude that there is no lagging effect in between the annual, three-year and five-year
updates, so the abnormal returns only seem to occur from the updating years and not in
the years in between the updates. However, this is not the case for the QoE component
Margin Score, where significant lagging effects were found, hinting that holding stocks
in the Margin Score portfolio for longer periods of time is beneficial for obtaining a higher
alpha. Additionally, the Margin Score variable performed surprisingly well; with
statistically significant alphas on all update frequencies tested, ranging from 12.6-14.6%
annually, it turned out to be the single most superior variable in the regard of finding
alpha. Furthermore, both the annual and five-year Margin Score portfolios had superior
cumulative returns relative to the S&P 500, however, their Sharpe ratios were lower due
to the high volatility of these portfolios. Finally, our study finds the risk-adjusted returns
of the QoE Score portfolio to exceed the risk-adjusted returns of the S&P 500 with the
former having a Sharpe ratio ranging between 0.89-0.94 depending on the update
frequency, compared with 0.86 for the S&P 500. Hence, we arrive at similar results as
Francis et. al (2005) about the equity risk; investors view firms with high earnings quality
as low risk, which is why the equity risk was found to be lower in such firms than for
firms with poor earnings quality. The same holds in our study; the firms in the QoE Score
portfolio demonstrate a greater Sharpe ratio than the S&P 500 market portfolio and hence
return a greater risk-adjusted yield, meaning such firms are less risky to hold in the
portfolio.
       Our findings should be put in relation to the articles presented in the literature
review for this paper. From our results, we could not establish the short-term stock price
effect that Bhojraj et al. (2009) found for firms with extensive earnings management
practices, that is, firms with low quality of earnings. However, our study makes no
portfolio distinction between high and low QoE Score stocks – instead, we only
considered the aggregate returns from the short selling of the 20th percentile and the
buying of the 80th percentile. Hence, our study is not entirely comparable with theirs.
Our results do, however, correspond with the findings established by Nguyen et al. (2022)
as portfolios constructed with the ESCORE through the 80/20 percentile buy/sell
approach yielded abnormal returns, similar to the portfolios created with our QoE Score
through the same buy/sell distribution, also yielding abnormal returns. Lastly, our results
show similarities with Ali & Bansal (2021) with regards to their conclusion that firms
with low (high) degree of earnings management return greater (lower) stock returns,
although the scope of our QoE Score is a bit beyond earnings management, as we consider
aspects capturing earnings quality which was our primary interest in this study.
        To refer to the initial questions, we conclude from this study that (1) stocks with
higher earnings quality, according to our definition of the QoE Score, outperform the
S&P 500 both in terms of absolute returns as well as risk-adjusted returns and that (2) the

                                  31
QoE Score can be used as a stock selection metric to systematically obtain returns
exceeding the S&P 500. Our initial hypotheses are thus confirmed, while the study
provided more insight into the different time spans when the earnings-quality effect on
stock returns is the greatest.
       Due to the complicated data retrieving process and the limited computational
power of our equipment, we must reserve for the fact that our findings might suffer from
low statistical power. In this paper, the fundamentals of every company in the S&P 500
between 2009 and 2023 have been used as a data sample, allowing us to create a
maximum of 15 portfolios for our statistical analyses and sometimes as few as merely
four portfolios, depending on what is tested. We acknowledge that this research can be
further elaborated with a more extensive data set to attain greater statistical robustness.
       To extend this study further, an examination of other markets, preferably outside
the United States, could provide insights to whether the QoE Score is as applicable in
markets with firms having other capital structures, regulations and characteristics.
Furthermore, since the highest abnormal returns were displayed from the shortest update
frequency tested, that is, the annual update frequency, it could be interesting to test
quarterly updates which would be the most frequent update frequency possible with this
score.

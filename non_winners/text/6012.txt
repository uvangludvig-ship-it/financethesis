Abstract:

This paper examines the impact of social capital, as measured by corporate social
responsibility (CSR), on stock returns during economic downturns, specifically the
Covid-19 crash and the 2022 market downturn. It highlights the nuanced role of CSR
during different types of economic stress and adds a contemporary dimension to
existing literature by offering a new perspective on investor behavior in the context of
recent global challenges. I find that higher CSR intensity had a positive and significant
impact on stock returns during the 2022 market downturn, albeit influenced by the
firm's broader financial health. However, this relationship was not evident during the
Covid-19 crash, suggesting that the nature of the economic downturn could be pivotal in
CSR's protective value.

   CSR, Stock returns, Market crisis, Investor behavior, Financial resilience

                      1. Introduction

    The role of trust in economics is a well-explored topic and has been the topic of promi-
nent research papers for many years. Putnam (1993) explores the positive impact that trust,
as measured by social capital, has on the effectiveness and stability of democratic gover-
nance. He finds that the level of social capital in a society is a good indicator of economic
growth which highlights the macroeconomic importance of trust. Guiso, Sapienza and Zin-
gales (2004) elaborate on the importance of trust by investigating how social capital-building
activities foster capital market participation. Over the years, the literature on the broader eco-
nomic impact of social capital has expanded but it lacks in-depth analysis of its impact on
firm-level performance and development. One of the primary challenges associated with
analyzing firms at the individual level is determining the appropriate proxy for measuring
social capital and trust. Both Putnam (1993) and Guiso et al. (2004) are able to use regional
differences to gauge differences in trust. However, a study focusing on the firm level neces-
sitates a more nuanced understanding of trust differences. Relying solely on regional trust
variations proves inadequate, as it is the distinctions between individual firms that are of
interest. This presents a challenge, as much of what constitutes trust is embedded within cul-
tural and social norms (Scrivens and Smith (2013)). Building on this foundation, this paper
examines how social capital impacts stock returns during economic downturns, specifically
among US-headquartered firms, focusing on the Covid-19 crash and the 2022 market down-
turn.
    One of the few works that provides an in-depth analysis of the relationship between social
capital and firm performance is authored by Lins, Servaes, and Tamayo (2017). They argue
that social capital or trust on a firm level can be measured by Corporate Social Responsibility
(CSR) and that stakeholders recognize CSR as a proxy for social capital. Lins et al. (2017)
address the challenge of the lack of exogenous change in social capital. Varying levels of
social capital over time would provide the required exogenous change to measure the rela-
tionship between social capital and firm performance. However, firm-level social capital is
relatively static over time. Lins et al. (2017) propose that times of economic crisis provide
the necessary setting. As overall trust in corporations and markets declines during an eco-
nomic crisis, firms perceived as trustworthy by stakeholders will be valued higher than their
low-trust counterparts. Firms that engaged in social capital-building practices in the past are
reciprocated by employees, shareholders and the community when overall trust is low. Lins
et al. (2017) find that CSR had a positive and significant impact on firm performance during
both the financial crisis of 2008/2009 and the Enron/Worldcom crisis of the early 2000s.
    While the study by Lins et al. (2017) offers a novel approach to measure the impact of so-
cial capital on firm performance, their study only concerns the financial crisis of 2008/2009
and the Enron/Worldcom crisis of the early 2000s. This prompts a study that investigates
more contemporary market downturns. Kee-Hong, El Ghoul, Gong and Guedhami (2021)
examine the relationship between social capital and firm performance during the Covid-19
crash. In contrast to Lins et al. (2017), Kee-Hong et al. (2021) do not find that social capital
has a significant positive impact on firm performance.
    Although the Covid-19 crash in 2020 provides a more recent context than the financial

crises of 2008/2009, I aim to further enhance the exploration of social capital’s impact on
firm performance by analyzing the market downturn in 2022. In 2022, the S&P 500 index
retraced 25%, reflecting the economy’s anticipation of aggressive interest rate hikes amid
global economic pressures from inflation and geopolitical tensions, particularly the conflict
in Ukraine (Caldara, Conlisk, Iacoviello and Penn (2022)). To my knowledge, the impact of
social capital on firm performance during the market downturn in 2022 is unexplored terri-
tory. Since this is also the most recent major market correction, my study offers the most
recent examination of the relationship between social capital and firm performance during
an economic crisis.
   Furthermore, previous studies have only analyzed economic downturns in isolation (Kee-
Hong et al. (2021)) or compared similar economic downturns to each other (Lins et al.
(2017) with the financial crisis of 2008/2009 and the Enron/Worldcom crisis of the early
2000s). Thus, there is a noticeable lack of work on how CSR impacts firm performance
differently depending on the characteristics of the economic downturn. For instance, the
financial crisis in 2008/2009 persisted for eight months, in contrast to the Covid-19 crash,
which lasted only one month. The opposing findings by Kee-Hong et al. (2021) and Lins et
al. (2017) point to major differences in how stakeholders value social capital depending on
the characteristics of the economic downturn. Therefore, apart from offering an analysis on
the most recent market downturn in 2022, my study compares the impact of social capital on
firm performance from two vastly different economic downturns, the Covid-19 crash and the
market downturn in 2022.
    At the core of this study, I conduct an empirical analysis to examine the relationship
between social capital, quantified through CSR, and stock returns. I build on the regression
model presented by Lins et al. (2017). I use CSR data from 2341 firms for the market down-
turn in 2022 and 1973 firms for the Covid-19 crash to build an aggregate measure of a firm’s
CSR rating for each crisis period. Subsequently, I analyze stock returns during these two
economic downturns and conduct regression analysis to examine the relationship between
CSR and stock returns. Furthermore, I control for firm characteristics and factors that are
related to the financial health of the firm to add further robustness to my findings. Through
the application of my regression model to data from both the 2022 market downturn and the
2020 Covid-19 crash, I derive estimates of the impact of CSR on firm performance across
both periods. These results serve as the foundation for my discussion on how stakeholders
perceive the value of social capital, varying with the characteristics of economic downturns.
    My findings offer support to existing literature. I find that social capital had a positive
significant impact on firm performance during the market downturn in 2022 but not in the
post-crisis period. Although the results do not exhibit significance across all robustness tests,
they nevertheless suggest that social capital had a positive impact on firm performance dur-
ing the market downturn, similar to that observed by Lins et al. (2017). According to my
analysis, a one standard deviation increase in CSR intensity resulted in a 1.41 percentage
points increase in stock return during the 2022 market downturn. Lins et al. (2017) observe
similar, positive significant, results for the financial crisis of 2008/2009. This indicates that
stakeholders put a value premium on firms with high social capital during both the finan-
cial crisis of 2008/2009 and the market downturn in 2022. I contend that this is logical, as

the market downturn in 2022 shares similar features with the financial crisis in 2008/2009.
However, I do not find a positive significant relationship between social capital and firm per-
formance during the Covid-19 crash or the post-crisis period. This aligns with the findings
by Kee-Hong et al. (2021). I argue that the varying importance of CSR in determining firm
performance arises from the differing characteristics of the Covid-19 crash and the market
downturn in 2022.

                    2. Background

       There’s been a growing body of literature that examines the impact of social capital
on firm performance during times of economic stress. Lins et al. (2017) were the first ones
to comprehensively analyze this relationship, laying a foundation for subsequent research in
the field. They introduced the novel method of using CSR as a proxy for social capital when
examining the relationship between social capital and firm performance during economic
downturns. In their paper, they argue that CSR activities should act as a good measure of
social capital as ”definitions of CSR, which generally involve aspects of civic engagement,
shared beliefs, and disposition towards cooperation between the firm and its stakeholders,
tend to map directly into the theoretical foundations of social capital.”. Lins et al. (2017)
argue that the empirical study on the relationship between CSR and firm performance should
be performed during periods of low market trust, such as economic downturns, due to is-
sues of endogeneity. This argument is based on their observation that CSR’s impact on firm
performance is particularly pronounced during economic downturns when trust is otherwise
low. The authors suggest that endogeneity issues, where it is challenging to separate the ef-
fects of CSR from other variables that might also be influencing firm performance, are more
manageable during crises. This is because the crisis acts as a form of natural experiment
in which there is an exogenous shock to overall trust while CSR ratings are kept constant.
Lins et al. (2017) find a positive significant relationship between CSR and firm performance
during both the financial crisis of 2008/2009 and the Enron/Worldcom Fraud Scandal in the
early 2000s. A one-standard deviation increase in CSR led to a 1.83 percentage point in-
crease in stock returns during the financial crisis of 2008/2009.
    The findings by Lins et al. (2017) prompted Kee-Hong et al. (2021) to perform a sim-
ilar empirical study on a more recent economic downturn, the Covid-19 crash. However,
contrary to previous studies, Kee-Hong et al. (2021) do not find that CSR had a significant
impact on firm performance during the Covid-19 crash. Their findings hold after perform-
ing various robustness tests such as including industry dummies and controlling for variables
related to the financial health of firms. Additionally, to provide them with a baseline relation-
ship between CSR and firm performance, they test whether CSR impacts firm performance
both before and after the crisis. With this, they can compare the impact of CSR during nor-
mal times with the impact of CSR during economic downturns. Once again, Kee-Hong et
al. (2021) find that CSR had no significant impact on firm performance. They conclude
that their findings indicate a contrarian view on the impact of CSR on stock returns during
times of economic crisis and suggest that the significance of CSR during a crisis warrants

reevaluation.
    This provides me with the opportunity to broaden the existing knowledge about the im-
pact of CSR on firm performance during times of economic crisis in two ways. Firstly, I
apply the methodological framework outlined by Lins et al. (2017) and Kee-Hong et al.
(2021) to a new period, the market downturn in 2022. By extending the analysis to a more
recent economic downturn, I can gauge whether the observed relationship between CSR and
firm performance persists across varying economic contexts. Secondly, my analysis of both
the market downturn in 2022 and the Covid-19 crash enables me to compare the impact of
CSR on firm performance during two distinct periods characterized by differing underlying
causes of economic downturns. While the Covid-19 crash was primarily driven by a global
health crisis and subsequent lockdown measures, the market downturn in 2022 was influ-
enced by factors such as inflationary pressures and geopolitical tensions. This comparative
analysis sheds light on the adaptability of CSR strategies across different crisis scenarios.

                3. Data and methodology

Data

All the data that I use in this study is collected from the Eikon Refinitiv Database. The Eikon
Refinitiv Database holds all data that relates to the CSR ratings of publicly traded firms
with headquarters in the U.S. There are a multitude of different CSR ratings available but I
select the ones that represent measures in community, diversity, employee relations, environ-
ment and human rights which is consistent with the CSR categories used by both Lins et al.
(2017) and Kee-Hong et al. (2021)). However, as both of these studies collect data from the
MSCI ESG Stats database, as opposed to the Eikon Refinitiv Database, there may be varia-
tions in the exact definitions of individual CSR measures. In the Eikon Refinitiv Database,
these CSR measures are named Community Score (measures the company’s commitment to-
wards being a good citizen, protecting public health, and respecting business ethics), Policy
Diversity and Opportunity Score (score based on the degree that the company implements
policy to drive diversity and equal opportunity), Workforce Score (measures the company’s
effectiveness towards job satisfaction and healthy and safe workplace), Environmental Pillar
Score (measures the company’s impact on living and non-living natural systems) and Human
Rights Score (measures the company’s effectiveness towards respecting the fundamental hu-
man rights convention). There are also differences in the scaling of CSR measures across
the MSCI ESG Stats Database and the Eikon Refinitiv Database. Instead of obtaining a CSR
score that ranges from -5 to 5 (Lins et al. (2017); Kee-Hong et al. (2021)), I use a final CSR
score that ranges from 0 to 100. This final CSR score is calculated as the arithmetic mean
of the five individual CSR scores from each category which all range from 0 to 100 individ-
ually. A value of zero in the final CSR score should be interpreted as if the company hasn’t
taken any measures of social responsibility. On the opposite, if a firm has a perfect social
responsibility score in each of the five categories, it obtains a final CSR score of 100. I call
the final measure of CSR score Total CSR and collect data on Total CSR at the conclusion of

                           Stockholm School of Economics

the fiscal year preceding each of the economic downturns. This means that Total CSR for the
market downturn in 2022 is based on the CSR measures of firms at the conclusion of the fis-
cal year 2021 and Total CSR for the Covid-19 crash is based on the CSR measures of firms
at the conclusion of the fiscal year 2019. I choose these dates as CSR scores are updated
at the conclusion of each fiscal year and I want the most recent CSR scores preceding the
economic downturns. Data of all five CSR categories is accessible for the majority of U.S.
headquartered firms. As missing data in CSR measures among firms is infrequent, I exclude
those lacking such data from the analysis without significantly impacting the analytical out-
comes.
    I also collect data on stock returns from the Eikon Refinitiv Database. The stock return
measure that I use, called Crisis-Period Return, is a measure of raw buy-and-hold return over
a given period of the firm’s stock. I define the market downturn in 2022 as the period from
the 4th of January 2022 to the 12th of October 2022 as these dates mark the highest daily
closing price of the S&P 500 index before the downturn and the lowest daily closing price
of the S&P 500 index during the downturn. I define the post-crisis period as the subsequent
291-day period (October 13-July 31, 2021) during which the S&P 500 index increased by
30%. For the Covid-19 crash, I’m interested in the 33-day period from the 19th of February
2020 to the 23rd of March 2020. This defines the period from the peak of the highest daily
closing price of the S&P 500 index before the crash and the lowest daily closing price of
the S&P 500 index during the crash. I define the post-crisis period as the subsequent 76-day
period (March 23–June 8, 2020) during which the S&P 500 index increased by 45%.
    As I encounter outliers in Crisis-Period Return on both ends, I winsorize these returns
at the 1st and 99th percentiles. Furthermore, I implement a market cap threshold at $250
million to exclude micro-cap stocks. This market cap threshold is implemented because
micro-cap stocks typically exhibit low liquidity and wide bid-ask spreads, making them more
susceptible to price pressure effects due to trading activities. These vulnerabilities are likely
to be exacerbated during times of financial crisis. Furthermore, micro-cap stocks are over-
represented in terms of missing CSR data. Thus, by implementing a market cap threshold I
can reduce the number of companies that need to be omitted due to missing CSR data. After
the data processing, I obtain a final sample of 2341 firms for the market downturn in 2022
and 1973 firms for the Covid-19 crash.

Model

I use an OLS regression model to regress Crisis-Period Return on Total CSR. This aligns
with the model selection by both Lins et al. (2017) and Kee-Hong et al. (2021). Using a
regression model, I am able to test the hypothesis of whether CSR had a significant impact
on firm performance during the market downturn in 2022 and the Covid-19 crash in 2020.
The regression model also enables me to test whether the hypothesis holds for various ro-
bustness tests. Based on a visual inspection of the preliminary data, I infer that any potential
relationship between CSR and stock returns would likely exhibit linearity. Fig 1 in the ap-
pendix shows a scatter plot of the data with a fitted regression line (Crisis-Period Return is

Basic regression model
The basic regression model that I use regresses Crisis-Period Return on Total CSR. I include
sector dummies to control for CSR differences across sectors. Sectors differ in their CSR
engagement and how they respond to economic downturns. I use the NAICS sector division
which consists of 20 sectors such as Manufacturing; Retail Trade; Finance and Insurance;
and Information. However, four sectors with less than 10 instances in the data are removed
to ensure reliable statistical inferences. These are the four sectors: Agriculture, Forestry,
Fishing and Hunting; Arts, Entertainment, and Recreation; Management of Companies and
Enterprises; and Public Administration. The basic regression model is presented below (1)
(Note that one dummy variable is captured by the intercept.)

          Crisis-Period Return = β0 + β1 × Total CSR
                           + β2 × Sector Dummy1 + β3 × Sector Dummy2       (1)
                           + . . . + β16 × Sector Dummy15 + ε

Full regression model
However, in addition to the firm’s sector belonging, there are likely other factors that are cor-
related with Total CSR that could explain the variations in return during the market downturn.
Lins et al. (2017) argue that such factors should include a firm’s financial health and other
firm characteristics that have proven to have an impact on stock returns historically. There-
fore, following previous studies (Lins et al. (2017); Kee-Hong et al. (2021)), I control for
the following factors: First, Cash Holdings is a firm’s cash divided by its assets. I use Cash
Holdings as it shows me how liquid the company is and how fast it can meet short-term
obligations without difficulty. High levels of Cash Holdings also allow the firm to seize
opportunities or invest in growth areas without relying heavily on external financing. Sec-
ondly, I define Short-Term Debt as the ratio of the firm’s current liabilities to its assets. I use
Short-Term Debt as it indicates the company’s ability to meet short-term obligations. Higher
ratios suggest potential liquidity challenges. Additionally, high ratios indicate higher finan-
cial risk, as short-term debt often carries higher interest rates. Thirdly, I use Long-Term Debt
(long-term debt divided by assets). Long-term debt indicates a firm’s leverage and solvency.
A higher ratio suggests greater reliance on debt to finance operations, potentially increas-
ing financial risk. Finally, I calculate Profitability by dividing operating income by assets.
Profitability gives me an idea of how effectively the company generates profits relative to
its asset base. I decide to divide all four metrics by the firm’s assets as this normalizes the
financial metrics which makes them comparable across differently-sized firms. To further
control for the size of firms, I include the logarithm of the firm’s market capitalization (Size).

I also control for Book-to-Market in my regression. Fama and French (1992) demonstrated
that the Book-to-Market ratio significantly influences the anticipated returns of companies in
a systematic way. Finally, I control for a factor that I call Momentum. I define momentum as
the raw buy-and-hold return of the firm’s stock over the last 12 months leading up to the start
of the downturns (4th of January 2022 for the 2022 market downturn and 19th of February
2020 for the Covid-19 crash). A firm entering a market downturn with positive momentum
may sustain this momentum during the downturn, thereby mitigating the observable effect
of the CSR measure on stock returns, as momentum itself explains some of the variations in
Crisis-Period Return throughout the downturn.
    I obtain measures of Cash Holdings, Short-Term Debt, Long-Term Debt, Profitability,
Size and Book-to-Market at the conclusion of the fiscal year preceding the market down-
turns. The full regression model is presented below (2).

                          + β2 × Cash Holdings + β3 × Short-Term Debt
                          + β4 × Long-Term Debt + β5 × Profitability
                          + β6 × Size + β7 × Book-to-Market                (2)

Robustness Tests
In this section, I focus on various implementations of robustness checks for the main find-
ings. Firstly, it is of interest to test whether the results that I obtain differ depending on the
CSR measure that I use. Therefore, I test whether specific components of the CSR measure
had a significant impact on stock returns during the market downturn in 2022 and the Covid-
19 crash. The five metrics that I choose to include in my aggregate CSR measure cover a
broad spectrum of CSR activities and provide me with an overall assessment of the firm’s
CSR commitment. However, some of the activities could be especially important in building
trust among stakeholders and yielding positive stock returns. Therefore, I decompose To-
tal CSR into two subcategories, Internal Stakeholders CSR and External Stakeholders CSR.
Internal Stakeholders CSR consists of measurements of Policy Diversity and Opportunity
Score and Workforce Score and concern CSR metrics aimed at internal stakeholders. Exter-
nal Stakeholders CSR is an aggregate measure of Community Score, Environmental Pillar
Score and Human Rights Score and address external stakeholders. With the new CSR scores,
I use the full regression model (2) to test the relationship between CSR and stock returns. Ta-
ble 13 in the appendix presents the results for the two economic downturns. For the market
downturn in 2022, I note that the coefficients for both Internal Stakeholders CSR and Ex-
ternal Stakeholders CSR are positive and significant at the 10% level, similarly to the main
findings in Table 3. A one-point increase Internal Stakeholders CSR leads to a 0.06 percent-
age point increase in Crisis-Period Return and a one-point increase in External Stakeholders
CSR leads to a 0.05 percentage point increase in Crisis-Period Return, indicating that they
were of similar significance. As for the Covid-19 crash, neither Internal Stakeholders CSR
nor External Stakeholders CSR had a significant impact on stock returns. This, once again,
aligns with the main findings presented in Table 7. Thus, I conclude that a focus on either
internal or external stakeholders was of equal significance, or non-significance, to investors
during the market downturn in 2022 and the Covid-19 crash.
    Next, I want to test whether my main findings are robust to the inclusion of smaller firms
(I here include firms with market capitalization below $250 million). I run my full regression
model (2) on this new dataset for both the market downturn in 2022 and the Covid-19 crash.

    Lastly, I test the robustness of my main findings by varying the point in time at which I
obtain CSR measures of the firms. I argue that I want to use CSR measures that had been
updated as recently as possible prior to the onset of the economic downturns. This means
that I obtain CSR measures at the conclusion of the fiscal year 2021 for the market downturn
in 2022 and at the conclusion of the fiscal year 2019 for the Covid-19 crash. However, as
outlined by Lins et al. (2017), firms may anticipate economic downturns and thus adjust their
CSR engagement as a response. The results that I report could be biased if there is a corre-
lation between those firms that managed to readjust their CSR prior to the crisis and those
firms that handled the crisis well. It is unlikely that firms anticipated the Covid-19 crash
at the conclusion of the fiscal year 2018, one year before the onset of the crash, due to its
unexpected nature. I nonetheless present the results from my full regression model (2) with
CSR measures collected at the conclusion of the fiscal year 2018 in Table 15 in the appendix.
The estimated coefficient of Total CSR is still non-significant for the Covid-19 crash. It is
more likely that managers anticipated the market downturn in 2022 one year in advance due
to the risk of inflationary pressures from the extensive stimulus packages implemented in the
wake of the Covid-19 crash. The estimated coefficient of Total CSR (seen in Table 15 in the
appendix) is positive but non-significant for the market downturn in 2022 when I measure
CSR at the conclusion of the fiscal year 2020 instead of 2021. The non-significant results
contrast with my findings in the main regression (see Table 3), suggesting that the relation-
ship between CSR and stock returns for the market downturn in 2022 is sensitive to the year
in which the CSR measure is obtained. One aspect needs to be considered; Kee-Hong et al.
(2021) note that the Covid-19 crash led to heightened interest in CSR among stakeholders.
I substantiate this by calculating the correlation coefficients of TotalCSR across consecutive
years - 2020-2021: 0.920, 2021-2022: 0.927 and 2022-2023: 0.944. The correlation co-
efficients indicate that managers changed their CSR commitments more between the years
2020-2021 and 2021-2022 compared to 2022-2023. Thus, it is plausible that the apparent
lack of correlation between CSR measures from the conclusion of fiscal year 2020 and stock
returns during the 2022 market downturn isn’t solely attributable to managers anticipating
the downturn, but rather that it reflects changing CSR behavior as an effect of the Covid-19
crash.

Comparison of economic downturns
I find that CSR had a significant impact on stock returns during the 2022 market downturn,
albeit weak and not consistent across all robustness checks, which contrasts with the non-
significant result that I observe for the Covid-19 crash.
    One factor that I need to consider is if the market downturn in 2022 led to a true ex-

                                    22
                           Stockholm School of Economics

ogenous shock to the level of trust in the economy. The Covid-19 crash presents me with
a clear exogenous event as the pandemic led to an unexpected increase in demand for CSR
(Kee-Hong et al. (2021)). The characterization of the 2022 downturn as exogenous shock
to trust levels is not as clear. By using an economic downturn as a natural experiment, I am
able to bypass endogeneity concerns that complicate discerning the actual impact of CSR
activities on firm value. Economic downturns disrupt the existing equilibrium level of trust
without altering CSR levels in the short term, allowing me to assess how investors adjust
their valuations based on firms’ CSR stances. This framework mirrors the approach that
Lins et al. (2017) implement when they analyze the 2008/2009 financial crisis. For me to be
able to draw similar conclusions about exogeneity for the market downturn in 2022, I need
to substantiate the claim that the market downturn in 2022 truly disrupted the equilibrium
level of trust. The 2023 Edelman Trust Barometer indicates that the overall trust in the world
economy declined significantly from 2022 to 2023 with 24 out of 28 countries with trust
levels at an all-time low. This suggests a widespread disruption of trust due to an exogenous
shock. I substantiate my second claim, that CSR levels remained fixed, by noting that the
correlation coefficient for TotalCSR between 2022 and 2023 is 0.944. Thus, I infer that the
differing results between the two economic downturns are not due to the market downturn in
2022 failing to act as an exogenous shock to trust levels in the economy.
    Given the alignment of my findings with those of Kee-Hong et al. (2021) regarding
the Covid-19 crash and the similarities between my results for the 2022 market downturn
and those of Lins et al. (2017) for the 2008/2009 financial crisis, I investigate whether the
characteristics of these economic downturns explain the differing results between the market
downturn in 2022 and the Covid-19 crash. One potential explanation for the diverging results
is the distinct temporal characteristics of the two economic downturns. The Covid-19 crash
only lasted for 33 days (March 24, 2020–June 8, 2020) compared to 281 days for the market
downturn in 2022 (January 4, 2022–October 12, 2022). Lins et al. (2017) define the financial
crisis of 2008/2009 to have lasted for 240 days (August 1, 2008–March 31, 2009). I note the
similar duration of the market downturn in 2022 and the financial crisis of 2008/2009, both
of which showed a significant impact of CSR on firm performance. This contrasts with the
Covid-19 crash in which the S&P 500 dropped more than 20% from its peak in just 22 days,
making it the quickest decline into bear market territory ever recorded. H. Kim, T. Kim,
Y. Kim, and Park (2019) find that longer investment horizons are positively related to CSR,
indicating that CSR is perceived as a long-term investment metric. Thus, considering the
prolonged nature of both the market downturn in 2022 and the financial crisis of 2008/2009,
investors likely placed a higher value on CSR during these economic downturns compared to
the Covid-19 crash, where short-term metrics held greater importance due to its brevity. (see
control variables in column (2) in Table 7). Fig 2 in the appendix highlights the difference
in the duration of the market downturn in 2022, the financial crisis of 2008/2009 and the
Covid-19 crash.

                      6. Conclusion

My paper examines the relationship between social capital, measured by CSR, and stock
returns during two economic downturns, the market downturn in 2022 and the Covid-19
crash in 2020. I find no evidence that CSR influenced firm performance during the Covid-19
crisis, and this finding remains consistent across various robustness tests. However, for the
market downturn in 2022, I find weak evidence suggesting that CSR had a positive impact
on stock returns. As the results differ across the two economic downturns, I argue that the
characteristics of the economic downturn play a major role in determining the impact of CSR
on firm performance. The duration of the economic downturn needs to be considered when
reflecting on the importance of CSR during times of economic crisis. Overall, my findings
underscore the ambiguous impact of CSR on firm performance when overall trust is low.
This contrasts with the prevailing view in the literature, which suggests that firms should
enhance social capital to safeguard themselves during market downturns. I challenge the
unequivocal perception that social capital offers protective power during market downturns,
highlighting that a contextualized and situation-specific approach is needed to determine the
impact of CSR on firm performance.

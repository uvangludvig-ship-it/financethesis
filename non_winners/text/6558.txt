Brand Power Meets Market Fear: A Downside Risk Perspective

Abstract:

   Brand equity represents an important intangible asset, yet its link to market behavior
   in negative-return months remains insufficiently understood. Understanding whether
   brand equity mitigates this so-called downside risk has implications for valuation,
   portfolio construction and corporate strategy. Despite its attention in contemporary
   discussions, there is no overarching measure of both downside risk and brand equity.
   We apply a novel measure of brand equity and a threefold framework to measure
   downside risk. We find an association between brand equity and downside risk,
   depending on the measure of downside risk and the portfolio-weighting scheme.
   Further, we show that crises affect this relationship non-universally. Also, strong
   brands tend to lower B2C firms' risk and partly offset the inherent risk we identify of
   following a B2B strategy. We consider various robustness tests and show that they do
   not materially invalidate our key results.

Keywords:

  Brand Equity, Downside Risk, Crisis Moderation, Business Model, Intangible Assets

1 Introduction

Brand equity and downside risk are concepts with unclear and often inconsistent defini-
tions. Yet, managers and investors confront both on a daily basis, whether in strategic
brand decisions or in financial market evaluations. Despite this practical relevance, we
still lack a clear understanding of how to measure them and how brand equity relates
to firms’ downside risk. Therefore, we address this ambiguity by using output-facing
methods for measuring brands and combining them with established approaches for esti-
mating downside risk. From this, the main contribution of this paper is to analyze the
relationship between brand equity and downside risk and to present an understanding of
how crisis types and business models affect this interaction. In our analysis, we observe
that the relationship between downside risk and brand equity is not universal. Instead,
whether an effect appears depends on the specific downside risk measure, the underlying
crisis mechanism and the firm’s business model. Throughout the analysis, we also ob-
serve value-weighted portfolios exhibiting higher downside risk, whereas equal-weighted
portfolios can be associated with lower downside risk. We additionally find support that
strong B2B firms show to have an embedded higher downside risk than strong B2C firms.
Further, we show that downside risk patterns differ when the analysis is performed at the
portfolio- or firm-level, and that results vary with how brands are measured.
   In today’s knowledge-driven economy, the composition of corporate value has un-
dergone a fundamental transformation. The Asset Management Council (Heidi, 2024)
highlights this by stating that intangible assets, such as intellectual property, recently
outweigh traditional tangible assets in determining firm value. This shift emphasizes that
value creation now relies more on non-physical resources to strengthen customer loyalty
and secure long-term competitiveness. For instance, the ratio of intangible assets to en-
                                  1
terprise value for the FAANG  companies and the largest global players is around 90%
(Corporate Intangible Assets Grew to USD 61.9 trillion in 2023, ).
   Among intangible assets, brand capital stands out as one of the most visible and
financially relevant drivers of firm value. One of the most influential studies linking brand
value to firm performance is Belo, Lin, and Vitorino (2014). They conceptualize brand
capital as the stock of intangible assets accumulated through firms’ past marketing and
advertising investments. Belo et al. (2014) find that firms with such elevated brand capital
enjoy lower volatility in revenues and higher valuations relative to peers. Therefore, their
findings position brand capital as a distinct productive asset that influences both firm
risk and expected returns.
   Strong brands exhibit a competitive advantage since they enhance customer loyalty,
reduce price sensitivity and support stable and recurring cash flows (Keller, 2013). How-
ever, brands have an inherent difficulty to being measured at their true economic value,
as their value cannot directly be observed due to the lack of market prices. Further-
more, conventional accounting systems fail to capture brand value since most marketing
or innovation expenditures are treated as expenses rather than capitalized investments.
Additionally, they are inconsistently disclosed or are strategically reported (Lev and Gu,
2016; Shi, Grewal, and Sridhar, 2021). This underscores that they remain difficult to be
measured and compared accurately.
   Recent advances attempt to address these limitations through output-based measures
of brand equity. A key contribution is by Boustanifar and Kang (2025) who propose

two methods to measure brand equity. They use a third-party provider ranking brands
annually, which is called Interbrand, and a text-based brand frequency metric from SEC
annual filings. The authors argue for their method by showing that traditional input-based
proxies such as advertising expenditures do not reliably predict higher excess returns.
Their results show that strong brand firms earn annual excess returns of 2.2 - 3% from the
year 2000 until 2020. Their findings suggest that brand capital is not only a source of firm
value, but may also be systematically mispriced in equity markets. Moreover, while they
note that brand reputation shapes investor perceptions across varying market conditions,
their core analysis focuses on full-sample average annual excess returns. Although they
broadly assess distinct sub-periods, they do not evaluate whether strong brands provide
lower risk during downside markets, periods when such effects are likely to matter most.
In this thesis, we define downside markets as months in which the market excess return
is negative.
   This missing link is crucial. While recent research has shown how brands influence
firms’ average performance, considerably less is understood about their relationship with
downside risk. Rego, Billett, and Morgan (2009) provide early evidence that firms with
high brand equity tend to experience lower credit risk during downside market months.
However, this finding is based on aggregated survey downside market data between the
years 2000 and 2006. Further research supports this possible stabilizing role. Lou (2014)
finds that increased advertising activity can raise investor attention and mitigate stock
price declines following shocks. Yet, Lou (2014) examines periods as a whole and does
not distinguish between market conditions nor adverse effects such as crises. McAlis-
ter, Srinivasan, and Kim (2007) provide further evidence that brand-related investments,
particularly advertising, reduce firms’ exposure to systematic risk. In particular, these
investments influence risk profiles by stabilizing demand, moderating revenue volatility
and reducing sensitivity to market-wide shocks. This supports the idea that brand capital
has a risk-mitigating role beyond its effect on returns.
   Cross-crisis analyses are a dimension that could benefit from further exploration in
brand contexts. Research in macro-finance shows that crises differ in their underlying
mechanisms, such as credit contraction, demand collapse, uncertainty shocks or liquidity
freezes (Claessens and Kose, 2013). This implies that firms are not uniformly affected
across crises and that the relationship of brand equity and downside risk may vary de-
pending on the structure of the shock. To examine the relationship, we follow established
crisis definitions from prior research that best capture the crisis dynamics from a US mar-
ket perspective. Kinal and Kinal (2022) define the Dotcom crisis as spanning March 2000
to October 2002, a period marked by the collapse of technology valuations. Ofek and
Richardson (2003) further examine the buildup of the bubble and its burst in early 2000,
providing additional insight into the dynamics of this episode. The Global Financial Cri-
sis can be defined as spanning from August 2007 to March 2009 as a period capturing the
escalation of credit market stress and systemic failures in financial institutions (Fahlen-
brach and Stulz, 2011). The COVID-19 crisis window in our study follows Baek, Mohanty,
and Glambosky (2020). They analyze the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic using
industry-level volatility data from the first months of 2020. They focus on the period of
heightened uncertainty, from which we define the COVID-19 crisis window as January to
April 2020. Finally, we define the energy shock crisis from June 2021 to December 2022,
based on the Annual Economic Report 2022 (2022). The report attributes the sustained
inflationary shock to rising energy prices, supply disruptions and geopolitical tensions.
Using these well-established definitions ensures that our crisis periods align with earlier

                                      4
empirical work and reflect the dominant mechanisms characterizing each shock.
   A further critical point that has been debated in brand research concerns the role
of business models in shaping how brands create value. Previous work shows that busi-
ness models fundamentally influence customer relationships and therefore suggests that
brand equity does not operate uniformly across business-facing and consumer-facing firms.
Ulaga and Eggert (2006) show that B2B value is created through long-term partnerships,
interdependence and service performance. They also highlight that supplier reputation
functions as a signal of reliability and reduced transactional risk. In line with this dis-
tinction, Lindgreen and Wynstra (2005) summarize prior consumer research showing that
value in consumer markets is often driven by individual perceptions, emotional attach-
ment and brand-based differentiation. However, their own analysis emphasizes that B2B
value is more functional and relational. Kowalkowski, Kindstr¨om, and Brehmer (2011)
demonstrate that industrial (B2B) offerings are evaluated using technical criteria and
professional decision processes. Vargo and Lusch (2004) argue that value creation mech-
anisms differ depending on how customers engage with the firm, reinforcing the idea that
the way brands generate value is contingent on the underlying business model. Finally,
Homburg, Jensen, and Krohmer (2008) document structural differences in how firms or-
ganize marketing and customer interaction across industries, some of which correspond
to consumer-facing versus business-facing contexts. This supports the view that brand
equity functions differently depending on the firm’s market orientation. Together, these
findings suggest that the role and effectiveness of brand equity vary systematically across
business-facing, consumer-facing and hybrid firms.
   Practically, this paper classifies firms based on previously established literature on
business model typologies (Teece, 2010; Zott, Amit, and Massa, 2011; Iankova, Davies,
Archer-Brown, Marder, and Yau, 2019). The classification builds a framework of each
firm’s primary customer type, brand communication, industry context and any events that
materially changed its business mix over time. Examples of how each firm was classified,
together with the reasoning behind these decisions, are provided in the appendix (A.4).
   Despite significant progress in measuring brands and understanding their firm value
relevance, prior research has not examined whether different types of crises or differing
business models affect the relationship between brand equity and downside risk. This
leaves an important gap in understanding how brand capital behaves under stress. Thus,
this thesis examines whether stronger brand equity exhibits lower downside risk and
whether this effect differs across distinct types of crises and business models.
    Understanding this gap is highly relevant for investors and managers. Investors need to
know whether brand equity could be associated with risk in crisis environments. Managers
rely on brand equity to stabilize cash flows, justify marketing budgets and signal resilience
to markets. Our contribution to previous research allows both academics and practitioners
to extend their understanding of how brand equity relates to risk in downside markets.
This makes the question both theoretically important and practically valuable.

2 Data Description and Descriptive Statistics

2.1 Interbrand Data

We derive the core dataset used in this study from Interbrand’s Best Global Brands
rankings (Best Global Brands 2024 — Ranking, ). Interbrand publishes this ranking
annually and has done so since the year 2000. The first edition included the top 75
global brands, after which the list expanded to the top 100 brands from 2001 onward.
Although the 2025 ranking is already available, we exclude it in our analysis because the
corresponding financial market data in WRDS is not yet accessible. We also assume the
rankings from 2000 to be the same in the years 1998 and 1999 in order to construct 18-
month rolling downside betas later in the analysis. We see this as a reasonable assumption,
considering that on average, only two companies get dropped and added in the rankings
yearly. Our sample therefore spans the period 1998 to 2024.
   To construct a dataset that can be integrated with financial market information, we
extract every brand and its year-specific ranking from the annual reports. Some brands,
such as Coca-Cola and Microsoft, are consistently present throughout the sample period,
while others enter or exit the ranking over time. We then match each brand to its U.S.-
listed parent company by merging the Interbrand data with WRDS identifiers including
PERMNO, GVKEY and CIK (CRSP and Compustat: CRSP/Compustat Merged (CCM)
Database, 2025). We restrict the sample to brands whose ultimate parent company is
publicly traded in the United States to ensure that the resulting portfolios are tradeable.
We remove brands owned by non-U.S. parents from the dataset unless the brand originated
as a U.S. asset before being acquired by a foreign company. We check all tickers manually
and verify them through WRDS to ensure accurate parent-firm identification.
   For each brand-year observation we also document any adjustments that were neces-
sary due to mergers, acquisitions or other corporate events that affect brand ownership or
classification. In an additional step, we merge several layers of industry classification data,
including NAICS and the full GICS hierarchy (sector, group, industry and sub-industry),
for both brands and their parent firms. This allows us to construct within-industry portfo-
lios and to apply our classification of firms into consumer-facing, business-facing or hybrid
business models. Therefore, we assign each brand to one of these categories on a reasoned
basis. We refer to this constructed strong brand portfolio as the Interbrand portfolio.

2.2 Stock Universe

The stock universe serves as both our input to fill the Interbrand data with market
specifiers and as our weak portfolio for the regressions we are running.
   We build the stock universe by merging the CRSP/Compustat Merged Fundamentals
Monthly File and the CRSP Monthly Stock File (CRSP and Compustat: CRSP/Compustat
Merged (CCM) Database, 2025). The CCM panel provides firm-level accounting data,
linking Compustat fundamentals with market identifiers from CRSP. The monthly stock
file supplies monthly stock-level data for all securities listed on U.S. exchanges. We export
both datasets directly from WRDS and cover the period from 1998 to 2024. The merge
yields a panel of U.S. publicly listed firms containing monthly observations of returns,
market capitalization and key balance sheet variables.

                                      6
   To ensure consistency across analyses, we restrict the sample to common shares, use
main exchanges and incorporate delisting returns to capture total returns. We ensure each
observation to be uniquely identified by PERMNO, GVKEY and CIK, which enables the
one-to-one linkage back to the Interbrand brand-parent mappings to build the strong
brand portfolio. To ensure the exclusion of Interbrand firms in order to create the weak
brand portfolio, we flag all Interbrand firms in the code with 1 and all others with 0. We
also sort every entry into one of ten self-defined overarching industry groups by match-
ing the firms by their NAICS-code. These entail Industrials, Consumer Discretionary,
Financials, Consumer Staples, Business Services, Communication Services, Technology,
Materials, Energy & Utilities and Healthcare. We perform a similar sort for classifying
each company in the stock universe after its dominant business model with the help of
the NAICS-code. Lastly, we also add the current market excess rate and risk-free rate
for all timestamps by considering the 1-month US T-bill rate. The data for the risk-free
rate comes from the official Fama French Library, which uses the ICE BofA US 1-Month
Treasury Bill Index.

3 Methodology and Theoretical Framework

We make use of three complementary downside risk measures to get a broad perspective
of downside risk. First, we use downside beta as defined by Ang, Chen, and Xing (2006).
They show that beta can be divided up into upside and downside beta. Downside beta
captures how strongly a portfolio or firm responds to market movements in months with
negative market excess return. We calculate it using an 18-month rolling window in order
to always be able to have the most precise estimate of downside beta, especially for the
crisis analysis which we perform. As the underlying model, we use the Fama-French six-
factor model, so that the beta we consider will be the downside market beta adjusted for
the other systematic risk factors. Second, we consider downside excess return, that is the
equity return minus the risk-free rate conditional on months when the market excess return

                                      11
is negative. We define equity return as the total return earned by stakeholders through
dividends yield and capital gains (Berk and DeMarzo, 2024). We acknowledge this not
being a theoretically ideal way of testing for risk, but it shows relative risk for investors or
managers. Third, we measure downside risk using a conditional semi-variance. In general,
semi-variance is defined as the downside variance of returns and calculated as the expected
squared shortfall below the mean (Estrada, 2002). We adjust this general definition as we
are interested in months when the market excess return is negative. Therefore, we treat
semi-variance as the average squared negative deviation of the portfolio’s excess return
from the market excess return in those months. This now captures the part of volatility
that investors are most concerned about. Here again, we acknowledge that this method
does not fully translate to risk, but can validly be used as an estimate. Because downside
risk can manifest in different ways and each measure captures a distinct aspect of negative
return behaviour, we use all three measures to ensure that our results are not driven by
one single definition.
   In order to measure brand equity in this paper, we will follow the approach presented
by Boustanifar and Kang (2025). As they have shown that previous brand measures
present imprecise results and their approach resembles economic reality, we see their
approach as state-of-the-art in measuring brand equity. Therefore, we adopt an output-
based approach in our study by using Interbrand’s annual ranking as our primary measure
of brand equity.
   We create a portfolio from the Interbrand data, which we call our strong brand port-
folio. This combines all brands and their respective public parent companies listed in the
Interbrand annual rankings into one portfolio. As Interbrand publishes its ranking once
a year in October, we assume the composition of the strong brand portfolio to remain
constant from November until the end of October of the following year. We choose this
method, as the strong brand portfolio can be understood to be tradeable starting Novem-
ber each year. From this, both yearly equal-weighted and value-weighted versions of the
portfolio are constructed. We construct the value-weighted portfolio based on market
capitalization.
   To provide a meaningful benchmark and a portfolio to compare, we also construct a
weak brand portfolio. This portfolio consists of all US-listed firms in our stock universe
except for the public parent companies that hold brands listed in the Interbrand data. As
this entails almost all stock-listed firms on the US market, we also refer to this portfolio
as our market portfolio. Since we construct this weak brand portfolio through all parent
companies not considered in the Interbrand ranking, the composition changes similarly
on a yearly basis. We also create both yearly equal-weighted and value-weighted versions
of the portfolio. This ensures direct comparability of the strong brand and weak brand
portfolios.
    Portfolio-based analysis is a central tool in empirical asset pricing, and characteristic-
sorted portfolios such as those introduced by Fama and French (1993) are widely used
to reduce noise and reveal systematic return patterns. We acknowledge that portfolio
analysis has some limitations such as possible misleading results due to portfolios’ strong
covariance structure making many models overstate fit (Lewellen, Nagel, and Shanken,
2010). Therefore, we complement our analysis with firm-level models.
   In order to control for systematic market risks in our study, we rely on the Fama-
French Five Factor model extended with the momentum factor. Fama and French (1993,
2015) show that firm size, value orientation, profitability and investment behaviour ex-

                                      12
plain important cross-sectional differences in returns. The momentum factor introduced
by Carhart (1997) captures that firms with strong recent performance tend to continue
outperforming in the short run.
   Before proceeding with our own analyses, we start by replicating the main empirical
result of Boustanifar and Kang (2025), namely the existence of a brand premium. This
replication allows us to validate the quality and structure of our dataset. We deem this
to be necessary as matching the brands with both their respective parent companies
changing over the years and matching them with database identifiers is a manual process.
By confirming the results, we establish a solid base for our subsequent analysis of brand
equity and downside risk.
   Now we will turn towards our main regressions. Using both portfolio-level and firm-

level analysis, as well as three downside risk measures , allows us to closely evaluate how
brand equity relates to downside risk under different market environments. Econometri-
cally, our testing entails the following:

   H1. A portfolio of firms with stronger brand equity exhibits a different level of down-
side risk than the overall market portfolio.

   The first relationship framework (Figure 5) focuses on the link between the Strong
Brands Portfolio (SBP) and downside risk. As we treat brand equity as a core strategic
asset that might be able to explain variation in downside risk, we see SBP as an ap-
propriate explanatory variable. We begin by examining the full sample period to assess
whether strong brands are associated with lower downside risk. If brand equity exhibits
resilience, we expect the strong brand portfolio to exhibit weaker sensitivity to negative
market movements and smaller losses in bad states. Crisis periods play a central role
in shaping how firms behave in adverse markets and they may meaningfully alter the

                                      14
relationship between brand equity and downside risk. We therefore then add to H1 and
test whether crises have a moderating effect, such that the behaviour of portfolios differs
in crisis periods compared with downside months outside the defined crisis periods.
   The second relationship framework (Figure 6) examines Strong Brand Firms (SBF)
at the firm-level. The SBF variable mirrors the logic behind SBP but instead classifies
strong brands at the firm-level rather than on the portfolio-level. This allows us to study
whether the relationship between brand equity and downside risk is similar at the firm-
level. On the firm-level, we also implement industry fixed effects to mitigate concerns
that industry-specific risk exposure, sectoral cyclicality or technological intensity could
bias the estimated coefficients. We first test H1 and H2 again, but now at the firm-level.
The aim here is to establish if the relationship is the same once firm-level heterogeneity
is taken into account. Furthermore, we show whether another moderator influences the
relationship significantly. In order to do this, we first test the association between business
model and industry. We expect the relation to be strong and thus proceed by focusing on
business model as another moderator and include our industry classifications as controls
to avoid multicollinearity.
   Our approach relies on understanding what generates variation in brand equity and
downside risk. This means ensuring that this variation is not driven by unobserved factors.
The key identifying variation in both SBP and SBF is generated by Interbrand’s ranking
methodology (A.3). The structural differences embedded in this methodology determine
which firms appear in the rankings and therefore generate the β1 in both relationship
frameworks.
   At the same time, downside risk is influenced by several factors unrelated to brand
equity. We believe that these include industry cyclicality, financial structure, macroeco-
nomic sensitivities and idiosyncratic firm events. These elements form the economic con-
tent of the error term in both portfolio- and firm-level regressions. To mitigate omitted-
variable concerns, we incorporate standard asset-pricing controls. Both downside beta
and downside excess returns are estimated using the Fama-French six-factor model. At
the firm-level, industry fixed effects further absorb sector-specific risk exposure and tech-
nological intensity, while crisis dummies isolate the influence of major macroeconomic
episodes. These controls make it more plausible that unobserved downside risk factors
are not systematically correlated with brand equity.
   Thereafter, we conduct several robustness tests to verify that our empirical results
are not driven by model choices or classification decisions. We re-estimate our baseline
and crisis models using alternative full-year crisis definitions to see whether our baseline
definitions overlook any effects. Also, we test if our results hold when removing the
assumption of holding the Interbrand rankings from 2000 steady for 1998 and 1999. As a
final robustness check, we conduct a further analysis based on an alternative measure of
brand equity introduced by Boustanifar and Kang (2025). We construct new strong brand
portfolios, both value-weighted and equal-weighted, based on the firms that appear most

We run the same type of analyses as with the Interbrand data and compare the results

our relationship.
   For hybrid firms, the pattern differs. The hybrid dummy is positive and significant,

                                      23
and the interaction between strong brands and hybrid firms is also positive and signifi-
cant. This indicates that hybrid firms tend to have higher downside beta, and that strong
brand status is associated with even greater downside sensitivity within this group. This
holds even when adding the main strong brand coefficient. Hybrid firms therefore appear
to exhibit the highest downside exposure in this measure of risk. A possible explanation
is explained by Markides and Charitou (2004). They argue that dual business models
can create internal tension and strategic misalignment when firms rely on different cus-
tomer logics or operating processes. Their work shows that managing two models within
the same organisation is often associated with conflicting priorities, inconsistent resource
allocation and difficulty sustaining a clear strategic direction.
   B2C firms act as the omitted variable category in our model. The strong brand
coefficient reflects the association between brand equity and downside beta within B2C
firms. This coefficient is negative and significant, indicating that strong brand B2C firms
tend to exhibit lower downside beta than weak brand B2C firms. This pattern suggests
that brand equity is associated with greater downside stability for consumer-facing firms.
   The significant interaction term of hybrid for downside beta allows us to reject the
null hypothesis. We find that business model moderates the relationship between brand
equity and downside risk in a meaningful way.
    For the other risk measures, the baseline coefficient for strong brand B2C firms follows
the same significant and directional pattern. For downside excess return, having a strong
B2B firm is able to offset the risk of having a business-facing strategy, which was not
possible for downside beta. This is because we are now able to significantly incorporate
our moderating effect into our total effect of having a B2B strategy. Moving on to hybrid,
we see a similar result as in downside beta, although with a different reasoning. Having a
weak brand hybrid firm is associated with a lower risk than having a strong brand hybrid
firm. Inherently, a weak hybrid model does not have a different downside risk than
following a B2C strategy since the coefficient is insignificant. Regarding the hypothesis
testing, we observe the same results as under downside beta.
   Having a B2B when using semi-variance as a risk measure, we see the same direction
as the downside beta. However, now we observe significant results for the baseline, the
B2B coefficient and the B2B interaction term. Together, we associate strong brand B2B
firms with a lower downside risk than weak brand B2B firms. Inherently, having a weak
brand B2B firms is associated with a higher downside risk, while we can observe that
strong brand B2B firms exhibit lower downside risk. Furthermore, we observe the same
effects of having a hybrid strategy as in the downside beta case. Lastly, for semi-variance,
we are able to reject the null hypothesis.

5 Discussion and Conclusion

Our findings reveal that brand equity’s relationship with downside risk is conditional
rather than universal. This challenges the conventional view of strong brands as uni-
formly defensive assets. For the average firm, reflected in equal-weighted portfolios and
firm-level regressions, we find that strong brands tend to face lower downside risk across
most measures. This pattern likely stems from customer loyalty, pricing power and re-
duced information asymmetry, providing cash flow stability during downside markets.
However, this risk-minimization effect weakens or reverses for large-cap firms in value-
weighted portfolios. Here, strong brand portfolios exhibit higher downside beta and lower

                                      31
excess returns. A plausible explanation for this is that larger firms are more exposed to
institutional rebalancing and liquidity pressures in crises. This might outweigh any fun-
damental brand advantages. In parallel, we observe heterogeneous effects across different
crisis types and varying impact on the relationship between downside risk and brand eq-
uity. Given that crises emerge from different underlying mechanisms, this makes sense as
their effects are unlikely to be uniform across firms. Alongside this, our business model
analysis reveals that the business model affects the relationship between downside risk
and brand equity. B2B firms with strong brands tend to remain inherently riskier than
B2C firms during downside markets, even after controlling for brand equity. We appre-
hend that the mechanism through which brands provide some sort of downside protection
operates differently in business-to-business contexts where purchasing decisions are driven
by rational cost analyses and contract structures rather than consumer sentiment. These
findings indicate that brand equity is an asset whose risk-mitigating properties depend
on weighting schemes, market context and the nature of customer relationships.
   A potential rationale for why strong B2B brands might be inherently riskier than
strong B2C brands, based on our interpretation, is that B2B firms appear more exposed
to customer concentration. We understand this structure as creating a situation in which
revenues are highly dependent on a limited set of buyers, which could, in theory, amplify
vulnerability to idiosyncratic shocks such as client loss or contract renegotiation. Fur-
thermore, we interpret purchasing behavior in B2B markets as more rational, which offers
less scope for the kind of emotional brand attachment that may help stabilize demand
in consumer markets. From this perspective, the value of brand equity in B2B contexts
seems more contingent on performance-based assessments and cost-benefit calculations,
which could be especially exposed during periods of economic stress or organizational
change.
   A possible way to understand why different crises affect firms and brands differently
is to assume that each crisis is driven by distinct mechanisms that translate into different
types of pressures on firms. For example, the DotCom crash can be interpreted as a crisis
rooted in valuation dynamics and sector-specific speculation. This may have made firms
connected to technology and high-growth sectors particularly sensitive to rapid changes in
market sentiment. By contrast, the Global Financial Crisis appears to have been driven
by credit shortages and liquidity constraints, which we assume could have disproportion-
ately affected firms that rely on external financing or that operate in environments where
access to credit is crucial for day-to-day operations. Energy-related crises, on the other
hand, seem to be linked to commodity price shocks and regional dependencies. This
potentially exposes firms located in or dependent on energy-sensitive markets to cost
volatility and supply uncertainty. Under this interpretation, the type of mechanism at
play may determine which firms are more exposed to adverse outcomes during a crisis.
Crises that suppress demand might be assumed to affect firms with more elastic demand
or those dependent on discretionary spending. On the other hand, supply-side or finan-
cially driven crises might place greater strain on firms that face input cost variability or
short-term funding requirements. In this sense, we interpret crisis effects as contingent
on the underlying mechanism, with different crises likely impacting different sets of firms
rather than exerting a uniform influence across the market.
   Assessing the robustness of our findings, we see that the main patterns remain direc-
tionally consistent, although downside beta and semi-variance in value-weighted portfolios
are sensitive to the Interbrand ranking assumption. Additionally, we find similar results

                                      32
when using another output measure of brands, which means that we can associate value-
weighted portfolios with higher risk, while equal-weighted portfolios are associated with
lower risk. Importantly, this still depends on the measure of downside risk. For the crisis
moderation, we can conclude that the results are consistent for downside excess return
and semi-variance. However, for downside beta, we now observe that the equal-weighted
portfolio exhibits crises as a moderating effect while the value-weighted portfolio does
not. The different results shown by our alternative brand measure reinforce the difficulty
of measuring brands.
    Interpreting our findings in relation to existing theory highlights several nuances rela-
tive to prior research. In our analysis for downside excess returns, we show partly similar
results compared to Boustanifar and Kang (2025). They document a strong brand pre-
mium in excess return averaged over longer periods, whereas we can now support this
by finding that strong brands in equal-weighted portfolios have higher excess returns in
downside markets. This is also the case for our downside excess return in our firm-analysis.
However, our results contradict when it comes to the value-weighted portfolio. We cannot
support value-weighted portfolios having higher excess returns in our analysis of down-
side periods. A possible explanation for coexistence of our finding with Boustanifar and
Kang (2025) could be that value-weighted portfolios perform stronger in upside market
periods, offsetting the underperformance during downside market turns. Another possi-
ble explanation could be the structure of the value-weighted portfolios, which concentrate
weight on the largest firms in the market. These large-cap strong brand firms tend to
move closely with aggregate market dynamics, and their returns exhibit a high degree of
sensitivity to broad macro conditions. Further research, such as Rego et al. (2009), links
brand equity to lower exposure to adverse conditions, but our results also indicate that
such associations are not universal. Instead, the strength and direction of the statisti-
cal relationships vary across downside risk measures, crisis environments, firm size and
business model context.
   Moreover, looking at downside market periods separately deepens our understanding
of brand equity by showing how it performs in both average and critical conditions. Our
results for downside market beta offer further insight into the underlying mechanisms.
By examining how strong brands co-move with the market, specifically during negative
periods, we better understand whether brand equity provides resilience through reduced
sensitivity to adverse market shocks. An interesting pattern emerges when comparing
downside beta with realized downside performance. Although downside beta is the only
measure that does not rely on a relative risk definition, it generally yields weaker statistical
evidence for brand-related effects compared to the realized measures. This suggests that
sensitivity to systematic downside movements does not align well with the channels high-
lighted in the theoretical literature, whereas downside excess returns and semi-variance
reflect these channels more consistently.
   Our crisis-related findings reinforce earlier research by showing that the impact of a
crisis is not universal but depends on its underlying structural mechanisms. The firm-
level analysis reveals a clearer and stronger effect, indicating that crisis dynamics operate
primarily at the firm-level and that portfolio aggregation can mask meaningful hetero-
geneity. These results are consistent with the conclusions of Claessens and Kose (2013).
They argue that crises differ in their fundamental features and therefore generate hetero-
geneous outcomes across firms. For example, when considering the downside measures, we
observe that for the Energy Shock, we associate the crisis moderation as an increased risk

                                      33
for value-weighted portfolios using downside excess return and for semi-variance value-
weighted portfolios it is vice versa. Comparing within the downside beta measure, we
observe that the GFC interaction term shows an increased risk for the value-weighted
portfolio, but we cannot interpret the equal-weighted portfolio. At last, we observe that
the firm-level shows that the DotCom crisis moderates across all three downside measures,
while the portfolio analysis does not show a consistent moderating effect.
   The business model results mirror distinctions emphasised in marketing research. Prior
research documents systematic differences between B2C, B2B and hybrid contexts (Ulaga
and Eggert, 2006; Lindgreen and Wynstra, 2005; Vargo and Lusch, 2004; Kowalkowski,
Kindstr¨om, and Brehmer, 2011) and our estimates follow these broad patterns. Having a
strong brand as a B2C company can be related to lower downside risk, consistent with the
role that emotional attachment and customer-level loyalty play in consumer markets. In
B2B settings, our results align with research emphasizing that value creation relies more on
contractual relationships and service performance. Interestingly though, we would expect
B2B brands to show lower downside risk, given the trust-based and relationship-driven
nature of B2B markets (Ulaga and Eggert, 2006). While strong B2B brands do indeed
have lower downside risk than weak B2B brands, B2B firms as a whole remain riskier
than B2C firms. This suggests that core B2B market characteristics still shape downside
risk, even when brand equity is taken into account. Considering a hybrid strategy, the
mixed results we find are broadly compatible with the mentioned suggestion that mixed
customer orientations may dilute brand-related effects.
   These findings also have implications for investors and managers. For investors, the
results indicate that strong brands should not be treated as a universal defensive charac-
teristic. Downside resilience varies with firm size, crisis mechanism and business model,
and large-cap strong brand firms often move closely with the market. This means that
value-weighted brand-based portfolio tilts are unlikely to offer reliable downside protec-
tion. Firm-level fundamentals provide a more informative basis for evaluating downside
exposure. Relative to downside beta, downside excess returns and semi-variance exhibit
stronger empirical signals, particularly during crises when realized performance may be
more relevant than systematic exposure.
   For managers, the results show that brand building remains important, but its risk-
related benefits are conditional. However, having a strong brand and following a B2C
strategy can be associated with lower downside risk. Following a B2C strategy is also
safer than incorporating a B2B or hybrid strategy in our context. The crisis-specific
results also suggest that resilience varies by shock type, meaning that brand strategy
should be aligned with the mechanisms most relevant to each firm’s environment rather
than assumed to operate uniformly across downturns.
   We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, brand equity is inherently
difficult to measure and available metrics serve as proxies rather than complete repre-
sentations of brand equity. Second, although the model diagnostics indicate violations of
several OLS assumptions, we mitigate these through the use of HAC standard errors and
robustness checks. Third, although WRDS and Interbrand are established and reliable
data sources, they present practical challenges. The Interbrand dataset is not directly
compatible with financial databases such as WRDS, requiring substantial manual match-
ing of brands to firm identifiers, which is labour-intensive and potentially prone to human
error. Constructing a consistent time series across multiple years also adds operational
complexity that may affect sample size and coverage. These challenges do not undermine

                                      34
the reliability of the data but highlight limitations related to accessibility, integration and
measurement consistency. Finally, although our crisis windows and downside risk mea-
sures follow established research, they necessarily simplify complex events and cannot
fully capture the organizational or market-level mechanisms influencing resilience.
    These limitations point toward several avenues for future research. One direction is to
test alternative crisis windows to determine whether brand resilience is most pronounced
at the onset, peak or recovery phase of a shock. Another is to extend the analysis to
non-US markets to examine how variation in regulation, consumer behavior or financial
structure affects the brand-risk relationship. Finally, incorporating qualitative dimen-
sions, such as leadership traits or strategic responses during downturns, could deepen
the understanding of how brand equity interacts with broader organizational behavior in
crisis contexts.

Table 12 reports a set of diagnostic tests for the portfolio-level regressions related to H1.
The Durbin-Watson statistics suggest either no autocorrelation or only mild, therefore
acceptable levels. However, the Ljung-Box tests show a consistent rejection of the null of
uncorrelated residuals across downside risk measures and weighting schemes, indicating
persistent serial dependence. The Breusch-Pagan test reveals heteroscedasticity in several
models, most notably for downside beta in the equal-weighted specification. Importantly,
heteroscedasticity does not imply that linear models are inappropriate per se. Rather,
it indicates that the variance of the residuals changes with the predictor, which can
arise when the relationship between variables differs by subgroup or context. In some
cases, such patterns reflect a moderating effect, where a third variable interacts with the
independent variable so that the effect size (beta) is not constant, but varies depending
on the moderator.
   The Anderson-Darling test uniformly rejects normality, which indicates non-normal
residuals rather than normally distributed errors. However, given our large sample size,
the central limit theorem mitigates the practical consequences. Lastly, all VIF values fall
well below conventional thresholds, implying no issues with multicollinearity. Together,
these results show that several OLS assumptions are violated, primarily concerning in-
dependence, homoscedasticity, and normality, which can affect the validity of standard
errors. To address autocorrelation and heteroscedasticity, we use HAC standard errors,
which provide robust inference under these conditions.

                                      40
   One of the core OLS assumptions is linearity. In practice, this requires a continu-
ous relationship between predictors and the dependent variable. In our case, however, all
predictors are dummy variables, which only take values 0 and 1, leading to inherently step-
wise, rather than continuous, relationships. This makes traditional linearity diagnostics
neither meaningful nor informative. While this could theoretically limit the interpretation
of linearity in our models, we mitigate such concerns by applying alternative measures and
definitions of downside risk as robustness checks, supporting the stability of our results.

performance of branded products and services, the extent to which the brand influences
customer purchase decisions, and the overall strength of the brand, which reflects its
ability to generate future earnings that can be attributed directly to the brand itself.

of brand equity and an appropriate proxy for identifying firms that possess strong and

A.6 Alternative Brand Measure Method Extension

A.6.1 Expanded Data Description and Methodology

As a further robustness check, we use Boustanifar and Kang’s (2025) text-based measure.
We count the amount of times the word “brand” or similar is used in the annual filings
of U.S. listed companies. The underlying logic is that if a brand would be a core element
of a business’s strategy, it would appear more often in the filings of the company.
   We introduce multiple enhancements of the measure in order to improve accuracy. This
includes that we will not only search through the reports based on the keyword “brand”,
but we will also consider “brands”, “branded”, “branding”, “trademark”, “trademarks”,
“label”, “logo” and “logos”. We treat these terms as being similar to the word brand
and assume them to have a similar effect. As another control, we also consider both
the entire filing and the business section of the filing separately and normalize by 10,000
words for both. We do this as we understand that one of the biggest downsides of this
measure might be the overinterpretation of these terms used or them just being named
as legal terms. A third adjustment we make is that companies that include the word
“brand” in their business name will be excluded from this measure, as this would lead to
an overinterpretation.
    We collect this data with a webscraping file that reads the annual filings of all entries
in our stock universe. From this data, we then create yearly strong brand portfolio out
of the leading 100 firms with the highest brand counts and run the same analyses as for
the Interbrand portfolio.

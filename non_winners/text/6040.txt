It’s Raining Yen: A Study on the Household Response to the Japanese Special Cash
Payments

Abstract: This study analyses the savings, investment, and debt repayment responses of Japanese
households to the Special Cash Payments (SCP) distributed as part of the government's COVID-
19 economic relief measures in 2020. While previous Japanese studies have focused on the
marginal propensity to consume from the SCP, this paper provides novel insights into households'
broader financial decisions following an unanticipated positive income shock. Using monthly
panel data from the Family Income and Expenditure Survey across 52 cities in Japan, I employ an
instrumental variable approach to estimate the proportion of the SCP used for saving, investment,
and debt repayment. I find that on average, households demonstrated a strong propensity to save
the SCP funds, a reduced propensity for debt repayment in response to the SCP and an insignificant
investment response. In contrast, households with liquidity constraints showed an increased
propensity to save, invest and repay debt compared to unconstrained households, suggesting
heterogeneous effects based on liquidity constraints.

Keywords: Positive Income Shock, Fiscal Stimulus, Special Cash Payments, Household
Behavior, Saving Behavior, Debt Repayment Behavior, Consumption Behavior, Income-Based,
Liquidity Constraints, Heterogeneous Effects

 1. Introduction

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Japanese government initiated the "Emergency Economic
Measures for Response to COVID-19”. This stimulus package included “Special Cash Transfers”
(SCP) of 100,000 Yen for all individuals, irrespective of age, income level, employment status etc.
This was part of a broader global trend where governments introduced similar initiatives to
alleviate the effects of the economic slowdown. Notable examples include the US CARES Act,
which provided one-time payments of $1200 per, household, while similar measures were also
adopted by South Korea, Hong Kong, and Singapore.

From an economic perspective, the SCP can be seen as a temporary and unanticipated positive
income shock. Baker, Farrokhnia, Meyer, Pagel, & Yannelis (2023) highlight that the primary aim
of such fiscal packages is to “stimulate the economy through a multiplier effect”: increased
household consumption should lead to an increase in production, and employment and so on. It
follows that the success of direct cash transfers' in stimulating the economy depends upon a
household’s marginal propensity to consume out of the transfer (Barro, 1989). However, existing
literature highlights that there is significant nuance to the question of efficacy.

The Japanese case of SCP makes for a very interesting study due to various reasons. First, a variety
of literature looking at the impact of the receipt of the SCP finds that the marginal propensity to
consume out of the SCP was relatively low. Secondly, while literature exploring the SCP has
predominantly focused on its impact on consumption patterns, there exists a notable gap in
research on its effects on savings, investment, and debt. This stands in contrast to studies conducted
in other countries where similar policies have been introduced, indicating a need for further
exploration of these financial aspects in the Japanese context.

Lastly, Japan's macroeconomic trajectory amid the pandemic diverged notably from that of other
developed nations. Utilizing the OECD countries as a comparative bench, Japan's GDP exhibited
a modest decline during the initial two quarters of 2020 when this policy initiative was enacted,
contrasting starkly with the sharper downturn experienced by many OECD counterparts (see
Appendix A). Furthermore, the unemployment rate saw only a marginal uptick to 2.8% (from
2.35% in 2019) which was lower than the pre-pandemic levels observed in most OECD countries
(see Appendix B). In light of this surface-level comparison, it is evident that the situation in Japan
was not as harshly affected (economically) as the other countries, and a study of Japanese
households’ response to the SCP in this context becomes very interesting.

To build on and address the gap in the literature, this study analyses Japanese households' savings,
investment, and debt responses to the receipt of Special Cash Payments (SCP). I utilize monthly
FIES survey data from the Statistics Bureau of Japan to construct a panel of 52 cities across 2020
in Japan. For the main estimation, I utilize an instrumental variable regression to estimate the
amount transferred to each household, following which I estimate the proportion of the SCP that

                                      2
the households saved, invested, and used to pay down the mortgage. Furthermore, I expand upon
my general estimation by evaluating the variability in responses among the top quartile and bottom
quartile based on disposable income to investigate the differential response to the receipt of the
SCP.

Through the course of this investigation, I found that on average households across Japan
demonstrated a strong propensity to save, a reduced propensity for debt repayment, and an
insignificant investment response. Additionally, in line with existing literature that notes a
differential response to positive income shocks based on liquidity constraints, I present evidence
of a significant differential response: households the in bottom quartile showed an increased
propensity to save, invest and repay debt more in comparison to households in the top quartile.

 2. Institutional Background

In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, on the 20th of April 2020, the Japanese cabinet approved
the “Emergency Economic Measures for Response to COVID-19”. This extensive plan allocated
117 trillion yen towards economic recovery, equivalent to over 20% of the GDP at that time. The
plan targeted businesses and households, and this paper particularly looks at the payment of
100,000 yen to all Japanese residents in the form of ‘Special Cash Payments’. Although the brief
lacked specific targets or objectives, its overarching aim was to help households maintain their
“lifestyle” during the crisis.

These cash payments were largely akin to the direct transfers made to individuals in the US under
the CARES Act, which was passed on the 25th of March 2020 (Baker et al., 2023). However,
unlike the US payments, the amount disbursed to individuals in Japan was uniform across all
recipients regardless of age, income, employment status, etc. All individuals, including foreigners,
registered in the Basic Registration System before the 27th of April 2020 were eligible to receive
this 100 000-yen payment (Appendix C).

The Japanese government left the implementation of the SCP up to each municipality and therefore
there is a variation in acceptance windows due to differences in administrative capacity. However,
the application windows across the country lasted for three months and as a rule of thumb,
households received their payments two weeks after they applied. To claim the 100,000 yen,
residents were required to apply to their municipality’s offices during the application period, which
would confirm their eligibility before processing the transaction.

 3. Related literature

Several studies collectively suggest that these direct transfer policies have the potential to stimulate
aggregate demand but that their effectiveness depends upon liquidity constraints faced by
households/individuals and the severity of the crises. Johnson, Parker, & Souleles (2006) and
Baker et al. (2023), studying the impact of the positive income shocks, emphasize increased
spending among households facing liquidity constraints. Similarly, Agarwal, S., Liu, C., &
Souleles, N. S. (2007) focus on the impact of 2001 tax rebates in the US, showing consumption
smoothing behaviours with households saving around two-thirds of rebates. In contrast, Parker
Soulless, Johnson & McClelland (2013), studying direct transfers following the 2008 recession in
the US, highlights a high Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC) signalling successful fiscal
policy outcomes. Misra & Surico (2014) discuss these differences and attribute the higher MPC
during 2008 to more severe economic conditions and stricter liquidity constraints.

Complementing this literature, Bayer, Born, Luetticke & Müller (2023) quantify the multiplier
effect, reporting that for unconditional transfers, like those enacted under the CARES Act, the
effect was 0.25, compared to approximately 1.5 for conditional transfers. In line with these results,
Aaronson, Agarwal, & French (2012) also find evidence that targeted fiscal policies lead to a
higher MPC out of stimulus amount.

Another aspect influencing the efficacy of these programs is individuals' prior information about
them. Baugh, Ben-David, Park, & Parker (2018): note that households spend more out of an

                                      4
unanticipated income shock than from an expected income shock, suggesting a stronger
asymmetry for households with liquidity constraints. Furthermore, these stimulus checks affect
more than just consumption. In a natural experiment conducted in Singapore, Agarwal & Qian
(2014) analysed direct cash payments made by the Singaporean Government, noting that a
significant portion of that payment was used to pay down outstanding debt, particularly credit
cards and other unsecured debt. Furthermore, they found that individuals with lower liquid asset
holdings/ higher debt levels exhibited a stronger propensity to pay down debt in response to the
income shock. Finally, in the first study of its kind, Greenwood, Laarits & Wurgler (2023) analyse
the effect of the US CARES stimulus check in the US on retail investor trading activity, finding a
sharp increase in trading activity and share price of what they call “retail dominated portfolios”.

Summarily, the body of literature concludes that the propensity to consume in response to an
income shock depends on the severity of the crises, household liquidity constraints, and the
information available to households. Relatedly, while the efficacy of these programmes depends
upon boosting consumption, the funds from these income shocks can be utilized for savings,
investments and paying down debt.

Having noted the results from the general body of literature, we can turn to literature that studies
similar programmes in Japan. Specifically, in a study of the same fiscal stimulus Hattori, Komura,
and Unayama (2021) estimate the MPC out of the Special Cash Payments (SCP) to be
approximately 10%, with slight variations based on model specifications. Similarly, Kaneda,
Kubota, & Tanaka (2021) utilize proprietary data from Money Forward (financial services app)
and estimate the cumulative MPC in the six weeks following receipt to range from 6% to 27%,
with their benchmark specification putting the MPC at 16%. In stark contrast, Kubota, Onishi, &
Toyama (2020), found the estimated MPC to be roughly 50%, using data from 2.8 million bank
accounts. Referring to Kubota et al. (2020), Hattori et al. (2021) suggest that the difference in the
results stems from the use of ATM withdrawals and bank transfers as proxies for consumption.

Similarly, Hsieh, Shimizutani, & Hori (2010) investigated the impact of a similar program from
1999 where “shopping coupons” worth 20 000 Yen were provided to families with children and
elderly. Like the SCP program, the key idea was that these would stimulate consumption. Results
from their estimations, however, showed an MPC between 10% and 20%. Hattori et al. (2021) also
report in their paper that a study by the Japanese government looking at the MPC following similar
cash payments in 2009 found the MPC to be 8% in the month of receipt. With the exception of
Kubota, Onishi, and Toyama (2020), the findings from the literature suggest that cash transfers did
not lead to a notable increase in consumption.

Besides the obvious question of the efficacy of these programmes, the question remains of how
the money is used by the households. To that end, this paper aims to contribute to the existing
literature by looking at the proportion of money – saved, invested, and used to pay down the

                                      5
mortgage. These contributions, by going beyond the MPC, provided a more holistic overview of
Japanese household's financial decisions in response to the unanticipated positive income shock,
that was the Special Cash Payments.

 4. Data & Methodology

   4.1 FIES Survey Data

This study utilizes monthly data on savings, investments, and liabilities sourced from the Family
Income and Expenditure Survey (FIES) collected by the Japanese Bureau of Statistics. The survey
has a sample size of 9000 households with two or more members, representing about 96.5% of the
total households, selected randomly to ensure that the sample is representative of the entire
population. Notably, this dataset maintains a yearly standard error rate of 0.4% and is used by
governmental entities in policy formulation. Furthermore, Hattori et al. (2021) highlight that it is
widely regarded as one of the most reliable data sets for household data in Japan.

In the empirical analysis, I focus on dependent variables: the net increase in savings ("貯蓄純増
"), net purchase of securities ("有価証券純購入"), and net decrease in land and house debt ("土
地家屋借金純減"). Sourced from the survey, these variables represent flow variables, quantifying
the net amount (¥) saved, invested, and paid to reduce land and mortgage debt in a particular
month. The savings variable captures deposits to financial institutions, the investment variable
captures the money used to purchase stocks, bonds and money paid into trusts (including fees) and
the debt variable captures money paid towards housing finance loans.

The key explanatory variable in all empirical models is "Other Special Income" ("特別収入"),
which captures receipts other than earned income, including receipts from special cash payments.
For some households, it may include revenue from miscellaneous sources, such as consolation
payments, settlement, and compensation funds. This broad scope may introduce bias and lead to
the contamination of the independent variable. To address this issue, this study employs the
instrumental variable approach recommended by Hattori et al. (2021), as will be elaborated in the
upcoming section. Furthermore, in line with Hattori et al. (2021), this study uses changes in the
number of household members and homeownership rate as the control variables.

Using this monthly data, I construct a panel dataset comprising 52 cities across Japan with data
from January 2015 to December 2020. I modify the variables by subtracting their average value
for month i from 2015 to 2019 from the corresponding 2020 value for the same month. By using
the deviation from the average, I eliminate seasonal variations and long-term trends, which enables
me to isolate and better analyse the response SCP. The choice of years is motivated by this period
(2015 to 2019) being the most recent time frame during which Japan did not experience any

                                      6
significant economic shocks. Furthermore, this modification is largely in line with Hattori et al.
(2021) who make use of the same dataset to estimate the MPC following the payment of the SCP.
Following this modification, I am left with observations for all 12 months of 2020 for all 52 cities
in the FIES dataset for a total of 624 observations. Table 1 presents the summary statistics for the

Leveraging the panel data structure, I incorporate city-fixed effects to account for time-invariant,
city-specific factors like local health policies and varying infection rates across cities. Some
specifications also include monthly fixed effects to control for monthly-level variations such as
common policy effects and macroeconomic trends across all entities. Despite these additions,
certain variables that exhibit time-entity variation need to be explicitly controlled for.

2020 values and the average of 2015-2019 values across all specifications. Additionally, I
introduce income tax as a control variable. The rationale is that income tax should serve as a proxy
for labour income which has a direct impact on the dependent variables of interest in this study.
Note that the control variables vary over time, with their average values changing across months
and cities. This implies that city-fixed effects cannot be fully controlled for them. Similarly, as
these variables change across entities, suggesting time-fixed effects alone cannot effectively
control for them either.

                                     10
would indicate that in response to a unit increase in income, savings increase by 50%. In the
context of this study, assuming that my model controls for all confounding factors, it would imply
a more direct relationship: 50% of the additional income, SCP in this context, is saved. Conversely,
a negative sign would show a reduction in the propensity to save.

As mentioned in section 4.1, it is worth noting that the “Other Special Income” variable could
include a variety of non-recurring income receipts besides the Special Cash Payments. These other
receipts would lead to the contamination of our explanatory variable. To reduce this contamination,
I use the difference in 2020 value and the average value from 2015-2019. The logic follows that
since there were no similar fiscal stimuli from 2015-2019, the average values for each month
should capture the part of the “Other Special Income” that is not the SCP. Notwithstanding this
modification, there might still be elements specific to 2020 in the variable which might lead to
contamination and would introduce a bias in the estimation if they coincide with the dependent
variable.

Furthermore, the timing of SCP disbursement, contingent on households' application submissions,
could coincide with periods of heightened savings. For example, the implementation of COVID-
related restrictions in Japan might prompt individuals to stay indoors and save the money they
would have otherwise spent on discretionary expenses. The following concerns point to potential
endogeneity issues with the 𝛥𝑆𝑝𝑒𝑐𝑖𝑎𝑙 𝐼𝑛𝑐𝑜𝑚𝑒𝑖,𝑡 variable. To address these concerns and avoid
biased estimates, I adopt the instrumental variable approach suggested by Hattori et al. (2021).

As such, I use the timing of the payment of the SCP as an instrumental variable. The instrumental
variable is constructed by taking the ratio of the “Number of Receive Days” to the “Total Days”.
“Number of Receive Days” estimates the period after the application opens in a prefecture when
households might get their payment. This span begins two weeks after the application starts and
extends to six weeks afterwards, creating a 28-day (4-week) period for potential payment receipt.
“Total Days” in the month, on the other hand, are just the sum of total days in the months when
households could receive the payment. Consider the following example, the application starting

date for Osaka city in Osaka Prefecture was the 25  of May, two weeks after that date would be
      th                                                         th
the 8  of June and six weeks after that date would be 6  of July. Then, the ratio for May would be

Assuming that households submit their applications shortly after the opening of the application,
this instrument has a robust theoretical foundation. Since we know that the SCP is transferred to
the households two weeks after applying, the starting date of accepting the application is highly
correlated with when the households receive their payment. In other words, under the assumption
that households apply shortly after the opening of the application, the starting date of the
application affects when households receive their payments. Followingly, the starting date of the
application is dependent on the administrative capacity of the prefecture and thus is exogenous. In

6. Robustness

The conclusiveness of findings in a study is contingent upon the robustness of its methodology
and results. Given the strong pre-existing academic foundations of the methods employed in this
study, our focus shifts to the finer details that contribute to its robustness. The data used in this
study was randomly collected by Japan's bureau and is used for policymaking, suggesting that it
is representative of the entire population, thereby mitigating the risk of sample selection bias.
Moreover, the estimated results align with the figures reported in the summary statistics, further
reinforcing the study's consistency.

To address potential endogeneity issues arising from contaminated or noisy data, I employ an
instrumental variable approach in my specifications, as explained earlier. The strength of an
instrumental variable is assessed using the F statistic, which, as a rule of thumb, should exceed a
value of 10. Notably, the reported F statistic for the first stage of the regression shows that for each
specification in Table 1, the F stat surpasses this threshold by a considerable margin, underscoring
the appropriateness and effectiveness of the chosen instrumental variable.

Furthermore, the results from regressions, in Table 1 largely align with the findings of Hattori et
al. (2021) with the slight differences in estimations can be attributed to this study’s addition of a
control variable. All in all, results resemble those of related literature, adopting proven methods
and specifications.

7. Limitations

Having mentioned some measures of robustness, this section highlights some limitations of the
study. First, there might be some relevant concerns about the sample size in some of the model
specifications that this study employs. Specifically, talking about the specifications with a subset
of data from June to August, the sample size in the heterogeneous effects models is as low as 39,
which might lead to erroneous estimates. Secondly, owing to data availability limitations, this
study uses data for working households with two or more people. This leaves out data on
households that are unemployed and single-people households. The exclusion of unemployed
households is critical to acknowledge as this means that their response cannot be captured when
studying the heterogeneous response to the receipt of SCP. Thirdly, land and mortgage debt was
used as a proxy to measure the “change in debt” variable. This excludes other forms of debt such

                                     25
as credit card debt, that have been used by other papers in the literature. It is imperative to
acknowledge that this could lead to different estimates, depending on what forms of debt are
included. Lastly, due to a lack of significant effects on lags, this paper contributes an understanding
of the most immediate response to the SCP, excluding the households’ long-term reaction to the
SCP.

8. Discussion

The outcomes of the general estimation suggest that, on average, across Japan, households
demonstrated an increased propensity to save, saving in the range of 80% and 95% of the SCP, as
their immediate response to the receipt. On the flip side, at least initially, they demonstrated a
reduced propensity to pay down debt and invest. These results align closely with existing academic
literature from Japan, which suggests that the Marginal Propensity to Consume (MPC) in response
to SCP was relatively low or comparable to pre-pandemic levels (Hattori et al., 2021; Kaneda et
al., 2021). Cumulatively, this suggests that in the case of the SCP, the alternative to consumption
in this case was savings, at least initially.

Focusing specifically on the high savings rate, there is a large proportion of literature that my
results align with. Pistaferri (1999) who investigates the Permanent Income Hypothesis in the
context of income shocks, suggests that contingent on expectations about future changes in
income, households respond to a transitory positive income shock by saving a majority of the
income, which is also the case with the SCP. Similarly, Agarwal et al. (2007) study of the 2001
tax rebates in the US where they find that households saved up to two-thirds of rebates initially.
In line with these studies, the high savings response can be seen as a consumption-smoothing
response by households.

In contrast, the reduced propensity to pay down debt and invest is a little more complex to argue
for, which I come back to later in the section. However, at this point, it might be prudent to turn to
the heterogeneity in response to the receipt between the top quartile of the population and the
bottom quartile of the population. They demonstrate differing reactions to the SCP: in comparison
to the top quartile, the bottom quartile saved more, invested more and increased debt repayment in
response to the receipt of the SCP. This is noteworthy as Japanese academic research showed a
similar variation in the consumption responses based on income levels. Kaneda et al. (2021) found
that higher earners (top quartile) had a marginal propensity to consume (MPC) of 10-15%, while
lower earners (bottom quartile) had an MPC of about 20% in response to Special Cash Payments
(SCP).

The findings from Kaneda et al. (2021)’s papers allow for a more holistic understanding of
response. While these results might seem a little counterintuitive at first glance, consider that

                                     26
savings, debt, investment, and consumption are collectively exhaustive as long as the estimates
collectively do not exceed the amount paid in the SCP. Having mentioned that it is not possible to
make that estimation considering that consumption is not in the scope of this paper and that using
the estimates of Kaneda et al. (2021) paper would not be prudent because of the difference in data
and methodology. A hypothetical explanation could be made by, clubbing consumption and debt
payments as “money used” and savings and investments as money put away, it stands the results
suggest households in the bottom quartile, in immediate response, used the money in line as
required per their liquidity constraints while putting way the remainder as a consumption
smoothing response.

Furthermore, focusing specifically on debt repayments, findings from this paper find support in
both Agarwal et al. (2007) and Agarwal et al. (2014). On the flip side, there is little academic
support for the higher investment and savings response to the receipt of the SCP.

Interpreting the policy implications, based on the results presents some complexity. If we were to
consider that the goal of the stimulus was solely to boost economic growth by stimulating
aggregate demand, then the policy seemed to have missed its mark as highlighted by the literature
on consumption. On the flip side, if we read the government’s espoused goal of helping households
“maintain their lifestyle” in a literal sense as providing aid to households beyond consumption
then it can stand to reason that it was most successful in doing that for households with constraints.
Regardless of the interpretation of the goal, it is evident that a more targeted policy would have
been appropriate.

With that said, it is important to note that this study presents results limited to the immediate
response of the households (within the first 4 weeks of receipt). In that light, the high savings in
the initial periods may be substituted for consumption in the following periods. Results from this
paper do not find a significant estimate for lags which implies that it is not possible to comment
on the relationship between the dependent variables and SCP for the following periods.

9. Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides novel insights into how Japanese households allocated the
Special Cash Payments (SCP) received during the COVID-19 pandemic. Utilising household
survey data and an instrumental variable approach, the findings reveal that on average, households
demonstrated a strong propensity to save the SCP funds and a reduced propensity to pay down
debt, while the investment response was insignificant. Notably, there was a differential response
based on liquidity constraints – the bottom quartile of households tended to save and invest more
of the transfer, as well as use the transfer money for debt repayment. Finally, assessing the

                                     27
implications of these findings, I suggest that policies directly targeting households with liquidity
constraints would have been more appropriate in this situation.

This analysis makes an important contribution to extant literature by going beyond the commonly
studied impact on consumption patterns and instead shedding light on other financial outcomes
like savings, investment, and debt repayment. Moving forward, several avenues can be explored
to build upon these findings. First, an investigation into reasons for strong savings and investment
response by the Japanese households in the bottom quartile is in contrast to what existing literature
has found. A similar investigation can also be made into the results of the general estimation, where
I find a reduced propensity to pay down debt. Second, investigate the underlying reasons for the
differential responses across income groups, such as differences in liquidity constraints,
precautionary savings motives, or debt burdens, in the Japanese context. Third, examining the
longer-term impacts of the SCP transfers on household balance sheets and financial well-being
would be beneficial.

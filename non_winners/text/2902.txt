                                          Abstract

The purpose of this  paper is to examine  the relationship between capital structure and capital
structure determinants for Swedish SMEs during  financial instability. The study will focus on the
2008 financial crisis, using data from 2005-2014, which is divided into three separate periods for
analysis.  The unbalanced panel data set includes observations for 10,713 firms.  The determinants
of capital structure used in this study are firm’s  size, tangibility of assets, profitability, liquidity,
non-debt tax  shields and growth potential. The study uses the theoretical framework of the
pecking order theory and the trade-off theory as a base analysis point for the empirical data. We
find significant results indicating that firm’s size, tangibility  of assets, liquidity and growth
potential  are positively related to firm’s capital structure. Profitability indicates a  significant
negative relationship to  firm’s  capital structure.  Tangibility and profitability exert  a relatively
strong influence on  capital structure compared to the other determinants. Overall we find that
trade-off theory and pecking order theory are applicable for Swedish SMEs’ capital structure
decisions during the 2008 financial crisis. Our findings are in accordance with majority of
previous studies and provide valuable  country specific data.

Keywords:  Capital Structure Determinants,  Leverage,  Financial Crisis, Swedish SMEs

1.     Introduction

Capital structure  is of  importance for  companies’  potential profitability and solvency. The
distinguished authors Modigliani and Miller (1958) are considered to have developed the modern
perspective of capital structure. They state that,  under strict assumptions, capital structure is
irrelevant for  the value of a company. With the  relaxation of certain assumptions,  such as the
absence of tax, their findings become more applicable to real  firms  and factors influencing capital
structure   can   be   identified.   Two   dominant   theories,   static   trade-off   theory   (Kraus   and
Litzenberger, 1973) and  the pecking order theory  (Myers and Majluf, 1984),  can  help explain how
managers determine a company’s  optimal  capital structure.
    These theories  are predominantly tested on U.S. data samples. Rajan and Zingales  (1995)
used  an international  data set where  they found that the extent to which firms are levered  in
different countries are  fairly similar. However,  complex  adjustments had to be made  in order to
take  into  account  for  differences  between  countries,  such  as  accounting  regulations  and
institutional environments, which impact leverage decisions. More recent studies have also stated
that capital structures  differ between industries and countries, emphasizing the importance of
country specific data (Zietlow  et al.,  2007; Sibilkov, 2009).
    The aim of this study is to empirically examine the  relationship between Swedish small and
medium-sized  enterprises’  (SME)  capital structure and their determinants during the 2008
Financial Crisis, testing  if the static trade-off theory and pecking order theory  hold.  The
determinants of capital structure to be studied are firm’s size, tangibility of assets, profitability,
liquidity, non-debt tax shields and growth potential. These determinants are  the  most commonly
used in previous literature, allowing for comparison.
    Empirical research  during periods of  financial and economic instability  is important because
companies are acting under different circumstances, and it cannot be assumed that all previous
relationships  that have been  established will hold.  Previous literature  has also focused  on large
public U.S. companies  therefore we wish to study  Swedish  SMEs, which  account for majority of
Swedish companies. SMEs  have more restricted access to funding compared to large public
companies,  making it  vital to  test if capital structure theories hold for  SMEs.  It is also important
to provide country specific data due to recent findings of capital structure determinants differing
between countries  (Zietlow  et al.,  2007; Sibilkov, 2009).  The  findings  can aid in predicting firms’
future capital structure during financial instability.
    Our  results indicate that the static  trade-off theory  and pecking order theory have strong
explanatory values for Swedish SMEs prior to and during  the 2008  Financial Crisis.  We find size,
tangibility of assets, liquidity, non-debt tax shields and growth potential to have  a positive

                                                                          3
relationship to long-term  leverage. Profitability shows  a negative relationship to long-term
leverage.  The results indicate  that firm’s tangibility of assets and profitability  have the highest
explanatory power for an SME’s choice of  capital structure.  All  determinants experienced  similar
relationship  to long term leverage  before  and during the crisis, but their explanatory value on
capital structure changed.  Tangibility showed  an unexpected decreased influence on  leverage
during the crisis.
    The rest of the paper will consist of five  parts. Firstly, we  introduce the 2008 financial crisis
as well as the theories of capital structure. Secondly, we describe  the  chosen  determinants,
relevant previous literature and our hypothesis.  Thirdly, we  describe our data and present
summary statistics  for the data sample, together with  our empirical approach.  After this  we
present and  discuss our results as well as their robustness. Lastly, we summarise and conclude our
findings, as well as suggest future  potential research.

2.     Sweden and the  2008  Financial Crisis

The 2008 Global Financial Crisis provides  an opportunity  to study how capital structure  is
affected by financial instability and  to  test the robustness of the  static trade-off theory  and the
pecking order theory. The bursting of the U.S. housing bubble and the vast presence of subprime
loans resulted in U.S. securities tied to real estate taking a big loss and creating an unease in the
market. The impact of the broken housing bubble was  further  magnified by Credit Default  Swaps
(CDS), resulting in  the Lehman Brother’s filing for bankruptcy, this  can be said to have  pushed
the financial unease into an acute financial crisis, leading to  a global  financial  recession.
    Sweden’s economy being a small and open economy with extensive international trade, was
substantially impacted by the financial and economic downturn. 48% of Sweden’s GDP in 2007
came from exports (World Bank, 2016), which explains how  the  global  economic downturn
could have a considerable impact on Sweden. Sweden managed the crisis but  still experienced a
large downturn. The fall in Sweden’s GDP during the crisis years of 2008 and 2009 was the
greatest recorded since 1931  (Hassler, 2010).  Sweden acted quickly to manage the crisis.  The
Riksbank supplied banks with loans of longer  maturities worth 450 billion SEK, in both SEK
and U.S. dollar  (Öberg, 2009). It also set up a temporary credit facility,  based on commercial
paper, where companies could fund their operations by issuing  commercial papers and selling
them  to banks  (Öberg, 2009). During the Crisis, the National Debt Office  met the surge in
demand for treasury bills, resulting in the issue of more than 190 billion SEK over the planned
loan programme  (Öberg, 2009). The government issued an increased maximum sum in deposit

                                                                          4
guarantee scheme, and expansionary fiscal policy among many other actions were taken to
manage the crisis  (Öberg, 2009).  It is obvious that the 2008 Global Financial Crisis had a severe
impact on Sweden, providing an interesting period to study determinants of  capital structure
during financial instability.

3.     Capital Structure Theory

With the term “capital structure” we are referring to how a company is financed. A company can
be financed through either debt, equity, or a mix of the two. In 1958, Modigliani  and Miller
introduced the irrelevancy proposition, claiming that capital structure was irrelevant to the value
of a company in a perfect market, thus initiating the modern theory of capital structure.  Their
model was highly criticised for being purely theoretical and not applicable to reality due to its
strict assumptions, which led  them to modify  their proposition in 1963. Modigliani and Miller
(1963) applied corporate taxes to their theory, presenting debt to have a positive  influence  on  the
value of a firm. This relationship derives from the fact that interest  costs from debt are tax
deductible, creating an interest tax  shield, which increases the value of the firm by decreasing the
taxes paid.  When a company’s value is increased due to debt, it is referred to as being leveraged,
because the debt leverages the value of the firm.
    Since Modigliani and Miller’s publications, vast research has been done on the subject  of
capital structure, and  capital structure can  today  be viewed through many different theoretical
lenses. This paper will mainly focus on the  static trade-off theory  (Kraus and Litzenberger, 1973)
and the pecking order theory  (Myers and Majluf, 1984), which will to some extent take into
account  agency costs  and asymmetric information  problems.

3.1     Static Trade-Off Theory

There are two types of trade-off theories regarding capital structure; dynamic and static. The
static trade-off theory  suggests  that a company sets a target leverage, determined by the balancing
of benefits versus costs  associated with debt, and  gradually moves towards its target (Myers,
1984).  All firms  have different target leverages as their individual characteristics may cause
different benefits and costs.
       We will apply the static trade-off theory in this paper, and  from  now on  refer to  it  as
trade-off theory.  With Modigliani and Miller’s recognition of corporate tax, it enabled a benefit
for debt, since interest  rates on debt are tax  deductible,  without there being an opposing cost to

                                                                          5
debt (Modgliani and Miller, 1963). This resulted in 100% debt financing  being theoretically
optimum. Consequently, Kraus and Litzenberger (1973) introduced  costs associated with debt,
such as financial distress and risk of  bankruptcy, counterweighing the benefits proposed by
Modgliani and Miller (1963). Thereby implying that the  optimum debt level is  a trade-off between
the benefit of interest tax  shields  and the increased costs  that follow  the increased debt (Kraus
and Litzenberger, 1973). The trade-off  theory has been further extended by Jensen and Meckling
(1976) whom went on to include agency costs. The concept of agency costs relate to the
principal-agent problem, where if both parties are value maximising it can be assumed that the
agent will not always act in a value maximising way for the principal (Jensen and Meckling, 1976).
In this case the agents  would be the managers, and the principals  would be the shareholders. The
shareholders can decrease discrepancies in the agent-principal relationship through the use of
incentives and applying monitoring costs  (Jensen and Meckling, 1976). Debt can act as a
monitoring cost as it decreases the  amount of  free cash flow that managers may use in non value
maximising ways for the principals. This relationship between debt and agency costs plays an
important role in finding the optimum leverage to maximise a company’s value. In this paper we
will also take into account non-debt tax shields as proposed by De Angelo and Masulis (1980).
De Angelo and Masulis proposed the inclusion of non-debt tax shields, for example depreciation
and investment tax credits, which act as  substitutes  to the interest tax  shields  (De Angelo and
Masulis, 1980). The non-debt tax shields provide an alternative way to increase a company’s value
by decreasing tax payments.

3.2     Pecking Order  Theory

The  pecking  order  theory  derives  from  Myers  (1984),  whom  initially  was  influenced  by
Donaldson (1961). It does not propose an optimal leverage like the  trade-off  theory  does, but
instead proposes a ranking of financing options. The theory suggests that managers prefer to use
retained earnings (internal  funds) over debt (external  funds), and debt before the issue of new
equity. This ranking is supported by the adverse selection model brought forth by Myers and
Majluf (1984). The underlying idea of the  financing  order; (1) retained earnings, (2)  debt  issuance,
(3)  equity  issuance, derives from the fallacy of asymmetric information. Managers have more
information about their company’s prospects, risks and value compared to external parties,
therefore external financing can be more expensive than internal. The increased costs are due to
external parties not having all  the  information and requiring a discount for the  risk associated
with  asymmetric information, which could result in current shareholders’  value being diluted. The

                                                                          6
dilution of current shareholders’  value  is an added cost and  internal funds are  therefore
prioritized when financing projects.

3.3     Financial Crises  and Capital Structure

Research regarding  the impact  financial instability  has  on capital structure  is  lacking. So is the
research for leverage and general business cycles. The research that does exist can be perceived as
fairly contradictory, leaving the relationship between leverage and macroeconomic downturns
quite ambiguous. Hackbarth  et al  (2006) whom researched the value maximising leverage ratio
during  different  stages  of  the  business  cycle  find  that  the  value  maximising  leverage  is
countercyclical, ergo it is higher during recessions. Bhamra  et al.  (2010) and Chen (2010) propose
pro-cyclicality  for leverage. These articles  apply slightly different assumptions, reducing their
comparability, but  the overall understanding we  gain is that  leverage  dynamics over business
cycles are  ambiguous as various studies conclude different results.
        A financial crisis can be predicted to have a more drastic effect on non-public SMEs
compared to larger public companies since they are restricted to fewer financing sources (Hall  et
al., 2004). During a financial crisis the volatility in  the markets increase drastically. This increased
volatility results in  an increased risk for creditors, which increases  cost of debt  for companies that
borrow. Smaller companies may also be more averse to debt due to that the risk is shared
between less investors in smaller companies (Proença  et al., 2014). This means that internal funds
can be assumed to become further  prioritized during the crisis.
        When considering  the trade-off theory  during a financial crisis it is found that SMEs have
a larger gap from their optimal capital structure compared to larger companies (Lo ́pez-Gracia and
Sogorb-Mira,  2008). This can be understood by the fact that SMEs face larger bankruptcy costs
and  resource restrictions, and  will  thus  have a lower leverage. This gap was significantly large
during the 2008  Financial Crisis, leading researchers to believe the pecking order theory was
more applicable to the analysis of companies’  capital structure decisions (Mostarac and Petrovic,
2013). The increased applicability of the pecking order theory  can be because the taxable income
of companies decreased during the crisis, and SMEs may even be affected to the extent that they
have no taxable income. This removes  the possibility to deduct the interest tax  shields,  thereby
removing  the  benefit of debt  financing, which is a major factor in the trade-off theory  (Lo ́pez-
Gracia and  Sogorb-Mira,  2008).
       Overall we predict a decrease in long-term debt  leverage  during the crisis period for
SMEs due to the increased costs of debt.  Internal funds  are predicted to be  prioritised as they are

                                                                          7
cheaper, and lower debt is obtained due to the  costs increasing relatively to the  benefits, as
argued by the pecking order theory and the trade-off theory respectively.  We therefore state the
following hypothesis:

                      H.1: “Long-term  leverage  should  decrease during the crisis period”

4.     Measures  of Capital Structure

In this study, book values are used for  the measurements of capital structure. This is most
common  in empirical finance even though financial theories are primarily based on the usage of
market values of debt and equity (Sweeney  et al.,  1997). Book values of debt are used to proxy
market values of debt due to the focus on non-public SME companies in Sweden, which implies
that market values for these firms do not exist. The usage of book values instead of market
values can be supported by Bowman (1980), whom stated  that market values can be represented
by book values.  We will use both long-term debt ratio (LTDR) and  short-term  debt ratio (STDR)
as measurements for capital structures.  LTDR is defined as long-term debt over total assets and
STDR is defined as short-term debt over total assets.  This  paper will  focus  on  the relationship
between the capital structure determinants and  LTDR,  since it is  most commonly used in
previous studies and is considered to best represent capital structure.

5.     Determinants of Capital Structure

5.1     Firm  Size  (SIZE)

Large firms are usually more diversified in their business compared to smaller firms, implying a
lower volatility in earnings  (Titman and Wessels, 1988). More constant earnings decrease the risk
of not meeting debt obligations and the risk of bankruptcy (Warner, 1977; Frank and Goyal,
2009). This implies lower costs associated with debt financing as the risk of  financial distress
decreases and lower interest rates are therefore likely (Frank and Goyal, 2009; Ozkan, 2001). As a
consequence,  higher levels of leverage are favourable for larger firms to fully utilize  its benefit
(Titman and Wessel, 1998; Frank and Goyal, 2009; Deesomsak  et al., 2004).
        The agency theory  further explains  the relationship  between size and leverage, since larger
firms face more agency costs (Jensen and Meckling, 1976). With higher amounts of debt, external

                                                                          8
stakeholders are monitoring managers more closely, decreasing agency costs. This suggests
leverage to be positively correlated with size according to agency theory.
       Pecking order theory also influences the correlation between leverage and size, through
the information asymmetry perspective. Small firms are monitored less and do not publish as
thorough financial reports as  done by  larger companies, and are therefore more exposed to
information asymmetry problems (Hall  et al., 2004). This limits their possibility to raise  long-term
debt compared to larger firms (Hall  et al., 2004).
       The positive correlation between  long-term  leverage and the company size is consistent
with an extensive amount of previous studies (Titman and Wessel, 1988; Rajan and Zingales,
1995; Hall  et al., 2004; Frank and Goyal, 2009; Proença  et al., 2014). For  STDR, previous studies
have suggested the opposite  relationship. Small firms might exploit the usage of  short-term  debt
in the absence of raising  long-term  debt. Thereby a negative correlation between  STDR  and size
is assumed (Hall  et al., 2004; Proença  et al., 2014). Following previous literature we use the natural
logarithm of total assets as proxy for the size determinant (SIZE) (e.g.  Rajan and Zingales, 1995;
Deesomsak  et al., 2004; Frank and Goyal, 2009; Harrison and Widjaja, 2014; Proença  et al., 2014).
We state the following hypotheses:

                       H2.a: “Size should have a positive relation to  long-term  leverage”
                      H2.b: “Size should have a negative relation to  short-term  leverage”

5.2     Tangibility  of Assets  (TANG)

The  tangibility of a company’s assets  as a determinant of capital structure relates to its value as
potential collateral and its impact on agency costs. Majority of capital structure theories suggest
that  a company’s asset structure will impact the choice of  capital structure. Myers and Majluf
(1984) argue that  this is due to increased costs of issuing debt brought on by asymmetric
information in the principal-agent relationship. Managers of a company have more information
than lenders regarding a project,  and  it is therefore hard for lenders to evaluate a project’s value.
This lack of security results in an increased risk for the lender, and as can be explained by the
Capital Allocation Pricing Model, higher risk requires higher return from  the lenders perspective
(Galai and Masulis, 1976). A more tangible asset structure increases the collateral value, which is a
known and secure value for lenders. As lenders collateralize their lending they reduce their risk
and thereby reduce the required return, which  reduce  the financial distress cost for the company.
Managers are also believed to have an incentive to shift risk from equity holders to debt holders,
known as risk shifting (Myers, 1977). The use of collateral allows lenders to restrict a company’s

                                                                          9
use of borrowed funds to specific projects (Myers, 1977). If no collateral is available, lenders may
demand more favourable terms,  increasing the company’s costs. The company may not be able
to raise debt since the company is assumed to take on riskier projects, maximising their own
return on the creditors expense (Jensen and Meckling, 1976).  This predicts a positive relationship
to be found.
       On the contrary to what has been previously stated, Grossman and Hart (1982) argue
that companies with less collateralized capital will raise higher levels of debt in order to monitor
their managers, preventing the excess consumption of prerequisites and activities that may not
maximise the stockholder’s value. They argue that these types of agency costs are greater in less
collateralized companies due to it  being harder to monitor their capital outlay  (Grossman and
Hart, 1982). Therefore,  debt is raised in order to increase the monitoring from external parties, as
well as the increasing  debt obligations  will reduce managers’  unnecessary spending.
        The asset structure of a company relates to  the  trade-off theory  as the reduced costs
argued by Myers and Majluf (1984), Jensen and Meckling (1976) and Galai and Masulis (1976),
allow further debt to be taken on in order to fully maximise  its benefit, reaching an optimal
balance between the benefits and costs. Grossman and Hart (1982) argue the opposite, that the
issue of debt is a method to decrease agency costs, and not the  result of decreased asymmetric
information  brought on from tangible assets  acting  as collateral. They propose that the increased
cost of bankruptcy from the debt is  overcome  by the reduced agency cost from managers
(Grossman and Hart, 1982). Therefore, Grossman and Hart  (1982)  believe companies with less
collateral will take on more debt.
       Previous empirical studies on capital structure determinants have dominantly found
tangibility to have a positive effect on a company’s  LTDR  (Rajan and Zingales, 1995;  Hall  et al.,
2004; Frank and Goyal, 2009;  Harrison and Widjaja, 2014; Proença  et al., 2014;  Zhang and Mirza,
2015). It has also been found that  STDR  has a negative relationship with tangibility (Hall  et al.,
2004; Hwang  et al., 2013; Proença  et al., 2014; Zhang and Mirza, 2015). This can be explained by
the theory that debt length is matched with the investment horizon (Myers, 1977). Fixed tangible
assets are intended for longer time horizons, ergo relatively more  long-term  debt is issued in a
company with  a  larger fraction of  fixed  tangible  assets. We predict that firms  with greater
tangibility will take on more debt, thereby a positive correlation between tangibility and LTDR is
expected, and a negative correlation between tangibility and STDR.
       We will use the ratio between tangible fixed assets and total assets  as a proxy for
tangibility  (TANG)  (as done in Rajan and Zingales, 1995; Deesomsak  et al., 2004; Harrison and
Widjaja, 2014; Hall  et al., 2004).  We state  the following hypotheses:

                                                                         10
                   H3.a: “Tangibility should  have a positive relation to  long-term  leverage”
                  H3.b: “Tangibility should  have a negative relation to  short-term  leverage”

5.3    Profitability  (PROF)

With regards to the  trade-off theory, companies will prefer to use as much leverage as possible,
up to the point where costs outweigh the benefits (Kraus and Litzenberger, 1973). Firms with
higher profitability will face lower risk of financial distress and therefore also lower costs
associated with debt financing. This implies that more profitable firms would be able to use
higher  amounts  of  debt  before  reaching  the  break-even  point  (Frank  and  Goyal,  2009.
Furthermore, firms with higher profitability are assumed to face free cash flow problems, e.g.
unnecessary spending on perquisites. They would thereby gain from the payment obligations and
external monitoring that  comes with debt financing (Jensen, 1986). Profitability is therefore
predicted to be positively correlated with debt, from a trade-off theory perspective.
       In contrast, most recent studies have focused on pecking order theory  when studying the
relationship  between leverage and profitability. The core of the pecking order theory suggests
that firms prefer to finance their business with internally generated funds over raising external
capital (Myers and Majluf, 1984). Pecking order theory focusing  on the costs associated with
information asymmetry,  suggests that  more profitable firms  will avoid expensive debt financing
since they  have access to more internal capital. The pecking order theory thereby suggests
leverage to be negatively correlated to profitability. This inverse relationship between leverage
and profitability have been shown in majority of  previous empirical studies and give explanatory
power to the pecking order theory (Titman and Wessels, 1988; Rajan and Zingales, 1995; Ozkan,
2001; Deesomsak  et  al., 2004; Proença  et al., 2014). Following  majority of these studies, we use
earnings before interest, taxes and depreciation (EBITD) over total assets as the proxy for
profitability (PROF). We predict a negative correlation to be found between leverage and
profitability, as argued by the pecking order theory.

                  H4.a: “Profitability should have a negative relation to  long-term  leverage”
                  H4.b: “Profitability should have a negative relation to  short-term  leverage”

                                                                         11
5.4     Liquidity  (LIQ)

A firm’s liquidity ratio measures the firm’s capability to pay off its short-term liabilities, defined as
the ratio of current assets to current liabilities. The trade-off theory and the pecking order theory
provide different views on the relation between leverage and liquidity. Firms with sufficient
liquidity can be assumed to have a lower risk of financial distress and bankruptcy, since they are
able to  cover their obligations easier. Therefore,  they face less costs associated with debt
financing (Frank and Goyal, 2009). Trade-off theory argues that this will increase a  firm’s usage
of debt and  suggests a positive relation between  leverage and liquidity (Kraus and Litzenberger,
1973). However, firms with more liquid assets can use  these  for financing. Since  the pecking
order  theory favours internal funding, it argues that firms with higher liquidity will use it for
funding instead of raising debt  (Harrison and Widjaja, 2014). This means that a negative
relationship between leverage and liquidity would be expected when referring  to the pecking
order theory.
        Previous empirical studies have shown that liquidity has a negative relation to all leverage
measurements, supporting pecking order theory (Ozkan, 2001; Deesomsak  et al., 2004; Harrison
and Widjaja, 2014). In contradiction to  these studies, Proença  et al.  (2014) found that Portuguese
SMEs’ had a positive relation between liquidity and  long-term  debt, which can be explained by
trade-off  theory. The ratio of current assets to current liabilities is consistently used in previous
literature as  the proxy  for liquidity  (LIQ)  and will be  used  in this study (Ozkan, 2001; Deesomsak
et al., 2004; Harrison and Widjaja, 2014; Proença  et al., 2014).  The following hypotheses for
liquidity are stated:

                   H5.a: “Liquidity should have a negative relation to  long-term  leverage”
                   H5.b: “Liquidity should have a negative relation to  short-term  leverage”

5.5     Non-Debt Tax Shields  (NDTS)

De Angelo and Masulis (1980) continued Modgliani and Miller’s research on tax shields,
introducing posts that create  beneficial tax affects, increasing a company’s value through what
they call non-debt tax shields (NDTS). With the term “NDTS” we are referring to  expenses
other than interest from  debt  that are tax  deductible,  such as depreciation and investment tax
credits. By noting that companies have other alternatives than debt to decrease taxes, it is
suggested that debt and NDTS are substitutes,  resulting in NDTS being  negatively related to debt
because  they would replace each other (De Angelo and Masulis, 1980). If  the use of NDTS

                                                                         12
reduces the potential benefit of interest tax shields, an inverse relationship between NDTS and
leverage is assumed to be found. During the crisis period we expect an increased explanatory
value for NDTS as the increased cost of borrowing and increased bankruptcy costs should lead
to companies looking for alternative methods in order to minimise taxes (Deesomsak  et al., 2004).
       Empirical studies have found varied results as to how NDTS impacts a company’s
capital structure. Bradley  et al.  (1984) whom were among the first to empirically test De Angelo
and Masulis’ suggestion, found a positive relationship between NDTS and leverage when
defining NDTS as depreciation and investment tax credits over EBITD. This can be explained
by the fact that depreciation may also act as a proxy for growth potential and asset structure
(Barclay and Smith, 1995).  The  greater a company’s depreciation ratio is, the more tangible fixed
assets it will have and arguably more  collateral and  growth potential. Therefore,  it can be
suggested that a larger depreciation ratio will be positively related to leverage. Empirical studies
have also resulted in a negative relationship being observed when defining NDTS as depreciation
over total assets (Deesomsak  et al., 2004). Titman and Wessel (1988) note that the use of
depreciation and investment tax credits over total assets neglects other tax beneficial expenses
that aren’t associated with capital equipment, such as research and development. Titman and
Wessel (1988) find statistically significant results indicating a negative relationship between
NDTS and leverage, which is consistent with De Angelo and Masulis’  claim. However, they are
sceptical to their findings, because they  found that the coefficient’s sign changes depending  on
how  the variables used as indicators are scaled.
        We follow previous studies in their use of depreciation over total assets as a proxy for
NDTS, from which we expect a negative relation between NDTS and LTDR  (Titman and
Wessel, 1988; Deesomsak  et al., 2004; Proença  et al., 2014; Zhang and Mirza, 2015),  and a positive
relation  between NDTS and STDR (Proença  et al., 2014; Zhang and Mirza, 2015).

              H6.a: “Non-debt tax  shields  should  have a negative relation to  long-term  leverage”
              H6.b: “Non-debt tax  shields  should  have a positive  relation to  short-term  leverage”

5.6    Growth  Potential  (GROWTH)

Large growth opportunities can at first be believed to increase debt as it will exhaust internal
funds and demand external financing to utilise the potential. Ross (1977) claims that the high
growth will signal to lenders  that the company will not default  and will  reduce their risk  and the
company’s cost of debt. This allows  further debt to be taken on according to  the trade-off theory.
However, Myers (1977) proposes that leverage is negatively related to the company’s  growth

                                                                         13
opportunities due to the  increased moral hazard. Companies in high growth industries are said to
have higher agency costs because they have more opportunities to shift risk onto the creditors
(Titman and Wessel, 1988; Booth  et al., 2001). It relates to the conflict stated by Jensen and
Meckling (1976) where the creditors are not able to benefit from the entire gain but they do take
on all of the cost, therefore they may require a higher premium on  loans. Especially during high
growth phases, smaller entrepreneurs will be willing to take higher risks and increase risk for
lenders (Myers, 1977) The increased agency costs makes issuing debt expensive, and as suggested
by the pecking order theory, companies may turn to internal funds for financing their growth.
       The growth opportunities  classify as capital assets, but these assets are intangible and
cannot be used as collateral (Titman and Wessel, 1988). They will increase the value of the firm
but if the company faces bankruptcy the value will rapidly decrease, suggesting  more volatility
(Williamson, 1988; Myers, 1984; Harris and Raviv, 1990). The increased  volatility raises the  costs
of debt, which is  predicted to  decrease leverage according to  the  trade-off  theory.
        Majority of empirical studies use market value of assets over book value of assets as a
proxy for  growth  potential  (Barclay and Smith, 1995; and Rajan and Zingales, 1995; Ozkan, 2001;
Deesomsak, 2004; Frank and Goyal, 2009). We do  not have  access to companies’  market values
because we focus on non-public  firms. We will instead use the percentage change in sales
compared to the previous period as our proxy for growth  potential  (GROWTH), which has been
done in for example  Proença  et al.  (2014).
       Empirical studies have found both negative (Harrison and Widjaja 2014; Ozkan, 2001;
Hall  et al., 2004; Zhang and Mirza, 2015) and positive (Proença  et al., 2014) correlations between
growth  potential  and LTDR.
      We predict a  negative relationship between GROWTH  and  LTDR  because of the costs
discussed above. Especially because of our focus on SMEs, which is argued by Myers (1977) to
bring on higher agency costs. A positive relationship is expected between growth and  STDR
because of the increased need for  non-current assets when the firm is growing and becoming
larger.

6.     Data  and Methodology

6.1     Data

All the data for this paper have been collected from the Serrano database, which provides
information about Swedish companies gathered from the Swedish Companies Registration Office
(Bolagsverket). The database contains annual financial data from the companies’ balance sheets,
income statements and other supplemental information about the companies.
       The  data extracted from Serrano contains information from over 500.000 firms for each
year from 1998 to 2014. The extracted data was  in the form of unbalanced panel data since e.g.
companies have been registered or gone bankrupt during the sample period and have thereby not
provided  information for some of the years.  The specific variables used from Serrano can be seen
in Appendix A.
   Our sample dataset will run between  years  2005  and  2014. The following criteria have been
used to create the sample dataset used for the regressions and analyses:

                                                                         16
    1.   Since the study focuses on the capital structures for SMEs, standards from the European
       Commission  (2015)  have been used to distinguish those companies. These standards have
         been translated from EUR to SEK with the currency exchange rate of 9,2 SEK per EUR,

                                            1
       which is the historical average  (European Central Bank, 2016). This implies that the
       companies in the study will have:
           a.   More  than 10  employees,  but less than 250 employees.
          b.   An annual turnover higher than 18  MSEK but less than 460  MSEK,  and/or, a
              total value of the balance sheet larger than 18  MSEK,  but  less than 397  MSEK.
    2.   Financial companies (such as banks, private equity firms, insurance companies and leasing
       companies) and utility companies (such as electricity and gas companies) have been
       omitted. This is common when studying capital structures because these companies are
       regulated  differently  compared to  non-financial companies and  have different capital
        structure characteristics (Rajan and Zingales, 1995; Ozkan, 2001; Frank and Goyal, 2009).
    3.   Only non-public companies will be used, i.e. companies are not allowed to be listed on
       any stock exchange market. This has been decided because public companies have a
       greater access to capital, which askew the results.  It is also a fact that 99.7% of  companies
        in Sweden are non-public (Statistics Sweden, 2016) allowing us to focus on the majority.
       Furthermore, we only investigate independent companies and parent companies to avoid
        misrepresentative capital structures in subsidiaries. Subsidiaries might for example receive
        loans and group contributions from parent companies without regards to the company's
        determinants, which affects their capital structure.
    4.   All companies must have been active and have  total assets higher than zero, during the
       whole sample period.
    5.   All variables  used in the analyses must exist for every company in the dataset. Companies
      with missing values are  only dropped for the years  they miss values and kept the
       remaining years. This is due to that missing values are not expected to be randomly
       distributed and would therefore  bias the sample if the dataset was converted into a
        balanced  dataset.
    6.   We remove observations with  negative values for  long-term  debt,  short-term  debt and
       sales, as well as observations where leverage  ratios  are  higher than 1. As suggested by
         previous literature (e.g. Halling  et al., 2011).

6.2     Methodology

In the empirical  analysis of this paper, we  define two different dependent variables for leverage to
run our regressions on separately. Firstly,  LTDR is defined as long-term debt divided by total
assets. Similarly,  STDR is defined as short-term debt divided by  total assets. Our independent
variables are firm’s  size (SIZE) defined as the natural logarithm of sales;  tangibility of assets
(TANG) defined as total tangible fixed assets divided by total assets; profitability (PROF) defined
as EBITD divided by total assets; liquidity (LIQ) defined as total current assets divided by total
current liabilities;  non-debt tax shields (NTDS) defined as depreciation  divided by total assets and
growth  potential  (GROWTH) defined as the  percentage change in net sales  between year t and t-
1.  A crisis  period  variable (CrisD) is defined to separate the years  of the financial crisis period and
takes the value 1 during the years 2008-2010 and 0 otherwise. We also generate period  variables
for the pre-crisis  period  (2005-2007) and post-crisis  period  (2011-2014)  and for the full period
(2005-2014).  The crisis period is chosen based on previous studies analysing  the influence of the
2008 Financial Crisis on capital structures from other countries (e.g.  Zhang and Mirza, 2015;
Harrison and  Widjaja, 2014; Proença  et al., 2014). The year-by-year  definition of the periods are
further supported by Swedish unemployment statistics, used to indicate  the crisis period (Graph
I).   The impact of the crisis is clearly evident at the start of 2008 as seen by the relatively large
increase  in unemployment  from 6.3% in 2008 to 8.4% in 2009 (World Bank, 2016). During 2008-
2010, Sweden faced a negative growth in GDP, which it hadn’t experienced since the crisis in the
beginning of the 90s (World Bank, 2016).

6.3     Descriptive Statistics

The descriptive statistics  (table  VI) provide  evidence of the influence that the  2008 Financial
Crisis had on Swedish SMEs capital structures. A decrease in the mean value for both LTDR and

precaution towards leverage in the after-crisis period. The profitability determinant decreases
during the crisis period and increases in the post-crisis period and thereby shows the impact of
the  2008 Financial Crisis  on firms’  profitability.  It also supports our argument as to why pecking
order  theory  may  be  more  applicable  compared  to  trade-off  theory,  during  the  crisis.

7.2    SIZE

The size determinant is found to  have a positive and significant  impact on LTDR, during all
periods. The opposite relationship is found for STDR, where size is negatively correlated to
STDR, this is however not significant  before or  during the crisis  period. Hypothesis H2.a can
therefore  be confirmed and is consistent with previous findings (Hall  et al., 2004; Proença  et al.,
2014; Zhang and Mirza, 2015). The results  support hypothesis H2.b, but are only  significant  for
the post-crisis period.  Trade-off theory provides an explanation for  a positive relationship
between SIZE  and LTDR. Larger firms face lower costs related to debt financing and therefore
should use more debt in their capital structures. In contrast, firm size  can be explained to have a
negative relationship to STDR due to that long-term debt is more costly for smaller firms and
they therefore use relatively more short-term debt (Hall  et al., 2004; Proença  et al.,  2014). The
coefficient for SIZE  in relation to LTDR remains the same for the pre-crisis and crisis periods.

                                                                         25
The explanatory value  could be expected to increase during  the crisis period  since creditors will
be more conservative and primarily provide credit to larger firms with less risk of bankruptcy
(Deesomsak  et al., 2004; Trinh and Phuong, 2016).

7.3    TANG

As predicted, the tangibility determinant has a positive relationship with LTDR and a negative
relationship with STDR allowing us to accept hypothesis H3.a and H3.b. This indicates that
companies with more tangible asset structures will take on more leverage and are in less need for
short-term  debt. All coefficients have a strong statistic significance as is indicated by their
confidence level of 99 percent. The findings are consistent with  previous empirical studies
(Deesomsak  et al., 2004;  Hall  et al., 2004; Proença  et al., 2014;  Zhang and Mirza, 2015) and can be
explained by the  trade-off theory. Tangible fixed assets serve as collateral and ease the risk that
occurs between shareholders, thus reduces the agency costs (Jensen and Meckling, 1976). This
allows  firms with more tangible asset structures to issue more  long-term  debt, without the costs
outweighing the benefits. The results suggest that tangibility has a relatively strong influence on
leverage when compared to other determinants as seen by the magnitude of the coefficients,
indicating that the tangible assets serving as collateral is an important factor for companies to be
able to take on debt. The negative relationship between TANG and STDR can be explained by
the idea of matching assets life length with the length of the loan,  thus a company that has more
tangible fixed assets, which are assumed to have longer life lengths, will require relatively less
short term debt  (Myers, 1977).
        Surprisingly we find that tangibility has less of an influence on companies’  leverage during
the  crisis  period. The magnitude of the coefficient decreases by 8% during the crisis period,
however it should be noted that tangibility is still very large compared to the other determinants.
It was expected that the tangibility  coefficient would increase during the  crisis  period  as debt
becomes more expensive due to creditors requiring compensation for their increased risk and
may demand more collateral (Halling  et al., 2011). This has been found to be true in other studies
such  as Harrison and Widjaja (2014). The decrease is continued into the past crisis period. The
2008 Financial Crisis was a very unique crisis and has resulted in many unexpected scenarios. It
can be believed that this decrease in explanatory value for TANG may be due to the trade-off
theory loosing explanatory power due to companies having negative taxable income and
depleting the benefit of interest tax shields for those periods (Lo ́pez-Gracia  and Sogorb-Mira,
2008; Mostarac and Petrovic, 2013)

                                                                         26
7.4    PROF

The  coefficient of profitability is negative and significant for all periods. These results verify the
relationship we expected profitability to have on capital structures, and hypotheses H4.a and
H4.b are  confirmed. The negative relationship indicates that more profitable firms will have
relatively less debt in their capital structures. This confirms the hierarchical choice of financing
that Myers (1984) proposed as the pecking order theory, where firms prefer to finance their
operations with internal funds.  Firms with higher profitability can avoid external financing by
instead   using   their   earnings   to   finance   investments.   The   negative   relationship   between
profitability and leverage is supported by a majority of recent studies (Ozkan, 2001;  Deesomsak  et
al., 2004;  Hall  et al., 2004; Harrison and Widjaja, 2014; Proença  et al., 2014).  The explanatory value
of profitability weakens during the crisis period as it becomes more positive, approaching zero.  A
possible explanation is  the fact that most firms had  lower profitability during the crisis and it was
very volatile (Harrison and Widjaja, 2014). Firms were less willing to use  internal funds because
they had higher transaction costs as a result of the increased risk that  firms  may become  illiquid
since profits were fluctuating more. Thereby reducing its negative impact on leverage, which is in
accordance  with pecking order theory  (Harrison and Widjaja, 2014). In the post-crisis period the
profitability coefficients for both STDR and LTDR become more negative,  closer to its initial
value in the pre-crisis period.

7.5    LIQ

Liquidity is found to have a significant and positive relationship to LTDR before the crisis and
remains positive during the crisis  period  as well. For STDR the  relationship is the opposite,
liquidity has a significant negative correlation to STDR during all  periods. These results confirm
hypothesis H5.b,  which suggests a negative relationship between liquidity and STDR as was seen
in previous studies that have used STDR (Proença  et al., 2014; Zhang  and Mirza, 2015).
Hypothesis H5.a regarding liquidity and LTDR  cannot  be confirmed since the results show a
positive correlation. Although we didn’t expect a positive relationship between liquidity and
LTDR, previous studies have received the same relationship (Proença  et al., 2014; Zhang and
Mirza, 2015). Proença  et al.  (2014) argues that firms with higher liquidity have a better ability to
pay back their debt and therefore, from the trade-off theory perspective, can gain more from
higher levels of debt. For both  LTDR and STDR  the coefficients are almost the same  prior  to
and during  the  crisis period.  Larger  negative  changes in the liquidity coefficient are observed

                                                                         27
during the post-crisis period.  The decrease in its  relationship to LTDR may be due to companies
being more careful taking on loans.

7.6    NDTS

Non-debt tax shields  are  found to have a statistically significant positive relationship to  LTDR,
which results in us rejecting hypothesis H6.a.  Since  the determinant shows a positive relationship
to  STDR,  we find support for  hypothesis H6.b. However, this is  only for the pre-crisis period,
since the crisis and post-crisis periods  both  have negative  but insignificant  relationships  to STDR.
The NDTS  has the largest influence on LTDR  compared to all determinants we  analyse, but this
positive relationship contradicts De Angelo and Masulis’  proposition (1980). Companies with
non-debt tax shields  are predicted to have less leverage  since there are less benefits to be
achieved from the interest tax shield when other tax shields are used to deduct the taxable
income  (De Angelo and Masulis, 1980). Both positive (Bradley  et al.,  1984) and negative (Titman
and Wessels, 1988; Deesomsak  et al., 2004; Proença  et al., 2014; Zhang and Mirza, 2015)
relationships have been found between NDTS and LTDR. Majority of findings appear to
indicate a negative relationship, supporting De Angelo and Masulis’  (1980)  claim. However,  the
findings for NDTS should be interpreted cautiously as Titman and Wessels (1988) pointed out
that the coefficient sign could change depending on how the variables used as indicators are
scaled.  Barclay and Smith (1995) also suggest that the NDTS proxy,  depreciation over total
assets,  could be a proxy for tangibility as companies with greater depreciation can be assumed to
have more tangible fixed assets, thus  creating a mechanical positive relationship.  This is further
supported when looking at the strong correlation between NDTS and TANG (Table III)  The
NDTS decreases during the crisis period with 6%, which is similar to tangibility, enforcing the
suggestion that the proxy used may be measuring tangibility.

7.7    GROWTH

The growth  potential  determinant has a positive correlation to both LTDR and STDR,
contradicting our theoretical hypothesis for LTDR and  several  previous  studies  (Ozkan, 2001;
Deesomsak  et al., 2004; Harrison and Widjaja, 2014; Zhang and Mirza, 2015). Proença  et al.
(2014) and Hall  et al.  (2004) find the same positive relationship for both STDR and LTDR, but
Hall  et al.  (2004) lacks statistical significance for LTDR. We reject hypothesis H7.a and accept
H7.b. The results are significant but they are of a small magnitude, which means they have a
small impact on a firm’s  capital structure compared to other determinants, causing us to question

                                                                         28
their importance in capital structure decisions. It was expected that growth potential would have
a negative impact on capital structure due to the increased agency costs associated with growth
(Jensen and Meckling, 1976;  Myers, 1977). The positive relationship found could be explained  by
the fact that  increased growth potential signals to creditors that  the company has a smaller risk  to
default, thereby lowering the costs  (Ross, 1977).  This would  give a company more incentives  to
take on more debt, according to the trade-off theory.
       The growth  potential  determinant’s influence on capital structure decreases during the
crisis  period, which can be expected as the volatility and unpredictability brought on by the  crisis
removes any reliance on growth predictions. What was once believed to be too  big to fail, has
now failed, which can  put creditors in a scenario where no insecure predictions are made.
Therefore,  the growth potential  has less influence on capital structure decisions  during the crisis.
In the  post-crisis period we  see that the magnitude of the GROWTH  coefficient increases, clearly
marking that the decrease was associated with the crisis.

As argued in most of the previous studies on capital structure, the choice of determinants and
their proxies are very important since different measures might result in different outcomes
(Deesomsak  et al., 2004). This applies for both the dependent and independent variables. To
further assess the robustness of our results we have altered several of our independent variables’
proxies to check the implications it might have on our regressions.
       We start by replacing  the SIZE  proxy with the log of total sales  (initial proxy was  log of
total assets), which has  been used  less frequently  in previous studies of capital structure  (e.g.
Titman and Wessel, 1988; Ozkan, 2001). The result is that the  statistical  significance of the  SIZE
determinant decreases,  it has  less explanatory value  to leverage  and the determinant’s coefficient
sign changes  to negative  (Appendix C).  This is also seen in Ozkan (2001),  whom argues  the
inverse   relationship   it   is   due   to   larger   companies   having   lower   degrees   of   asymmetric

                                                                         29
information, thereby decreasing the transaction costs  of issuing equity  compared to debt. This
results in equity being preferred over debt, which leads to a lowered leverage.  The difference
between the two proxies could be due to the proxy of ln(total assets)  also being a proxy for  the
potential to use collateral, which lowers  the cost of debt  compared to equity, supported by both
the trade-off theory and the pecking order theory.  While ln(sales) measures size regardless of the
potential to use collateral, resulting in the negative relationship predicted by the pecking order
theory.  While both proxies are statistically significant we will use the natural logarithm of total
assets  because of its large presence  in literature.
       For tangibility we implement a proxy used by Titman and Wessel (1988), property, plant
and equipment over  total assets, instead of total tangible fixed assets over total assets. By doing
this we gain the same positive correlation with a relatively high explanatory value  (Appendix D).
It also decreases during the crisis period  and continues to decrease in the  post-crisis period
(Appendix D), similar  to our initial proxy. This leads us to believe our results are robust and the
decreased explanatory value for tangibility  of assets  during the crisis may be due to drastic
shortages in lending during the  2008 Financial Crisis.
       We  try two different proxies for profitability beside our initial profitability proxy  PROF,
which  initially  was  EBITD over  total  assets. First we test  EBIT over sales turnover,  as done in
Hall  et al.  (2004), which causes the profitability determinant to loose its significance  (Appendix
E). Secondly we use  net income divided by total assets, following Trinh and Phuong (2016). This
gives similar results to our initial proxy, which concludes  a robust  estimation of profitability in
relation to leverage  (Appendix E).
       When testing the robustness of our results for GROWTH we use the proxy of change in
total assets, which results in loss of significance and extremely small magnitudes (Appendix  F).
Therefore,  we rely on our initial proxy  (percentage change in net sales between t and t-1). As
stated earlier, majority of studies use market value over book value as a proxy for growth
potential, but since we are not able to use market value when we only have non-listed companies,
we have used the best proxy  available to our data (change in sales) and it has significant results.
        To investigate the impact of subsidiaries and group relations on the results we check the
robustness by running regressions  once  on only independent companies and  once on all
companies, i.e. including subsidiaries. These robustness tests provide similar results as our main
regressions and the determinants show the similar  movements  in the periods (Appendix G). An

8.    Conclusion

This paper analyses  Swedish SMEs’  determinants of capital structure  before, during and after the
2008 Financial Crisis, investigating the determinants’  relationship to capital structure  during
periods of financial instability. We analyse capital structure with reference to  the  trade-off theory
and the pecking order theory. Six determinants were applied in the analysis: Size, Profitability,
Liquidity, Tangibility  of Assets, Non-Debt Tax Shields  and Growth Potential, which were chosen
due to their dominance in previous literature (e.g. Rajan and Zingales, 1995; Ozkan, 2001;
Harrison and Widjaja, 2014;  Proença  et al.,  2014) allowing for comparability.
        Our results are similar to previous studies (e.g.  Hall  et al., 2004;  Harrison and Widjaja,
2014;  Proença  et al., 2014). Overall we observe a downward trend in leverage during the crisis
period, as expected. We identify a positive  significant  relationship for Size, Tangibility  of Assets,
Liquidity,  Growth  Potential. A  significant  negative  relationship  is found  for Profitability.  NDTS

                                                                         31
indicates a  strong positive relationship, which is believed to be because of  the proxy failing to
represent NDTS, instead representing  tangibility, therefore its results are not  acknowledged.
Liquidity and  growth potential both contradict  majority of previous studies (e.g.  Ozkan, 2001;
Deesomsak  et al., 2004; Harrison and Widjaja, 2014), but  their relationship can be explained using
trade-off theory and are in accordance with  Proença  et al.  (2014), which  has focused  on SMEs.
This  suggests that  SMEs have different relationships  for  liquidity and growth potential compared
to larger companies. The other determinants are found to follow the trade-off and pecking order
theory  and agrees with majority of past literature. The  determinants with the highest explanatory
values for companies’ capital structure are Tangibility  of Assets  and Profitability, indicating that
they are of the highest importance in capital structure decisions  for Swedish SMEs.
       All determinants have similar relationships to leverage in all  periods, but  we observe
significant  changes  in the magnitude of their  coefficients.  Size remains constant over the crisis
period but increases in the post-crisis period.  Tangibility has an unpredicted decreased coefficient
during the crisis, which drops further during the post-crisis period.  Profitability increases, this
means it has less of a negative impact on  companies’  capital structure.  It can be  assumed  that
companies save more of their profits during volatile periods due to the increased risk of being
illiquid and decreased future profit, causing them to use external instead  of internal funds  in
order to ensure they have a buffer.  Liquidity has a minor  increase during the crisis period  and
then decreases during the post-crisis period.  This decrease may be  due  companies more aware of
costs  of  debt  resulting  in  increased  caution  when  taking  on  loans  due  to  the  potential
consequences.  Growth decreases for only the crisis-period, leading to growth potential  loosing
importance in capital structure decisions as they may be unreliable in volatile periods.
       This  paper adds significant results to past literature as it contributes valuable country
specific data, as well as empirical results for SMEs, enforcing  the trade-off theory and pecking
order theory’s  applicability  on Swedish SMEs  when predicting firms’ capital structure  in periods
of instability.  However, a limitation that  should  be recognised in our study is the length of the
crisis and our data set. Due to this, the crisis may not have been as evident as could be predicted.
Further research with longer data sets and additional crisis periods would be suggested to make
the results more robust. We  also recommend further research into NDTS and its relationship to
capital structure with more thorough proxies for the Swedish markets. This is in  order to  take
into account other  non-debt tax shields and remove the mechanical positive relationship  from
the proxy’s close relationship with tangibility.  It is  also  recommended to include a variable taking
into  account firm industry, since industries may  experience different forces regarding their capital
structure.

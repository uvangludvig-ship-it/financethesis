International Bank Flow Dynamics After the Global Financial Crisis

   Abstract:

       This paper examines the role of country-level bank regulatory reforms, and institu-
       tional quality in shaping cross-border capital flows following the Global Financial
       Crisis. Our study reveals that there has been a clear shift in the determinants of
      bank inflows from advanced to emerging market economies. Post-crisis findings
       suggest a diminished impact of institutional quality variables on the annual growth
       of foreign claims. Additionally, contrary to our pre-crisis results, more stringent
       bank regulatory regimes deterred foreign investment flows, underscoring the chang-
       ing dynamics of global capital movements. Hence, our research contributes to
       understanding the evolving relationship between banking regulations, institutional
       quality, and international capital flows after the Global Financial Crisis.

   Keywords:

       International Bank Flows, Foreign Claims, Institutional Quality, Bank Regulation,
       Global Financial Crisis.

1 Introduction

In the aftermath of the Global Financial Crisis (GFC) of 2007 to 2008, ex-post weaknesses in
the banking system prompted the introduction of seminal regulatory legislation. According
to a report by the International Monetary Fund (2015), post-GFC most countries have
authorities that adopt regulation that supports financial stability. They note that bank
supervision has intensified, particularly at large financial institutions. Additionally, bank
resolution regimes have been improved. An example is Basel III introduced by the Basel
Committee on Banking Supervision. Its purpose is to strengthen bank capital ratio
requirements, meaning banks must hold more funds relative to their outstanding loan
balance. This reform, along with stricter liquidity requirements, was intended to mitigate
systemic risk in the global banking system.
   Simultaneously, Barth, Caprio, and Levine (2013, ”BCL”) outlined extensive research
on how bank regulation had changed. They found evidence of a tightening trend in bank
regulation, in response to the catastrophic events of the GFC. Similarly, officials at the
ECB Forum on Banking Supervision (2021) acknowledged that, “the financial crisis showed
that financial regulation was too lax” and highlighted subsequent efforts to tighten them.
   As the regulatory regime was being redesigned, international capital flows were also
changing. The Bank for International Settlement (2021, “BIS”) reported that the post-GFC
decade saw significant shifts in the volume and composition of international bank flows.
Advanced economies experienced a dramatic decrease in capital inflows, especially through
bank channels. Conversely, inflows to emerging market economies have remained relatively
steady. Before the GFC, inflows to advanced economies peaked at 12 trillion in 2007,
representing 18% of global GDP. However, these inflows have since sharply declined and
remained volatile post-crisis. The drop-off was especially pronounced for cross-border
bank loans. The retreat of advanced economy banks from international lending has been
counterbalanced by the growing importance of asset managers, investment funds, and other
non-bank financial intermediaries.
    Recent literature suggests a shift in the composition of bank flows post-GFC. Cerutti,
Claessens, and Ratnovski (2014) point to broad factors like global stock market volatility,
term premia on U.S. long-term bond yields, and interest rate spreads as significant drivers
of cross-border bank flows.
    While the post-GFC period witnessed a regulatory overhaul, the impact on international
bank flows remains unclear. Prior research by Houston, Lin, and Ma (2012) established a
strong link between bank regulation and inflows pre-crisis. However, the changing landscape
of bank flows post-crisis necessitates a reevaluation of these determinants.

                                       3
    Given these sweeping changes, it is crucial to investigate whether bank regulation and
institutional quality remain prominent drivers of international flows in the post-GFC era.
Our research aims to answer the question,

To what extent do institutional quality and banking regulatory variables remain prominent
drivers of international bank flows following the Global Financial Crisis between the 2008
                                     to 2019?

   We intend to conduct two empirical tests to investigate whether banking regulatory
and institutional quality variables remain significant drivers of bank flows. The first test
seeks to replicate the findings of Houston et al. (2012), and the second test implements
the same empirical methodology but for a recent period. We use this approach to validate
our methodology.
    The tests look at bank inflows from 25 source countries to 120 recipient countries for
the period 1996 to 2007, and 26 source countries to 120 recipient countries for the period
2009 to 2019. We conduct seven incremental regressions for each period, gradually adding
variables to isolate their individual and combined effects on bank flows. Our independent
variables are divided into two classes; Institutional Quality and Regulatory variables. We
also include standard controls in the regressions for both periods. Additionally, we conduct
an instrumental variable analysis of the pre-crisis and post-crisis results.
    Similar to Houston et al. (2012), one concern in this study is the potential presence
of reverse causality. This is the possibility that bank inflows may prompt an endogenous
change in the country-level regulatory environment. To mitigate this effect, we follow
Bekaert, Harvey, and Lundblad (2005, 2007) and include the two control variables Financial
Liberalization and Growth Opportunities.
    We construct our regulatory variables by drawing extensively from the indices developed
by Barth et al. (2013), and the Bank Regulation and Supervision Surveys from the World
Bank. These surveys, spanning five iterations from 1999 to 2021, map out the banking
regulatory environment across 180 countries. Barth et al. (2013) have subsequently
published a revised dataset, which we have used for comparative and strengthening
purposes.
   We analyze the relationship between our dependent variable Bank Flows, and our
independent variables Institutional Quality, and Bank Regulation through OLS regressions.
We use linear panel models and include time-fixed and country-fixed effects.
    For the 1996-2007 period, we find evidence that high degrees of institutional quality,
particularly Creditor Rights and Property Rights, are significant drivers of bank inflows to
recipient countries. Our findings also show a positive association between bank inflows and
regulatory variables like Banks Owning Non-Financial Firms, and Capital Stringency. This
contrasts with the findings of Houston et al. (2012). However, the authors note that there
is the possibility that having a stricter regulatory environment could be an indication of
quality and stability, even when regulatory arbitrage is taking place, and capital is flowing
from source countries to less-regulated recipient countries.

                                       4
    In the post-crisis period of 2008-2019, we find that institutional quality variables no
longer appear to be key drivers of bank flows. For instance, Depth of Credit Information
exhibits a negative association with the growth in bank inflows. We also see that changes
in regulatory variables do not appear to be significant predictors of capital inflows, save
for Financial Statement Transparency where a higher value proved to deter bank inflows
throughout all regressions. Further, the variable Strength of External Audit also exhibits a
significant and negative association with bank inflow growth.
    We find that the results for both the pre-crisis and post-crisis periods show varying
levels of robustness. Pre-crisis, the institutional quality variables to some degree remain
virtually significant when including instrumental variables, except for Creditor Rights.
The pre-crisis impact of bank regulatory variables weakens when including instrumental
variables, while the post-crisis relationship appears to strengthen.
    Overall, our findings paint a nuanced picture regarding the influence of institutional
quality and regulatory variables on post-GFC bank inflows. While pre-crisis research by
Houston et al. (2012) established strong associations, our analysis reveals a diminished
overall impact. Thus, our key contribution is to provide an update on how country-level
institutional quality and bank regulation variables drive bank inflows post-crisis. We
have extended the original research and evaluated the ongoing relevance of the proposed
determinants. The original paper used research by Barth et al. (2013), who established
index measures of country-level bank regulatory aspects. Similarly, we build and extend
the measure for the post-crisis period of 2008-2019 and make inferences on the strength of
the regulatory variables to drive bank inflows to recipient countries post-GFC.
   This paper also contributes to research by Alfaro, Kalemli-Ozcan, and Volosovych
(2008), who demonstrated that cross-country differences in economic fundamentals must
be complemented by strong institutional quality for capital to flow to emerging market
economies. While Lucas (1990) highlights the potential for higher marginal returns in
emerging markets, capital flows have historically favored advanced economies. Papaioannou
(2009) suggests that institutional underdevelopment may be a key explanation for this
paradox. We have extended this research by including institutional quality variables and
examining whether they remain significant in explaining bank inflows to emerging markets
following the GFC.

2 Data Description
2.1 BIS and Foreign Claims
Data for our dependent variable are collected from the BIS International Banking Statistics
(2024), which provides quarterly figures on international foreign claims. These foreign claims
are a consolidated figure of capital flows, containing everything from portfolio investments
and bank loans to properties, from several source countries to recipient countries. The source
countries are mainly advanced economies, while the recipient countries are characterized as
emerging market economies. For a subset of source and recipient countries, we specifically
extract data on the consolidated foreign financial claims reported by domestic banks,
excluding domestic positions, for 1996-2019. This entails that the figures are reported
based on where the parent company of the banking group has its headquarters. For example,
if a subsidiary of a Swedish bank with offices in Denmark makes an equity investment in a
Danish bank, it will be denoted as a foreign claim. If the same Swedish bank subsidiary
makes a loan to a Swedish bank located in Sweden, it would constitute a domestic claim
and is subsequently not included in our data. It is also worth noting that source countries
are included among the recipient countries, meaning the international positions are in gross
terms.

Bank Flow Variable
We use the method outlined by Houston et al. (2012) to construct our dependent variable
Bank Flow. We utilize the outstanding claims balance on recipient countries as of December
of each year to arrive at an annual measure. This measure captures capital inflows from a
source country s to a recipient country r. The foreign claims from all source countries are
then summed up to create an annual change in the bank inflows variable. For example, a
hypothetical value of 3.5 indicates that the annual growth in bank inflows for that year
is 3.5%. In technical terms, Bank Flow is defined as 100 times the log-difference of total
foreign claims from t-1 to t, as follows:

2.2 Banking Regulation and Supervision
In 1998 the World Bank tasked Barth et al. (2013) with assembling the first comprehensive
cross-country database for bank regulation and supervision. They surveyed bank regulators,
supervisors, and economists, and the result was published in the World Bank (2023) data
catalog. The surveys analyze different aspects of banking regulation, such as entry barriers,
capital requirements, financial disclosure requirements, and the role of supervisory authori-
ties. The first survey covers over 100 countries and outlines the regulatory environment in
1999. The World Bank released the second survey in 2002. The third survey was published
in 2007 and provides information on banking regulation and policies in 2006. For our

                                       6
extension period of 2008 to 2019 we use the fourth survey which covers the banking policy
and regulatory situation in 2011. The latest survey was released in 2021.

Bank Regulatory Variables
The selected regulatory variables are defined and constructed by Barth et al. (2013). The
variables are based on indices and measure different aspects of banking regulation. For
instance, the variable Overall Activity Restrictions looks at the degree to which banks may
engage in auxiliary activities such as securities, insurance, and real estate. Banks Owning
Non-Financial Firms and Capital Stringency measure the degree to which banks may own
non-bank firms and the capital strictness on banks, respectively. The variables Official
Supervisory Power and Independence of Supervisory Authority measure different aspects of
the bank supervisor’s influence and independence, respectively. Additionally, the Strength
of External Audit measures the effectiveness of external audits, and Financial Statement
Transparency measures the degree of disclosure of certain elements in financial reporting.
    The surveys are retrospective, meaning the values are extrapolated. The surveys cover
five distinct periods: 1996 to 1999 (Survey I), 2000 to 2003 (Survey II), 2004 to 2007 (Survey
III), 2008 to 2011 (Survey IV), and 2012 to 2019 (Survey V). We deal with inconsistencies
and missing data points by cross-checking surveys. For example, a country may provide the
same information on a technical regulatory rule in Survey I, II, and IV, but an insufficient
answer for Survey III. In this case, data is extrapolated to provide a complete picture. For
indices with weighted sub-indicators, we may use a minimum level of required answers to
either include or omit the country in our tests.

2.3 Institutional Quality Variables
Our institutional quality variables are collected from different sources, which in some cases
complement each other. The World Bank “Doing Business” database contains information
on business regulations and their implementation in over 190 countries. The variable
Creditor Rights is sourced from La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes, Shleifer, and W. Vishny (1998)
leading up to 1998, and extended from Djankov, McLiesh, and Schleifer (“DMS”, 2007) up
until 2002. The variable captures the degree of influence given to senior creditors during
firm liquidation. Creditor Rights is used for the 1996-2007 period, while a World Bank
proxy variable: Resolving Insolvency is used for the 2008-2019 period. Lastly, Property
Rights is extracted from the annually released World Economic Freedom report published
by the Fraser Institute (2023).

2.4 Control Variables
We include control variables in our regressions to support the validity of our results. Log
Income, a measure of real GDP per capita (in inflation-adjusted constant USD terms)
is included in all incremental regressions. Log Population, which is the logarithmized
annual population size (in millions) is also included. The control variable Number of
Days to Enforce Contracts measures the number of days it takes for creditors to get

3 Empirical Analysis

We examine the relationship between bank regulation and institutional quality in recipient
countries, and the growth in foreign claims through the regression:

          BankInF lowr,t ∼ α0 + α1Regulatoryr,t + β1InstitutionalQualityr,t
                                                                                         (3)
                         + γ1Xr,t + φr + μt + εr,t

   The regression looks at the change in international bank flows, defined as the log
difference of total foreign claims from 25 source countries s to 120 recipient countries
r for the period 1996-2007 and 26 source countries to 120 recipient countries for the
2008-2019 period. The bank regulatory variables measure, for example, the strength of
external audits, capital stringency, and the degree of financial statement disclosure. The
institutional quality variables are Creditor Rights, Property Rights, and Depth of Credit
Information. The X variable represents, among others, the controls Log Income, Financial
Liberalization, and Growth Opportunities. To control for unobserved time-invariant effects
we include country-fixed effects and time-fixed effects denoted by φr and μt, respectively.
The p-values are calculated using heteroskedastic robust standard errors clustered at the
recipient country level.
    Our empirical analysis is divided into two sections. In the first section, we study the
regression results of the pre-crisis period of 1996-2007, which is a replication of Houston et
al. (2012). In the second section, we study the regression results of 2008-2019.

3.1 Pre-GFC Analysis
In this section, we assess the banking regulatory and institutional quality effect on interna-
tional bank inflows from 25 source countries to 120 recipient countries for the pre-GFC
period spanning 1996 to 2007.
   Our regressions are presented in Table 4, stretching from columns (1) to (7). The
results suggest that the institutional quality variables significantly predict bank inflows to
recipient countries for the pre-crisis period. The Creditor Rights variable exhibits a strong
association with the growth of bank inflows. As presented in regressions (2) through (7),
we find that a one-unit increase in the Creditor Rights variable corresponds to an increase
of between 6.88% to 8.33% in annual bank inflow growth. Notably, the average annual
bank inflow growth within our sample of 120 countries is 6.62%, indicating that the impact
of Creditor Rights is considerable. Further, having a higher Depth of Credit Information
score, meaning more transparent and informative credit institutions, increases the growth
of capital inflows by between 0.83% and 0.90% as seen in regressions (1) and (3). We also
find that Property Rights is a strong driver of bank inflows, where a one-unit increase in
the variable equates to an increase of between 1.48% and 2.00% in annual bank inflow
growth. The variable remains highly significant throughout all regressions.
    Overall, the results exhibit that having a higher degree of institutional quality attracts
more capital to recipient countries during the period 1996 to 2007. The findings on the

                                      13
institutional quality variables are in line with the findings of Houston et al. (2012).
    When testing for bank regulatory variables we are mainly concerned with regressions
(2) to (7). We find that Banks Owning Non-financial Firms exhibits positive but weak
predictability on annual bank inflow growth. Further, Capital Stringency exhibits a positive
and significant relationship with bank inflow growth in regression. A one-unit increase in
Capital Stringency, indicating tighter requirements on bank capital, and Banks Owning
Non-financial Firms, indicating higher restrictiveness, will on average lead to an increase
in annual bank inflow growth of 0.65% and 1.95%, respectively. Further, when including
all bank regulatory variables in regression (6), Capital Stringency strengthens its statistical
significance. Conversely, the significance of Banks Owning Non-financial Firms weakens.
These results differ from the original study, where both variables were negatively associated
with bank inflows. Houston et al. (2012, p. 1848) note that “bank capital may not
necessarily flock to low regulated environments” and, “that in some circumstances strong
regulations are prudent and may serve as a signal of quality and stability.” We believe that
the discrepancies in our results likely stem from differing approaches to data extrapolation,
and handling missing data points.
    Lastly, we find that Log Income is significant, and positively related to bank inflows
throughout all regressions, suggesting that the higher the GDP per capita, the higher the
growth in annual bank inflow.

3.2 Post-GFC Analysis
In this section, we assess the banking regulatory and institutional quality effect on interna-
tional bank inflows from 26 source countries to 120 recipient countries for the post-GFC
period spanning 2008 to 2019.
    Our regressions are presented in Table 5, stretching from column (1) to (7). Initially, a
clear shift in the results can be observed. Regarding the institutional quality variables, we
find that the Creditor Rights proxy variable Resolving Insolvency does not significantly
predict bank inflows post-crisis. Further, although Property Rights shows a positive and
significant association in regression (7), the results are not consistent throughout regressions
(1) to (6) to grant a conclusive remark on the variable’s predictability. Depth of Credit
Information exhibits highly significant results throughout all regressions but contrastingly,
now appears to discourage bank flows. A one-unit increase in the variable leads to an
average decrease of between 0.62% and 2.05% in annual bank inflow growth. Given that
prior research by Alfaro et al. (2008) found that institutional quality is a necessary
complement for capital to flow to emerging market economies, our findings on Depth of
Credit Information suggest otherwise in the post-GFC era.
    Looking at the regulatory variables, we find that Banks Owning Non-Financial Firms,
and Capital Stringency, are no longer significant drivers of bank flows. Instead, the
Strength of External Audit and Financial Statement Transparency variables are now
significant predictors and appear to discourage bank flow growth post-crisis. These two
variables remain significant when including all variables in regression (6), but only Financial
Statement Transparency retains its significance when including controls in regression (7).

post-crisis is 0.86%. This is a remarkable drop from the pre-crisis average of 6.62%.

3.3 Addressing Reverse Causality
A relevant concern in cross-country research is endogeneity, specifically reverse causal-
ity. The premise is that regulatory and institutional quality variables drive bank inflows.
However, a change in bank inflows itself might prompt changes to the regulatory and
institutional quality landscape. Prior research has proposed steps to deal with the endo-
geneity issue. For instance, Bekaert et al. (2005) find that when a country liberalizes its
equity markets to foreign investors, it creates economic growth domestically. However, the
uncertainty lies in whether the economic growth stems from the liberalization, or if the
domestic industries had growth potential either induced or not induced by the liberalization.
Thus, we include the control variable Financial Liberalization as presented by Abiad et
al. (2010), and extended by Omori (2022). Further, Bekaert et al. (2007) point out that
country PE values are a good proxy for evaluating future growth opportunities. However,
as these measures are volatile to discount rates, they propose using a five-year moving
average. The variable Growth Opportunities applies only to the post-GFC regressions, due
to a lack of data. Overall, we include Financial Liberalization and Growth Opportunities to
control for other macroeconomic drivers of bank flows. It is worth noting that our sample
size drops quite significantly after including these two controls.

3.4 Instrumental Variable Analysis
In addition to controlling for reverse causality, we also conduct an instrumental variable
analysis to further strengthen our results. Firstly, Beck, Demirg¨u¸c-Kunt, and Levine
(2006) posit that countries that have been independent for a longer time have had more
opportunities to implement economic and governmental policies that spur the expansion
of their economies. We include the relative measure, Country Independence, which looks
at how long a country has been independent since 1776. Following Houston et al. (2012),
we also include Supervisor Experience as an instrumental variable. This is because bank
supervisors with longer experience tend to develop more advanced banking regulations
that tackle significant challenges in their respective economies. Finally, we incorporate
the country-level Gini Coefficient as a five-year moving average, to measure the degree
of income inequality. As Beck, Levine, and Levkov (2010, p.1665) point out, income
distribution concerns have seen a growing importance when regulators set banking policies.
    We re-estimate our regressions and present them in Tables 6 and 7 for the pre-and
post-GFC periods, respectively. For the pre-GFC period, we find that Creditor Rights drops
in significance, but remains close to the 10% threshold. On the other hand, Property Rights
exhibits more promising results. The regulatory variables weaken in their explanatory
power.
    Post-GFC, Depth of Credit Information retains its significance across all regressions
with a slight increase in magnitude. Strength of External Audit and Financial Statement
Transparency retain their significance when regressed alone and together with all other
variables, exhibiting a high degree of robustness.

4 Conclusion

The Global Financial Crisis triggered a fundamental change in bank regulation, leading
to a sustained decline in cross-border bank flows. This paper focuses on the relationship
between country-level bank regulatory environments, institutional quality, and international
bank flows.
   We conduct a comparative analysis between the periods 1996 to 2007, and 2008 to
2019, and find support that there has been a clear shift in what drives foreign claims.
Pre-crisis, our results suggest that the protection of creditor rights and property rights
are prominent drivers. Similarly, stricter bank capital requirements also attract bank
flows. Post-crisis, we find strong evidence that these variables no longer appear to be key
drivers. Particularly, rich credit information, stronger external audits, and higher financial
statement transparency decrease the growth in annual bank flows. Our results for the
post-crisis period prove to be robust, even after controlling for potential endogeneity and
conducting an instrumental variable analysis.

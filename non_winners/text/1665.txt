                                      Abstract
This thesis studies the trading of High Frequency Trading firm Spire Europe on the
NASDAQ OMX  Nordic  exchange with the goal of trying to characterise the way they
make profit  and how they manage risk. We conclude that the return of high frequency
trading has very different characteristics from ordinary trading and hence methods
developed for researching ordinary trading has little value when trying to investigate high
frequency trading.

Keywords: High Frequency Trading, Spire Europe, Price anticipation

Introduction

We study the high frequency trading firm Spire Europe’s trading on the NASDAQ
OMX Nordic exchanges by looking at intraday trading data. We examine both their
profitability in individual stocks and what risk  they  take on by looking at  different
periods of  intraday Value  at Risk  measures. Furthermore we try to empirically explain the
intraday  returns by  use of  standard OLS regressions.
   Most  research produced on  the topic  of  market microstructure  is very theoretical in
nature and few studies use intraday data to study a specific firm. Our study is  empirically
oriented,  and  based  on  one firm’s trading,  we try to  understand what kind of return  the
firm  generates and what risk  that return comes  with.

Background

High frequency trading

High-frequency trading  (abbreviated HFT)  firms use advanced computer algorithms to
trade large  quantities of securities in  very brief  periods  of time.  This allows them to
capture small profit opportunities that may only last for fractions of a second, and
because of this they do not hold any overnight positions.  It was the move to smaller tick
sizes in the US capital markets in 2000, when pennies started being used in price quotes,
that really made market volume explode  according to Clark (2010). This led to computers
being more appropriate to handle the huge increases in data points rather than humans.
   High frequency trading is part of a  broader  category of trading called algorithmic
trading. Algorithmic trading is based on trading done in part or in whole by computer
algorithms. There are two general types of algorithms that are used,  execution algorithms
and speculative algorithms. Execution algorithms are designed to attain the best price for
a transaction specified by the trader. They are particularly useful for  partitioning block
trades into smaller parts. Block trades are  difficult to  trade because you need to  find a
counterparty that is willing to trade the entire block of shares  with you. The execution
algorithm tries to avoid this problem by dividing the trade into smaller ones to get a
better price and to avoid making a large impact on the market.  Speculative algorithms are
algorithms that make  automatic  decisions about what trades to make. In other words
they can make decisions on what to trade, when to trade it and how much of it to trade
without interference from humans.  High frequency traders employ  both execution and
speculative algorithms depending on what strategy they use.  Before implementing the
algorithms they go through extensive backtesting to  assess  their  functioning  in the
market.  To get an edge over competitors  high frequency trading firms  make  big
investments in hardware and software to make systems  faster,  and since there is a steep
learning curve to become a good high frequency trader human  capital is very important.
It is also a  de facto  standard to be directly connected to the exchanges’  servers,
something called colocation.
   Speed in particular is at a premium and new platforms increase speed by multiple
orders  of  magnitude.  Speed  can  be  broken  down  into  throughput  and  latency.
Throughput is the total number of transactions a system can execute each second.
Latency is the time it takes between the placement of an order and when it reaches the
market. Reliability is the second important factor to consider when designing quality
trading platforms. With orders being placed at such high frequency, down time that
results in leaving traders unable to place or cancel orders could prove very costly. The
solution is to make systems highly available and redundant so you can switch to a backup
system should you need it. Finally, Amicangioli (2006) writes that  service providers must
offer capabilities that allow traders to switch to other facilities in the event of some
natural disaster or terror attack
   Although there are relatively few HFT  firms  in the US markets, about 2 % of 20  000
firms,  they account for almost three-fourths of all equity trading volume on that market.
Firms  engaging in HFT can be everything from major investment banks and hedge funds
to small prop shops, Iati (2009) writes.  The TABB Group, a financial markets research
firm, estimates that aggregated annual profits of these firms exceeded $21 billion  in  2008.
Clark (2010) writes that  algorithmic trading on the US market grew from 30 %  in 2005 to
70 % in 2009  and according to research from consultancy Aite Group, high frequency
trading accounts for almost 40% of European trading  volume, Cave (2011a) reports.
   An important aspect of the competition among different HFT firms is that the
micro-level algorithms have a limited lifespan and that competitive advantages might
quickly be diluted. One reason for this is that many statistical relationships between
securities can change very fast, which requires a corresponding change in the code for
the algorithms. Secondly, there’s a risk that competitors will reverse-engineer your
algorithms, enabling them to front-run or trade against you, thus making them  obsolete
as Iati (2009) writes.
   To maximize the probability that they close a position profitably high frequency
traders cancel and send orders continuously to both sides of the order book. They also
vary between submitting aggressive and passive orders (market and limit orders) in order
to achieve the best position in the book. It is common to see cancellations of a lot of
orders on both sides of the book after a market order hits a bid or lifts an offer as a
result of high frequency traders withdrawing because they were not able to capture a
trade. Even though HFT firms can position themselves in the front of the order book
there is no guarantee that they will be able to close the position profitably, and HFTs
cannot know whether a trade is a liquidity trade or for example a trade from another
HFT.
    Many traders send orders through clearing members’ trading systems. To control risk
clearing members have limits on which trades may be executed based on price and
quantity. In addition, there are also exchanges that have a built-in function that cancels
clearly erroneous trades. To reduce latency HFT firms  choose to use their own platforms
to send their orders to the exchange. We distinguish between two different types of
arrangements: sponsored access and unfiltered sponsored access. The former allows
traders to access the exchange matching engine directly, and that arrangement also
includes the same kinds of pre-trade risk controls as the clearing members’ own trading
systems. The unfiltered sponsored access reduces latency by bypassing the risk controls
and sends orders directly to the matching engine. This type of access is not without risks,
as history clearly shows, as it is more susceptible to for example “fat finger trades”. In
2007 a Morgan Stanley trader entered an order to buy 100  000 shares while using a tool
that had a built-in multiplier of  1000. The order was for $10.8 billion instead of $10.8
million. Before the bank noticed the mistake and canceled the order close to $900 million
worth of shares had been traded, according to Clark (2010).  It is largely unknown how
the number of HFT firms has changed during the last couple of years. Depending on
where one looks the number of HFT firms has increased in some markets and decreased
in others, but the profits of early entrants seem to be stable.

NASDAQ OMX Nordic

On the  NASDAQ  OMX Nordic market  HFT firms doubled their trading in 2011 from
the year before.  The total value of equities traded by HFT firms rose to EUR170 billion
and they accounted for 12 % of all trading in 2011.  The value of equities traded for the
market as  a whole increased 5 % to  EUR1.4 trillion, as Cave (2012) reports.
   The priority among orders  on these markets  is  firstly “price”, secondly  “internal”,
then  “display”,  and lastly  “time”.  Unsurprisingly,  price has first priority and  the better
price has priority  over poorer prices.  Internal priority means that orders are first executed
against Exchange Members’ orders.  Orders  are  then  matched with regard to displayed
over non-displayed volume. Lastly,  orders  are  matched according to when it was sent to
the order book, and there is time priority for pre-open orders, as can be seen in the
NASDAQ OMX Nordic Market Model 2.6 (2011).

the European  financial  markets. It's owned by Tower Research Capital LLC, a financial

strategies.  Spire Europe was established in 2006, but began active trading in 2009. It
describes itself as a “proprietary trading firm dealing in equities, futures and foreign

according to accounts, and its pre-tax profits increased from £3.1m to £22m as is
reported in Cave (2011b).
   On the Stockholm Stock  Exchange Spire executes about 20 000 trades each day to a

on the NASDAQ OMX Nordic markets.  NASDAQ OMX is one of the only exchanges
in the world to reveal the level of trading activity of its member firms, Cave (2012).
NASDAQ OMX's data also reveal that Spire was second only to Getco among high-
frequency players in September 2011 by market share, and the eleventh-largest provider

It is one of 20 members of the European Principal Traders Association, a lobby group
set up in 2011 to represent the interests of firms that trade using their own capital
according to Cave (2011).  Mehta (2011) reports that the last couple of years have seen
trading volume  significantly lower than  was common in for example 2007,  and there is
some evidence to suggest that this has been bad  for HFT firms, including Spire.
   Tower  Research Capital  was founded in 1998 by Mark Gorton and is one of the first

speed up the lead time for trades.  Tower and Lime Brokerage were part of the Lime
Group  which also established the file sharing service LimeWire.  Lime  Brokerage was
bought by large  US  broker Wedbush in 2011.  Tower had $117 million in assets under

has  also  never  had a down year  from the start until at least 2008  as can be found in
Plambeck (2010) and on Tower’s official website (2012b).

How order books work

Trades on stock exchanges are coordinated by a system of double auction. Both buy
(bid) and sell (ask) orders are quoted in an order book where one can see how many
shares (volume) market participants are willing to buy or sell at a particular price. The
types of orders that appear in the order book are called limit orders. They are orders that
specify  both the volume  and to what  price  a market participant is  willing to  transact.
Another common order type  is a market order,  that is an order  where you only specify
the volume  of  the  stock  you wish to trade.  The order will then be executed immediately
(assuming there is someone willing to trade)  at  the best available price in the market  at
the time.
   The buy and sell orders  are arranged in two different columns and the best prices
(the highest buy price and lowest sell price) and their corresponding volume are quoted
at the top of the order book. The difference between these two best prices in the order
book is called the bid-ask spread. If there are matching orders, that is, if the ask price of
an order equals the bid price of an order,  the orders are  executed and  are subsequently
removed  from the order book.
High Frequency Trading Strategies

High frequency trading  firms employ  several different strategies with the common
characteristic  that they are  all  suited for very short  holding periods.  In Aldrige (2010)  the
different trading strategies are  classified and described in the following way.

Automated liquidity provision  –  algorithms automatically offers  bid and ask prices and
execute orders to make  a  market  in  financial  instruments

Market microstructure trading  –  Algorithms  detect changes in order flow and anticipate price
changes

Event trading  –  Short-term trading on  macroeconomic news events

Statistical arbitrage  –  Finding  combinations  of assets that have pricing inefficiencies, then
trading the assets in  anticipation  that the  market will eventually correct the  inefficiencies

The most straightforward of the market  microstructure trading strategies is a strategy
called  price anticipation  described by Cartea et. al. (2012).  The objective of a  high frequency
trader that uses price anticipation is to take advantage of imbalances between  the  buy
side  and  the sell side of  a  limit order book. Such an imbalance might be caused by, for
instance, a  large  institution wanting to buy or sell a large amount of shares in the market.
Price anticipation is further explained  below with the help of a hypothetical example.
   Assume that  there is an increase in buy orders in a particular stock. This might be due
to an institutional investor wanting to buy a large amount of shares. The HFT  algorithms
will detect the rising buying pressure and cancel all of its sell orders. They then add
additional  buy orders to clear  out  the top of the order book at the sell side and at the
same time  start  adding  new sell orders at a higher price than the  previous prevailing
price. Buyers realizing that they cannot get a fill at their  current bid price will  be forced
to raise their price in order to buy  shares. The HFT firm is then able to sell shares at the
new higher price. When the buying pressure wears off the HFT  algorithms  will notice
and will be able to cover  the rest of  its positions at a lower price.  The HFT  firm has now
earned  the spread between the higher price it sold shares  for and the  lower price it was
buying them back for.  In order to make it harder for  high frequency traders to detect
large orders  institutional investors  can  use  execution algorithms such as  VWAP,  liquidity
pools or  iceberg orders.

Theory and prior research

Market microstructure and high frequency trading

There  has been a lot of  research  made  on market microstructure and also specifically on
the effects of high frequency trading on markets,  but not much empirical research  about
the  risk and return from high frequency trading  has been produced.  The following is a
short  exposition of interesting and relevant findings within  market microstructure
research  and related fields.
   Vega  (2007) proposes observing the share of market orders in order to gain market
information. The motivation is that aggressive orders reflect beliefs that there is a higher
likelihood that prices will move away from the market price. Traders  will  therefore  want
to  submit market orders in an effort to capture the best available price  in anticipation of
an imminent price move, as described by Aldridge (2010).
   Berber and Caglio (2004)  find that  limit orders contain private information around
certain events such as earnings announcement. Furthermore Harris and Panchapagesan
(2005) show that traders that can fully view the order book can earn abnormal returns.
This suggest that traders that can process large amounts of data and observe the
evolution of the order book such as HFT firm have a big advantage in some cases
compared to slow traders by anticipating price moves and constantly change their
positions in response of market movements.
In  Wang, Hansch and Cao (2004) the shape of the limit order book  is  exploited  by
market makers and the breadth and the depth of the order book predict 30  % of the
impending price moves.  Holden and Subrahmanyam (1992) find that the more volatile
the price of a security is, the lower the depth of the information in the order book.  They
find that small changes in volatility will lead to large changes in transaction prices, and
that  this  in turn  will  lead to a lower probability that informed traders will provide
liquidity.  This insight is evidence that certain informed liquidity providers like HFT firms
withdraw from the market when it is not possible to get an informational advantage as a
result of high volatility.  Additionally,  according to the  SEC (2010) report,  6 out of 12
HFT firms withdrew from the market during the  Flash Crash of May 6 2010. They also
report a reduction in average execution time from 10.1 seconds in January 2005 to 0.7
seconds in October 2009. This has made possible faster price adjustments across
different   markets   and   made   markets   more   efficient   in   the   sense   that   arbitrage
opportunities exist for a shorter time.  Kirilenko et al. (2010) concludes that HFTs had
the highest price impact among all types of traders  during this time. They  also find that
HFTs  increased volume of trading without increasing the flow of information, and
increased noise around the fundamental value of the asset.  On the other hand, papers
like  Hendershott and Riordan (2012) high frequency trading have a positive effect on
price discovery and may dampen volatility and price impact.
   Cartea and Penalva (2012) find  that the price impact of liquidity trades is higher in
the presence of HFTs and that the impact increases with the size of the trade. As a
reaction to the presence of HFTs market participants adjust their security supply and
demand functions, which lead to an increase in price impact by creating a temporary
order imbalance thus creating an additional market impact cost on participants.  HFTs
also increase the microstructure noise of prices through intermediation of trades by
buying cheap and  selling a little higher and also through larger price impact. This
increases the perceived price risk for other traders, and makes the future uncertainty of
their execution costs result in an increase in the liquidity discount. HFTs also decrease
profits  for liquidity providers as well as for other traders by intermediating trades, but
they contribute to making higher liquidity discounts to the market price in many cases.
They also find that competition between HFTs create uncertainty for other liquidity
providers that distorts total market liquidity in equilibrium. Biais et al. (2011) conclude
that HFT’s  effect on liquidity is ambiguous, depends on several factors and requires
more quality empirical research, in which the authors of this paper concurs.
   Engle and Russell (1998)  report that two-thirds of transactions in intraday data are
without price change. Gorski, Drozdz and Speht (2002) report  basically  the same
phenomenon  but for another data sample. (Tsay, 2002) also notes that microstructure
effect may  induce serial correlation in high frequency returns. This is something that we
find in our data too.

Value at risk
Due to the nature of HFT,  the risk of generating errors faster and greater than other
forms of trading is always present.  For this  reason  risk management at these firms is
important.  Because HFT firms do not hold  a lot of inventory at any one time  and
because they trade on such short time scales  the  use of proper risk measures  to get valid
and useful results  is essential.  To control risk HFT  firms  may  sometimes  just withdraw
from the market  as discussed before  if their algorithms are not working,  and  humans are
used for  that purpose by  monitoring  positions.  Below,  we estimate  Intra-day Value at
Risk  measures using the Historical simulation method to model the  market risk that Spire
takes on.

confidence level  !  ∈  (0,1), VaRα(L) is the smallest number  l  such that the probability
that the loss  L  exceeds  l  is no larger than  1 − !, i.e.

in other words VaR can be thought of as the potential loss one might suffer from
holding a portfolio over some, fixed, time  interval.  It is often  used by institutions to
measure risk over next ten day.  The validity of a VaR is measured by determine the
frequency of periods where the realized loss exceeds the estimated VaR.
The three most common methods to calculate VaR are Historical Simulation, Monte
Carlo  Simulation  and  the  Variance-Covariance  method.  The  Variance-Covariance
method assumes  normally  distributed returns  but because intraday returns exhibit fat tails
as Giot (2011) writes,  it is not suitable for  our purpose.  The  Monte Carlo Simulation also
assumes a particular distribution, although it doesn’t need to be a normal distribution,
Vosper et al. (1999).  Since the data is so chaotic we believe that the non-parametric
Historical simulation  method serves us best.
   Historical simulation makes no assumption  about the probability  distribution of
losses except that they are iid during the different time intervals. The approach of the
method is that to estimate VaR for  the  time period VaRt+1  one uses the current portfolio
and simulates  a loss distribution by determining how  historical time periods would have
generated for losses in  the current portfolio. The disadvantage is that  the  worst simulated
outcome is never worse than  what has happened before.
    Historical simulation is appropriate only if returns are iid. However, that is violated
when volatility changes over time. This leads to inconsistent estimates that are shown to
underestimate the VaR. Filtered historical simulation uses a parametric GARCH-model
to filter past returns  to acquire a new iid innovation that is used together with the non-
parametric historical portfolio returns to simulate a VaR; Vosper et al. (1999).  FHS also
help remove serial correlation in the data.
   Intraday value at risk  (IVaR)  is a measure  of VaR using  shorter periods than one day.
Since high frequency trading firms  change their  inventory  very  rapidly, the  ordinary daily
VaR is an  inadequate risk measure.  It is well-known that high-frequency data exhibit
strong intraday seasonality due to the fact that  markets tend to  be more active at the
beginning and towards the end of the trading day  (Engle and Russell, 1998).  The  models
providing the best VaR estimates are those using intraday data and  that control  for
periodicity simply or not at all.  It is  also  more important  to have intraday data (as
compared to only daily observations) than what model is used  to get good results,
according to McMillan et al. (2007).  In addition  Historical simulation has proven to
outperformed the parametric models when calculating  VaR, Qi (2011).

Almeiday and Vicente (2008) explain that to  test whether VaR estimations are good they
have  to  be  tested  for  Unconditional  Coverage   Property  and  Independence   Property.  The
Unconditional coverage property is whether the average number of  violations are equal
to the statistically expected one and a violation is when realized loss exceeds the VaR
estimate. The Independence property means that the violations are independently
distributed i.e. that they are not clustered in time. If this condition is not met it means
that the violation carry information on future volatility.
To test for the unconditional coverage property the  Kupiec test  is used. We take the time
series of VaR-values and compare it to the corresponding time series of realized  returns.
Then we creates a hit sequence which take the value 1 for every time the realized loss is
greater than the VaR and zero otherwise.

Data description  and methodology

Data description

Our data consists of  the  NASDAQ OMX  Nordic  data  feed, stored in so called ITCH
files.  They  are files containing all the actions  undertaken on the NASDAQ OMX
NORDIC exchanges. The ITCH files are vast in size  –  between 30 to 40 million rows
per  day  –  which  makes  the  data  processing  quite  complex  and  computationally
expensive. We have used ITCH  files for ten days during the spring of 2012.  Also, the
data is heterogeneous in time, meaning that the data points are not evenly spaced in time.
    In our data Spire is trading in 44 different  very liquid  stocks. The  data is taken from
the stocks that we determined that Spire is trading in. Since the data set is so vast  we
have  clustered  the data. We are mostly  using 1, 10 or 30 minute  intervals although  some
regressions is done on microstructure data.

The  data feeds we have used contain between  30-50 million rows. The feed is saved as  a
regular ASCII  text in files that is about one gigabyte in size. The  files comprise
everything that happens on the exchange in chronological order. Some examples on how
to read the data feed is presented below.
   The row: “A 7072827B    300 1158   7725000” is interpreted as follows:
‘A’  means add a new order,  7072827 is a  reference number used when the order is
cancelled or executed.  ‘B’  indicated that it is a buy order and  300 is the quantity of shares
in the order. 1158 is the stock identifier, in this case Novo Nordisk B  stock  (which can
be determined from the messages  starting with an “R”) and 7725000 is the price 772.5
DKK.
   The row “E 1059830      85   202704NON ENS” means that the  order,  or part of
the order,  with reference number 1059830 has been executed. 85 is the number of shares
executed  (in this case out of  700, which can be seen in the corresponding added order
message in the table above). 202704 is a match number used if something goes wrong
and a Broken Trade message is sent. NON is the ID  of the owner to the order, in this
case Nordnet. ENS is the counterpart in the trade, in this case SEB. To determine who
the buyer and seller  was  one has to look at the order to see  if  it is a sell order  or a buy
order. In this case there is an ‘S’ at the end of the order which implies  that the owner
(Nordnet) is the seller and hence  SEB  is  the buyer. To see  the price and in what order
book the trade was,  one also has to look at the Add Order message.
This is just a small subset of the message types  present in a data feed. There is  a  lot of
different messages for different  types  of orders, different types  of order modifications,
trades  and system codes that indicate the state of the market.
In order to build a complete order book from a data feed one has to keep track of all the
messages that  impact  that order book.  That is,  when orders are added, modified, deleted
or executed and when trades in the order book are broken. All active orders in the book
have to be maintained properly  after every new message i.e. ranked on price and time of
arrival at both  the buy and sell side.

Analysis

Consider the  diagrams describing  ten  minute, one minute and  ten  second returns.  As the
time interval gets shorter we notice  that the returns  behave less and less  like if they were
normally distributed. We can also see that the autocorrelation increases with smaller
intervals and that  the kurtosis clearly  is  too  high for the returns  to be normally
distributed.  Due to the volatile  nature of  the returns we have  not been able to  find a
satisfying  estimate for risk. It is, however, possible to conclude that  Spire  keeps a rather
low market exposure  which is limiting risk. The fact that they  take  both  long and short
positions is decreasing risk in the sense that it makes Spire less sensitive to general
market movements. On the other hand, as always, short positions expose you to
potentially unlimited  price  risk.

Conclusions

Our aim with this thesis was to determine the behaviour of a high frequency trading
firms’ risk and return characteristics. We found almost no prediction power at all on
when Spire is are trading. That indicates that they use much more advanced models to
detect trading opportunities. Since limit orders seem to play a  big role in their strategies it
is unfortunate that  you cannot determine from the data feed who is adding orders, only
who is executing them. Otherwise one  might  have been able to  find  the latency between
the time an order gets added to the book and the time it gets  executed. We think that
such information will exposes a strategy where the HFT firm is posting orders at the
market while measuring the time it takes to get filled. By measuring the latency the HFT
firm tries to determine whether there exists a  hidden order book imbalance in the form
of iceberg orders or dark pools. One could also speculate that advanced machine learning
and pattern recognition algorithms probably can be tuned to detect execution algorithms
such as VWAP trying to execute big orders in the market.
    We think that it is  a bit of a misconception that high frequency trading  is a black box
where you load your algorithms and then let it trade  completely  unsupervised. Qualitative
inspection of data reveals that Spire does not trade some  stock for long periods of time
(by long we mean  hours). In the  talks  we  have  had with APS Capital they confirmed that
algorithms is taken down and re-tuned to better fit current market dynamics.
   It is, as mentioned above, hard  to find a good risk estimate  for the  trading  of HFT
firms.  Anecdotal evidence suggest a simple “pull the plug”  type of risk management. It
quickly tries to get  out of  current positions and take algorithms off the market if  they
start losing money. In light of our results we conjecture  that  factors that determine
relative competitiveness among HFT firms are not as much risk taking as much as
computer power (quite  easy  to obtain  as long  as adequate funding is available), skill  (the
ability develop algorithms) and experience (to be able to  know what works).
   We  would also like to  propose some ideas for future research related to this work as
well as  alternative  research  approaches to this subject in particular.  It would  have  been
nice to have  more data when we were trying to  analyse the behaviour of Spire. Since we
were kindly supplied with data, the limitation was not in getting data but our ability to
process it. We were constantly forced to cut down on data range and data resolution in
order to be able to process it. From the data supplied we were, for example, able to
rebuild the order book. We did, however, not have the computational power to fully
analyse the dynamics of them. Instead we had to settle with using the top of the order
books.
   A more thorough examination would use machine  learning and pattern recognition
to try to reverse-engineer how the algorithms approximately work. This would however
present difficulties, not only of computational complexity, but also by the fact that it is
only possible to identify the participants when an order is executed and not when an
order is placed. Extending the research to other firms and markets could perhaps help
determine the characteristics of markets especially well-suited for high frequency trading.
    Finally, in the section where we present the theoretical background, the references
are not always published in the best journals. Some of them are even working papers.
This has been a problem for us from the very beginning and we think that the lack of
quality research is due to the fact that  this is a very young research field where there is
little empirical research published applicable to this thesis. To give a somewhat adequate
presentation of the current state of the field we had to go beyond most of the prestigious
financial journals. Surely, as the field matures more articles will be published and that will
hopefully make it easier to find empirical research on many diverse topics on high
frequency trading in top ranked journals.

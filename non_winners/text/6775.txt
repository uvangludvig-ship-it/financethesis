When Deposits Run:  An Event Study on  the  Collapse of  Silicon Valley Bank

Abstract:
     We  find that  equity markets  penalized banks for fragile uninsured  deposit funding,
      rather than their duration exposure,  during the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank.  In
      our study  of 185 publicly  traded  U.S. banks, a  one-standard-deviation increase in
      the uninsured deposit ratio yields a 1.85 percentage point more negative cumulative
      abnormal return in the [0, +3] trading day window (coefficient  -0.117, p<0.01),
      while  duration exposure and its interaction with uninsured deposits are  statistically
      insignificant. The return penalty is  concentrated  in banks  above the sample-median
      uninsured deposit ratio of 0.40  (coefficient  -0.287, p<0.01), indicating  a  threshold
      beyond which markets price  run-prone funding.  Daily panel regressions on excess
      returns  provide  no evidence on  market pricing of either fragility measure  pre-
      collapse  in  2022,  and the ex-post coefficient on uninsured deposits is  essentially
      zero. The  event  thus  reveals  a three-stage pattern:  no market pricing pre-collapse,
      instant  repricing  of uninsured deposits  during the collapse,  and  ex-post  resolution
      by regulatory intervention.  In this episode,  market discipline operated only until
      policy intervened.

Keywords:
      Bank Runs, Uninsured Deposits,  Abnormal Returns, Duration Exposure,  Threshold
     Equilibrium

1.  Introduction

      The U.S.  banking industry faced its worst banking stress since the global financial
crisis in March 2023, following coordinated withdrawals that led to the collapse of Silicon
Valley Bank (SVB).  On March 9, withdrawals  reached  USD  42 billion,  with  around
                                                                    1
another USD 100  billion  expected the following day ,  largely due to SVB's fragile
funding structure. Roughly 92.5% of SVB's deposits were uninsured, and they were
mostly held by technology companies funded by venture capitalists (Jiang, Matvos,
Piskorski, and Seru, 2024). Uninsured deposits, along with unrealized losses on long-term
bonds were the mechanisms underlying the collapse and made the bank highly susceptible
to runs. Jiang et al. (2024) illustrate this process as they develop their model  of self-
fulfilling solvency runs that would have threatened the U.S. banking sector had regulators
not intervened.  Furthermore,  Silicon Valley Bank's collapse led to a sharp decline in the
value of bank stocks. The KBW Nasdaq Regional Banking Index, an index of publicly
                                                                       2
traded U.S. regional banks and thrifts, dropped more than 20%  during the period of
                                                                                   3
SVB's collapse. In contrast, the S&P 500 index  declined  by only 5% .
      Thus,  an important question emerges concerning the relationship between  market
discipline  and banking  research,  namely  whether  the  stock market  already  factored in  ex-
ante  bank  balance-sheet vulnerabilities, or  whether  the  event  revealed  systematic
mispricing  of  these weaknesses.  Such  a  question  is  relevant  not only  for  regulatory
requirements  and stakeholder monitoring,  but also  for  research  on  the  performance  of
banks  during systemic  crises.
      We contribute to the literature in four primary ways.  First,  our study extends the
closest comparable empirical  papers. The first is  Martins (2024), who  studies  the joint
SVB-Credit Suisse collapse on a sample of the 100 largest publicly traded U.S. banks. He
finds that uninsured deposits amplified negative cumulative abnormal returns (CARs).
The second is  Choi, Goldsmith-Pinkham, and Yorulmazer (2023), who  study a sample of
224 U.S. banks using cumulative excess returns  relative to  the S&P 500 from February 1
over two windows ending on March 17 and May 25, 2023. They document that uninsured
deposit funding and unrealized losses in held-to-maturity securities scaled by tier 1 capital
are  significantly priced during this period, while held-to-maturity securities over assets is
not.  We extend  these  prior  works  in  several  aspects.  We define the event date  on  March
8, which is the date of the first public announcement regarding SVB’s unrealized losses.
This lets us avoid the contamination Martins (2024) himself warns about in his joint study
and  separates  the run  dynamics  from the  depositor guarantee and Bank Term Funding

profitability, investment behavior, and return momentum.  Moreover,  we estimate panel
regressions of daily excess returns over  balanced  pre-  and post-event  windows  with
formal  Post  and  Post-interaction  terms,  examining  whether  markets  priced  these
fragilities  before and after the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank.  Finally, we provide
evidence on a non-linear  pricing effect through  a  sample-median split  that the linear
specifications used by Choi et al. (2023) and Martins (2024) cannot detect.  Throughout,
our analysis  primarily  builds upon  the theoretical  literature of bank runs  (Diamond and
Dybvig,  1983;  Demirgüç-Kunt  and Detragiache,  2002;  Egan, Hortaçsu, and Matvos,
2017), and  the  mechanisms  analyzed in  Jiang et al. (2024).
       Second, we examine the market pricing of uninsured deposit funding and duration
exposure  over the immediate event window of  the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank.  The
analysis uses  CARs  estimated with  the Fama-French five-factor model with momentum.
We find that  uninsured deposits  were  significantly priced at the 1% level,  with a
coefficient of  -0.117 in the [0, +3] window.  In contrast, duration exposure  is statistically
insignificant, suggesting that banks were penalized for their vulnerability to bank runs
stemming from depositor composition rather than from interest-rate risk arising from their
bond portfolios.
       Third, we investigate whether uninsured deposit exposure was priced prior to
SVB's collapse. Running panel regressions of daily excess returns over a pre-event
window  spanning  213 to 50 days before the collapse, we find no statistically significant
relationship between uninsured deposits and stock returns, robust to both pooled OLS and
firm  fixed  effects.  This result is consistent with the idea that certain kinds of risk can
remain latent, unappreciated by investors, until a relevant shock reveals them,  as  is
evident from the literature on  the 2007-2009 financial crisis (Beltratti and Stulz, 2012).
       Fourth, we  analyze  whether SVB's failure caused any systematic repricing of
banks based on their funding characteristics. We observe a positive and marginally
significant (10%  level)  Post  x  Uninsured Deposits  interaction term in the firm fixed
effects panel regression, while it remains insignificant in the standard OLS panel.
Combined with the severe penalty in the event-study CARs, we  find no evidence of a
systematic repricing.  Instead,  it is  consistent with  a flattening of the pricing schedule,
presumably  due  to the joint guarantee provided for SVB and Signature Bank depositors,
as well as  the initiation of the Bank Term Funding Program.

                                                                      3
       Overall, these findings contribute to the literature on bank crisis performance in
modern-day  banking  systems  characterized  by  digitization  and  rapid  information
diffusion. Beltratti and Stulz (2012) find that, during the 2007-2009 crisis, large banks
with  less short-term capital-market funding fragility, more deposits, more Tier 1 capital,
and less exposure to U.S. real estate performed better. Our results extend this to the
collapse of Silicon Valley Bank and test the market pricing of the mechanisms examined
by Jiang et al. (2024).  We complement Choi et al. (2023) by isolating a short event-
window  response,  using  cumulative  abnormal  returns  based  on  the  Fama-French
framework,  formal pre-  and post-event tests, and by detecting a  threshold for the return
penalty of uninsured deposit funding.  The 2023 episode differs from the 2007-2009 crisis
in that liability-side composition, particularly the share of uninsured deposits, was the
dominant market-priced risk characteristic. This is relevant for bank supervision and for
investor behavior in a setting where deposit outflows can happen within hours.
       Our  study  is conducted  on a sample of 185 publicly  listed  U.S. banks. Balance
sheet  data is  obtained from Capital IQ,  and  uninsured  deposit data  is sourced  from
regulatory call reports  available in  Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS). Stock
returns  are collected  from  the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP).

2.  Theoretical Framework

   2.1.  Uninsured Deposits and Run-Prone Funding Structures

      Diamond and Dybvig (1983)  provide the  canonical model in which  short-term
demandable liabilities are used to finance long-term illiquid assets. In these instances,
even fully solvent banks risk bankruptcy due to coordinated withdrawals arising from
depositors anticipating that other depositors will withdraw their funds. Furthermore,
Egan, Hortaçsu, and Matvos (2017) find that incentives for withdrawal diverge between
uninsured and insured depositors during financial distress. Depositors without insurance
are more likely to withdraw their funds, while those with insurance tend to keep their
deposits unchanged.
      Jiang, Matvos, Piskorski, and Seru (2024) build on these findings in their model of
self-fulfilling solvency runs in the context of the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank.
Specifically, they illustrate that even banks with fully liquid assets are vulnerable to self-
fulfilling solvency runs if they suffer from large asset losses while uninsured deposits
constitute a large proportion of their financing. Moreover, they also highlight that these
self-fulfilling solvency runs would have threatened several U.S. banks after the collapse
of Silicon Valley Bank, if the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), Federal
Reserve, and the U.S. Treasury would not have intervened.
     The theory remains ambiguous about the impact of deposit insurance on these
aspects. Demirgüç-Kunt and Detragiache (2002) point out that insurance reduces the
likelihood of bank runs due to panic, however, it simultaneously weakens monitoring of
bank risk-taking by equity holders, creditors, and depositors. Therefore, a higher ratio of
uninsured deposits reveals banks with high vulnerability to bank runs, which in theory
are also most heavily monitored by its depositors and equity holders. Essentially, it
suggests that equity markets should already have reflected this mechanism in bank stock
prices prior to the crisis. If the effect is absent, it highlights a core aspect of the run channel
and reveals whether market scrutiny was weakened due to deposit insurance.

   2.2.  Duration Exposure and Monetary Tightening

      Banks invest a significant share of funds into fixed-income securities recorded as
held-to-maturity (HTM) or available-for-sale (AFS). AFS securities are recognized at fair
value, while HTM securities are measured at amortized cost. Hanson, Shleifer, Stein, and
Vishny (2015) show that holdings of these long-duration securities rely on a funding
structure characterized by stable deposits, as it enables the owning institution to withstand
early liquidation due to security price volatility. The relationship between a bank’s
interest rate sensitive assets and its liability composition therefore becomes a risk if they
face withdrawals from their depositors.
      This relationship became evident when interest rates were rapidly raised during
2022. Jiang et al. (2024) observe that the entire U.S. banking sector experienced
significant unrealized losses stemming from their holdings of long-duration fixed-income
securities. However, they also underline that the funding structure was pivotal to the crisis
that occurred. Banks with mainly insured retail deposits could withstand considerable

                                                                      5
mark-to-market losses without being threatened by bank runs. Conversely, banks funded
with mainly uninsured deposits instead become vulnerable to large withdrawals as it
forces them to sell their securities at the current market value. Thus, as the market  value
is below book value, it means that to fulfill one dollar of withdrawals, they need to
liquidate assets with a book value above one dollar. Eventually the bank turns insolvent
and unable to meet remaining claims.
      Beltratti and Stulz (2012) establish a useful benchmark with their findings that the
stock market performance of large banks with less funding fragility, more deposits, more
Tier 1 capital, and less exposure to U.S. real estate performed markedly better during the
period July 2007-December 2008 of the global financial crisis. Therefore, a highly
relevant context for our study is whether similar aspects or bank liability structures were
the deciding factors in the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank, which was characterized by a
combination of duration exposure and fragile funding structures.

   2.3.  Market   Pricing   of   Latent   Risks   and   Identification   of   an
      Unfolding Event

      The market pricing of balance sheet vulnerability prior to exposure to an exogenous
shock is a different aspect than its actual presence. In accordance with the efficient market
hypothesis (Fama, 1970), such vulnerabilities should always be reflected in expected
equity returns and valuations. Following Armitage (1995) and MacKinlay (1997), an
event-study enables examination of this conjecture by measuring actual returns compared
to theoretical returns during a systematic event.  Methodological  details  are presented  in
Section 3. Through the event-study we are able to observe the market’s reaction at the
point in time when the variables become salient. In contrast, the panel regression
examines if the variables are systematically priced by investors.
      Despite public access to financial statements, pre-event market pricing may still be
absent. For instance, the moral hazard issue introduced by insured deposits may result in
weak investor monitoring prior to the shock (Demirgüç-Kunt and Detragiache, 2002).
Consequently,  there  are  two  possible  outcomes.  Either  fragility  is  not  reflected
systematically in returns and becomes significantly priced because of the collapse, or
equity valuations already incorporated these balance sheet vulnerabilities beforehand.
      There are also implications regarding the sequential arrival of information during
the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank. It started with announcements of large unrealized
losses and subsequent equity offering, followed by depositor withdrawals and regulatory
intervention (see the timeline in Appendix  E). Thus, we construct multiple short event
windows based on the initial public disclosure for the purpose of our event study. We also
ensure that the panel regressions remain close enough to the event window to  absorb the
market’s valuation adjustments while simultaneously minimizing the impact of event-
induced variance and cross-sectional dependence by excluding the surrounding trading
days.

   2.4.  Hypotheses

    Building on the literature reviewed above, we formulate three hypotheses. These are
stated below.

                                                                      6
      2.4.1. Hypothesis 1 (H1): The Fragility Pricing Hypothesis
   Banks with higher ratios of uninsured deposits and greater duration exposure
experienced significantly more negative cumulative abnormal returns during the collapse
of Silicon Valley Bank.

      2.4.2. Hypothesis 2 (H2): The Ex-Ante Underpricing Hypothesis
    Before the event, neither the uninsured  deposit ratio nor the duration-exposure ratio
was systematically priced in daily bank stock returns.

      2.4.3. Hypothesis 3 (H3): The Ex-Post Pricing Hypothesis
    The collapse of Silicon Valley Bank produced a discrete change in the cross-sectional
pricing of uninsured  deposit funding and duration exposure.

   3.2.  Fragility Measures

       3.2.1.  The  Uninsured Deposit Ratio

funding is captured by the uninsured deposit ratio. Uninsured deposits are those
exceeding the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) insurance limit. Egan,
Hortaçsu, and Matvos (2017) show that demand for uninsured deposits falls sharply as
bank distress increases. This encourages depositors to withdraw funds from banks with
perceived default risk during financial distress.

       3.2.2.  Duration Exposure Ratio
      Banks allocate significant resources to fixed-income securities because stable
deposit funding allows them to withstand price fluctuations without being forced into
early liquidation (Hanson, Shleifer, Stein, and Vishny, 2015). In the case of Silicon Valley
Bank, their deposits were primarily invested in securities classified as HTM (Board of
Governors of the Federal Reserve System, 2023). By year-end 2022, 78% of their
securities holdings were in HTM securities, while the average large banking organization
only had 42%. This was driven by their portfolio shift to HTM securities in January 2021.
Additionally, their HTM holdings had a weighted-average duration of 6.2 years.
      The market value of debt securities is inversely related to interest rates, which
exposes banks with large portfolios of these instruments to significant unrealized losses
during periods of rising interest rates, such as in 2022. Jiang et al. (2024) document that
the 2022-2023 monetary tightening cycle reduced the mark-to-market value of U.S. bank
assets by an average of 10%, implying aggregate unrealized losses of approximately USD
2 trillion. The distribution was skewed, with the median unrealized losses  of a bank
amounting to roughly 9% of assets, while the worst-affected 5% of banks experienced
declines exceeding 20%. Accordingly, HTM securities are used to construct a proxy for
these exposures, which led to forced security sales that precipitated the Silicon Valley
Bank run. To facilitate comparisons across banks and to illustrate balance sheet structure,
we divide HTM securities by total assets.

   3.3.  Event Study Design

      This study investigates the impact of the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank on the
equity performance of U.S. banks. In accordance with Armitage (1995), returns are
analyzed across three short event windows, which are selected based on the event date to
minimize estimation distortion from confounding events and to isolate the immediate
market response. The first public news regarding the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank
emerged on March 8, 2023, when significant unrealized losses in its bond portfolio were
reported. Therefore, this date is defined as t=0, and all days within the constructed event
windows are treated as trading days.
      The three event windows analyzed are [0, +2], [0, +3], and [0, +4]. The shortest
window captures the immediate stock market reaction to the initial announcements (see
Appendix E for the event timeline) and concludes on March 10 (t=2). The second window
extends to March 13, the next trading day, to incorporate news of regulatory intervention
in Silicon Valley Bank, including the subsequent deposit guarantee communicated by the
FDIC, Treasury, and Federal Reserve. The longest window accounts for the possibility of
delayed market valuation adjustments and ends on March 14.
       Expected returns are estimated by the Fama and French (2015) five-factor model,
which extends the three-factor model of Fama and French (1992) by adding profitability
and investment factors.  To improve the performance of the model, it is augmented with
the momentum factor (Carhart, 1997).  Relative to the three-factor specification used in
Martins (2024), the five-factor model with momentum provides a broader control for
systematic return variation, particularly relevant during the 2022–2023 tightening cycle
when profitability, investment intensity, and momentum effects varied substantially
across banks.  The estimation window for factor loadings follows recommendations by
Armitage (1995), spanning 250 to 50 trading days before March 8 (t=-250 to t=-50). This
ensures that anticipation effects or information leakage do not bias the parameter
estimates (MacKinlay, 1997). We obtain the Fama-French factors with momentum and
the market portfolio at a daily frequency from the Fama-French Data Library in WRDS
(Fama and French, 2023).  Daily stock returns are downloaded from CRSP.

   3.4.  Cross-Sectional Regression: H1

      This study conducts cross-sectional regressions of cumulative abnormal returns on
balance-sheet characteristics reported in Q4 2022 to estimate the effects of uninsured
deposits and duration exposure on banks’ returns during the crisis. The  choice of using
pre-event characteristics  follows Beltratti and Stulz (2012) and Ongena, Smith, and
Michalsen (2003),  as it  addresses reverse causality concerns and ensures that the results
reflect  ex-ante  exposures.

where the uninsured deposit to deposit ratio is denoted as  Unins, and  Dur  represents the
duration exposure ratio, is:

  𝐶𝐴𝑅!,4  =  𝛼5  +  𝛽6𝑈𝑛𝑖𝑛𝑠!  +  𝛽7𝐷𝑢𝑟!  +  𝛽8(𝑈𝑛𝑖𝑛𝑠!  ×  𝐷𝑢𝑟!)  +  𝛾′𝐶𝑜𝑛𝑡𝑟𝑜𝑙𝑠!  +  𝜀!,4

      To ensure robustness to the timing of different market reactions, multiple event
windows are constructed as market adjustments may occur over several trading days
(MacKinlay, 1997; Armitage, 1995; Fama, 1970). The inclusion of an interaction term
between uninsured deposits and duration exposure is motivated by Jiang et al. (2024),
who demonstrate that bank vulnerability increases when a fragile deposit composition
coincides with asset-side losses. Variables included in the interaction term are mean-
centered  to reduce multicollinearity. This procedure is done by subtracting the sample-
mean from each observation. HC3 heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors are used to
enhance inference reliability (MacKinnon and White, 1985).

   3.5.  Panel Regressions: H2 and H3

      Panel regressions of daily excess stock returns on balance sheet variables are
conducted to examine whether the market priced duration exposure and uninsured
deposits before and after the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank. We also impose a post-
crisis dummy.  All balance sheet variables are reported quarterly for fiscal year 2022 and
enter the regressions with a 30-day lag after the end of the relevant quarter to avoid look-
ahead bias.
      The regression is estimated over two non-overlapping windows. A pre-event
window of [-213,  -50] and a post-event window of [+50, +200] trading days relative to
t=0.  This provides sufficient observations for estimation and limits each window to
approximately one trading year on either side of the event. Days [-49, +49] are excluded
to avoid contamination from anticipatory investor behavior and idiosyncratic volatility

                                                                     12
related to the event. The exclusion of the event window and the surrounding trading days
is to avoid biased betas and distorted inference due to event-induced variance (Boehmer
et al., 1991; Kolari and Pynnönen, 2010).
      The panel regression, where  Ri,t  is the daily excess return of bank  i  on day  t,  Post  is
a dummy for the post-crisis window, and  F  denotes the Fama-French five-factor model
with momentum, is:

        Ri,t  = α + β1Uninsi  + β2Duri  + β3(Uninsi  × Duri) + β4Post  + β5(Post × Uninsi) +
            β6(Post × Duri) + β7(Post × Uninsi  × Duri) + δʹFt  + γʹ Controlsi  + εi,t.

      All specifications cluster standard errors by both firm and time to account for
residual serial correlation within firms and cross-sectional dependence across firms
within trading days, following Petersen (2009); see also Wooldridge (2010) for a textbook
treatment of clustered standard errors in panel data.  To validate our results in the presence
of potential omitted variable bias, we run robustness checks with firm fixed effects to
account for persistent differences in, for instance, culture, reputation, and business model.
Variables entering interaction terms are mean-centered by demeaning them at the sample
average. We will also run the regressions with the interaction term between uninsured
deposits and duration exposure excluded.

   3.6.  Overlap of Factor Estimation Window and  Panel Regressions

      The overlap between the factor estimation window and the pre-event pricing panel
regression window is intentional and has no implications for the validity of our results.
The event study uses this time period to estimate the betas of the respective banks with
respect to the Fama-French factors and momentum, while the panel regression analyzes
the systemic pricing of balance sheet characteristics.
      The choice is also conservative, as the pre-event window coincides with the period
of rapidly rising interest rates in 2022. Therefore, banks’ exposure to maturity mismatches
and uninsured deposit structures was highly economically relevant, yet is not reflected in
equity returns, as revealed by our results. If anything, this strengthens our finding that the
abnormal returns around March 8 reflect the market’s reaction to the collapse of Silicon
Valley Bank, as it was not a continuation of an already priced  market trend.

   3.7.  Control Variables

      In addition to the Fama-French five-factor model with momentum, the following
control variables, with data obtained from Capital IQ, are used for the purpose of this
study:
      Total Assets:  Bank size is measured as the logarithm of total assets. Size proxies
for “too-big-to-fail” expectations and perceived government support, both of which may
influence equity valuation (Beltratti and Stulz, 2012; O’Hara and Shaw, 1990).

                                                                     13
      Liquidity:  Liquidity is measured as cash and cash equivalents scaled by total assets.
Banks with stronger liquidity buffers are better positioned to meet sudden short-term
obligations (Carré and Klossner,  2024;  Acharya, Shin, and Yorulmazer, 2010).
      Tier 1 Capital Ratio:  The  tier  1 capital ratio, defined as  tier  1 capital  over  risk-
weighted assets, controls for regulatory capital strength. Well-capitalized banks are more
capable of absorbing losses, which reduces vulnerability during crises (Cecchetti,  Kress,
and Schoenholtz,  2025).
      Profitability:  Return on assets is used as the measure of profitability. Higher
profitability is associated with stronger cash flows and,  an improved ability to respond to
adverse shocks. Profitability is commonly included as a control variable in empirical
banking literature (e.g., Beltratti and Stulz, 2012;  Ongena, Smith, and Michalsen, 2003).
      Post-event Dummy:  A binary variable equal to one for observations subsequent to
the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank. It captures shifts in investor sentiment regarding the
banking sector.

      The explanatory power of our model is low, as the R values in Table 4 range from
0.038 to 0.085 across all regressions. In the unfolding of this sudden panic run, variation
in   the   cross-section   of   returns   is   mainly   driven   by   investor   coordination   and
communication, such as attention on social media, depositor composition, and correlated
beliefs (Cookson, Fox, Gil-Bazo, Imbet, and Schiller, 2026). These aspects are not
reflected in balance sheet variables. Cookson et al. (2026) illustrate that  a one-standard-
deviation increase in  pre-existing  Twitter exposure resulted in a 4.3  percentage point
larger stock market loss  during the run period. Moreover, they find that pre-exposure did
not displace classical bank-run channels; rather it was the interaction with uninsured
deposit funding and mark-to-market losses that was the main factor. The balance sheet
variables account  only for the component of return variation that the market priced, not
the risk channels per se. This study aims to examine whether markets priced these
mechanics rather than their explanatory power for returns, hence the low explanatory
power is not a major concern.

                                                                     16
       4.2.1.  Uninsured Deposit Ratio
      The uninsured deposit ratio negatively affects cumulative abnormal returns in all
three windows across all specifications. It is only statistically significant in the [0, +3]
and [0, +4] windows. In  columns  5 and 6, the coefficient on uninsured deposits is  -0.117
(significant at the 1% level) and  -0.079 (significant at the 5% level), respectively. All else
equal, a  10-percentage  point  increase in the uninsured deposit  ratio  hence yields a 1.17
percentage point and 0.79 percentage point more negative cumulative abnormal return. A
                         8
one standard deviation  increase in  the  uninsured deposit  ratio  results in about a 1.85
percentage point more negative cumulative abnormal return in the [0, +3] window, which
corresponds to approximately 21% of the  cross-sectional  standard deviation  of  CARs  of
8.87%. This result remains robust for regressions 2 and 3 without the interaction term,
with coefficients of  -0.120 and  -0.083. Significance levels remain at 1% and 5%.  The
negative and significant pricing of uninsured deposits is also consistent with the findings
of Martins (2024), despite differences in expected-return specification (our Fama-French
five-factor model with momentum versus Martins’ three-factor model), event-window
construction, and sample composition, suggesting that the result is robust to alternative
methodological choices.
      This result validates H1, as investors penalized banks with run-prone funding
structures. Uninsured depositors have strong incentives to withdraw funds during
financial distress, leading to a high proportion of uninsured deposits becoming an
amplified run  risk, a finding consistent with both Egan et al. (2017) and Diamond and
Dybvig (1983). It indicates that investors reassessed pre-existing vulnerabilities that were
ignored prior to the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank.  The result  is also compatible with
Choi et al. (2023)  who document a coefficient of  -0.035  on the standardized uninsured
deposit share in their multivariate specification. The larger  magnitude in our short-
window estimate is  consistent with the interpretation that the immediate investor panic
on March  8-14 was partially attenuated in their longer window due to the regulatory
intervention on March 12.
      Another  explanation  is  that  uninsured  deposits  serve  as  a  proxy  for  bank
vulnerability to runs and as measure of ex-ante depositor monitoring (Demirgüç-Kunt and
Detragiache, 2002). Larger shares of uninsured deposits should  attract  more monitoring,
which suggests that stock prices should already have reflected this factor. However, our
results indicate that pre-collapse monitoring was weak. This discussion is covered in
detail in Section 5 with panel regression insights.

       4.2.2.  Duration Exposure Ratio
      The duration exposure ratio is not statistically significant in any specification, and
the coefficient ranges from  -0.062 to  -0.135. Despite the collapse being partly triggered
by unrealized losses on long-term bonds, banks were not penalized for exposure  to this
risk. The result is consistent with Jiang et al. (2024), who underline that the asset-side
alone could not have driven the collapse. A bank with large holdings of HTM securities
and primarily insured retail depositors is faced with weaker withdrawal incentives than a
bank funded through concentrated, large deposits by major companies and institutions.

       8
        As reported in Table 1, the standard deviation for  the  uninsured deposit  ratio  is 15.8%.

Therefore, it is the funding structure that is the vulnerability, which was penalized by
investors.
      The absence of significance for the duration exposure ratio is unlikely to be caused
by multicollinearity. In Table 2, the reported correlation between the duration exposure
ratio and uninsured deposit ratio ranges from approximately 0.10 to 0.15. Hence, the
relationship is either truly insignificant, or it arises from elsewhere.
      A major concern is that  our study’s duration exposure proxy may be subject to
attenuation   bias,   introducing   measurement   error.   Jiang   et   al.   (2024)   state   that
discrepancies in estimated losses can arise from assumptions about asset characteristics
that are not directly observable  in call reports. Furthermore, the measure neglects the
bonds’ maturity structure and banks’ hedging strategies for interest rate risk.  A bank that
primarily holds 20% of assets in short-term treasury bills ends up with the same ratio as
a bank with 20% of assets in 10-year mortgage-backed securities, with the latter having
significantly higher exposure to interest-rate risk.  Thus, the absence of a statistically
significant relationship could be the result of an imperfect proxy.  This outcome is
consistent with Choi et al. (2023), who also report this measure as insignificant when
jointly included with  unrealized HTM losses divided by tier 1 capital. The pattern
reinforces our concerns of a coarse proxy.  With access to unrealized-loss data and
security-level maturity structures as in Jiang et al. (2024), we thus argue that this problem
can be mitigated.

       4.2.3. Interaction Between Uninsured Deposits and Duration Exposure
      As reported across regressions 4 to 6 in Table 4 above, the interaction term between
uninsured deposits and duration exposure is between 0.335 and 0.558 in every event
window, however statistically insignificant throughout. This result is inconsistent with
Jiang et al. (2024) whose model predicts that large duration exposure should amplify the
impact  of  uninsured  deposit  funding,  and  vice  versa.  We  develop  two  possible
interpretations of this result.
      The first  is based on  the contamination from the potential measurement error in our
duration proxy, as mentioned in  Section 4.2.2 above. Thus, the insignificance could
merely be a consequence of this shortcoming.  Choi et al. (2023) also document  an
insignificant interaction between uninsured deposits and the proportion of HTM holdings,
while the  interaction term between the share of uninsured deposits and unrealized HTM
losses over tier 1 capital  appears significant.  Hence,  the interacting effect  emphasized  by
Jiang et al. (2024)  appears to be detectable only when realized economic loss  exposure is
reflected directly in the proxy.
      The second explanation, however, suggests that investors evaluated fragility more
broadly than Jiang et al. (2024) predict in their model. Banks were penalized for investors’
perception of run-prone funding structures, irrespective of their unrealized losses in their
security   portfolios.   Investors   had   limited   time   to   analyse   bank   balance   sheet
characteristics during the window spanning from Wednesday, March 8, to the markets’
close on Friday evening. The uninsured deposit figures are easily identified and computed
from publicly available information, while the exposure to mark-to-market losses
highlighted by Jiang et al. (2024) requires combining approximated estimations of
unrealized losses with deposit characteristics. Table 4 above discloses that the coefficient
on the uninsured deposit ratio is barely affected by the interaction term. For instance, in

                                                                     18
column 2, the coefficient is  -0.120, while the measure’s corresponding coefficient in the
regression in column 5 is  -0.117, which is consistent with this view.
      Overall, we find evidence in support of H1 for fragile funding with uninsured
deposits as it is significantly priced by investors. We also find a qualified null for duration
exposure and the interaction between the two variables. The discussion is developed
further in Section 5.2.

       4.3.1.  H2: Was Fragility Priced  Before  the Collapse?
      As reported in Table 5,  the  main  specification coefficient on uninsured deposits in
column 2 is  -0.0003, with the corresponding no-interaction estimate at  -0.0004.  Neither
is statistically significant.  The same applies to the duration exposure measure, which  in
the interaction model has a coefficient of 0.0002 and in the no-interaction model  has a
coefficient  of  0.0005.  Finally, the interaction term is also insignificant with a coefficient
estimate of 0.0090. Thus, we fail to reject  H2 as neither fragility  measure was priced by
the equity markets prior to the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank.

                                                                     20
      This  outcome  is highly informative,  as the pre-event window  spans  one of the most
aggressive monetary-tightening cycles  in history. Investors had access to financial
statements and  quarterly reports at all times,  from which  exposure to  long-duration assets
and fragile funding  is  observable. Hence, investors should  be aware of the implications.
However, as our results  show, returns show no significant pricing of either factor. This
aligns with  the  empirical findings  of  Demirgüç-Kunt and  Detragiache  (2002), who state
that  monitoring  by equity investors and depositors may  weaken  if deposits are insured.
We also acknowledge that  the  absence of  significant pricing of fragility in excess returns
does not imply that  market prices were not adjusted to these risks. For instance,  banks
may have different price-to-book ratios  due to  their  exposure  to fragility, which is not
necessarily reflected in daily stock returns. Therefore, our null result concludes that
fragility delivered no abnormal returns during this period and should not be treated as
evidence  that stock prices did not reflect these vulnerabilities.

       4.3.2.  H3: Did the Collapse Trigger a Structural Repricing?
       To evaluate H3, we focus on the interaction of our fragility variables with the post-
event dummy. From column 2 in Table 5 above we show that all fragility measures remain
statistically insignificant in the post-period, i.e., the event did not result in a structural
repricing of these risk channels. In column 2, Post x Uninsured Deposits has a coefficient
of 0.0018  and in column 1 it is 0.0019.  Post x Duration Exposure  shows an estimate of  -
0.00003 in column 2 and  -0.0004 in column 1. Finally,  Post x Duration Exposure x
Uninsured Deposits has a coefficient of -0.0113. Thus, we do not find consistent evidence
in support of  H3.  In Table 8 under  Section 4.4.3 we report  a firm fixed effects
specification, which  shows significance at the 10% level for both  Post x Uninsured
Deposits  and  Post x Uninsured Deposits x Duration Exposure.
      Analyzing the coefficients closer and disregarding the significance contributes
further to the findings.  For instance, the pre-event slope on uninsured deposits in column
2  of Table 5  is  -0.0003  at the mean of duration exposure. Post-event the slope instead
becomes  -0.0003 + 0.0018 = 0.0015. In the firm fixed effects model  (Table 8, column 2),
the slope pre-event is  -0.0030 and the post-event slope  is  -0.0030 + 0.0023 =  -0.0007.  For
the interaction term, the  post-event  slopes are  -0.0023  and  -0.0050, respectively.  This
means that the  coefficient  is basically zero  and that the fragile banks did not outperform
following the collapse.  Combining this result with the pre-collapse and event-window
findings illustrates the different stages of the event, where fragility was not systematically
reflected in stock returns before the  collapse,  however  it  resulted in lower cumulative
abnormal returns during the event, and then regulatory intervention  mitigated the panic.
The discussion is  developed further in  Section 5.3. Hence, based on the marginal
significance in only one model, our rejection of H3 remains.

mainly  of  retail-funded banks, thus  with  lower uninsured deposit ratios.  In turn, the
residual variation may be a proxy for  e.g.,  commercial banking activities,  rather than
solely  a fragility funding channel.  Particularly since the  effect is insignificant in the
above-median group where the run channel is present.

all   specifications.   However,   the   concerns   described   in   4.2.2   regarding   potential
measurement error  remain.
      In conclusion, the findings reported in Table 7  strengthen the original insights from
Table 4.  The uninsured funding structure is a risk channel priced by investors;  however,
it is  contingent  on the institutions’ level of uninsured deposits.  Banks with higher shares
of uninsured deposits experienced lower returns  during the event  and vice versa.

5.  Discussion

   5.1.  Uninsured Deposit  Fragility as the Primary Pricing Channel
      Our findings that the cross-sectional negative pricing of exposure to uninsured
deposits in the event window  is  robust, statistically significant, and  concentrated among
banks with  above-median  uninsured deposit ratios  align with Diamond and Dybvig
(1983). When a bank is funded by short-term demandable deposits and invests those funds
in  long-term,  illiquid assets, it enables multiple equilibria. The first is a no-bank-run
equilibrium in which only depositors who need  liquidity withdraw,  resulting  in optimal
risk sharing. There is also an equilibrium for bank  runs,  where depositors  withdraw their
funds because they  anticipate  others will do the same. This occurs even for fully solvent
banks.  However, the asymmetrical pricing effect between the above-  and below-median
groups  introduces  considerations  beyond  panic-driven coordination. Egan et al. (2017)
show that  demand for uninsured deposits falls sharply  when a bank is perceived to be in
financial distress,  whereas demand for  insured deposits remains  largely  unaffected.  This
insurance status asymmetry reveals the connection between solvency and news regarding
withdrawal pressure through a depositor channel. A bank’s exposure to runs is dependent
on the uninsured proportion of  its  funding,  as insured depositors do not react.  All the
potential equilibria  are generated by the strength of this  depositor response.  Therefore,
the uninsured deposit ratio is a proxy for this identified run risk,  where the  likelihood
increases with weak bank fundamentals. It is not solely a panic-driven coordination
equilibrium independent of a bank’s financial position.
      The resulting pattern aligns with both the Diamond-Dybvig framework and the non-
linear  market  pricing.  Markets  penalized  above-median  institutions  through  more
negative  cumulative abnormal returns during the event window, while the effect on
below-median banks  was  either not statistically significant or resulted in  less negative
returns, as reported in column 3 of Table 7. This non-linearity was revealed by the median
split of the sample.  Thus, we can conclude that there is a  critical level, after which
investors begin to penalize banks for their exposure to uninsured deposits and self-
fulfilling  bank runs.
      Conversely, it reveals another  aspect  of  the discussion. A high ratio of uninsured
deposits should  accordingly increase  stakeholder  monitoring to control  firm  risk-taking
(Demirgüç-Kunt and Detragiache, 2002).  Ex ante  effective monitoring should  therefore
already be reflected in pre-event stock prices, resulting in lower abnormal return penalties
during the event.  The observed negative cumulative abnormal returns during the event,
as well as  the failure to reject H2, therefore indicate that the dominant mechanism during
this episode was  bank-run vulnerability and not depositor monitoring.  Consequently,
depositor insurance thus results in weaker monitoring,  and fragility is only priced in
response to an exogenous shock, as illustrated by the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank.
      The non-linear pattern also provides insights relative to Martins (2024), who reports
a significantly negative pooled linear coefficient on uninsured deposits similar  to our
coefficient of  -0.117  in the [0, +3] window in both magnitude and sign.  Our results from
the median-split in Table 7 indicate that such a pooled linear estimate is best interpreted
as a  sample-weighted average, with the negative effect concentrated in the upper half of
the distribution  and a positive or insignificant effect in the lower  half.

                                                                     27
   5.2.  Why Funding Fragility Was Priced and Duration Exposure Was
     Not
     The  most interesting  asymmetry  throughout our results is that  uninsured deposits
are  significantly and negatively priced, while duration exposure remains insignificant in
all settings.  Based on this,  we develop three  possible reasons discussed below.
     Measurement  error in our duration exposure proxy constitutes the first reason.
While it captures the balance-sheet composition of HTM securities relative to total assets,
it fails to  reflect the actual maturity structure of the underlying securities  and other
interest-rate hedging activities.  Jiang et al. (2024)  note  that  estimates of unrealized losses
are highly sensitive to assumptions  about  asset characteristics  that are  not directly
observable in call reports.  The  outcome is attenuation bias in the variable that affects the
final coefficient. Moreover,  as reported  in Table 2,  the  correlation between our fragility
variables  ranges  from 0.10 to 0.15.  Thus, we  eliminate multicollinearity as the  reason for
the  consistent insignificance of our duration exposure variable.  It is therefore fully
possible that the  market prices duration exposure,  and our imperfect proxy  fail  to  capture
the effect.
      The second  possibility arises from the conditional pricing  evidence from Jiang et
al. (2024),  who show that  losses on the asset  side of the balance sheet  are contingent on
a run-prone funding structure.  A bank  with  a  high allocation to HTM securities  and
funded mainly  by  insured retail depositors  does not face the threat of coordinated
withdrawals that  a bank funded by uninsured depositors does.  Consequently,  the banks’
mark-to-market losses do not result  in  a  high probability of forced liquidation.  This
essentially means that  the insignificance of  our duration exposure ratio  is the expected
result. However, it also suggests that the interaction  between duration exposure and
uninsured deposits  should be significant, which is not the case, as shown in  Section 4.2.3.
This remains ambiguous, although we refer to the  potential measurement error for an
alternative explanation.
     The most developed interpretation is the time  pressure investors  faced upon
receiving  information  about  Silicon Valley Bank.  From the March 8  announcement
covering large losses on securities and a planned equity offering  to the March 10
resolution, investors had  only  48 hours to identify  balance sheet vulnerabilities across the
banking sector.  The uninsured deposit ratio is easily  observable from call reports, while
unrealized losses are more difficult to estimate  by combining multiple noisy estimates, as
highlighted by Jiang et al. (2024).  Therefore, these aspects  are consistent with the view
that markets  price banks based  on their vulnerability to bank  runs rather than their
duration exposure in their security holdings.  The coefficient on uninsured deposits in  the
[0, +3] window of  Table 4  reflects this as well.  With the interaction term,  the coefficient
is  -0.117;  without it, it  is  -0.120. It is essentially unaffected by the interaction term’s
presence.

   5.3.  Interpretation of the Post-Event Flattening
     Now we provide an interpretation of the results reported in Section 4.3.2 and the
firm fixed effects analogue in Section 4.4.3. In both cases, the implied post-collapse slope
on uninsured deposits is almost flat (+0.0015 in pooled OLS and  -0.0007 with firm fixed
effects). Together with the inability to reject H2 during the pre-collapse period, this
provides a coherent three-stage dynamic in which the fragile-deposit pricing does not

                                                                     28
exist ex ante, becomes heavily priced during the acute period (see Table 4), and stops
being priced following the re-calibration of tail risk via policy intervention. The  Post  x
Uninsured Deposits  coefficient should therefore be seen as the return to a regime of no
pricing rather than a re-pricing toward greater fragility of banks.  We maintain our
interpretation, as the flat slope precludes any alternative interpretation in terms of
rewarding fragile banks post-event, as a positive sign on the  Post  x  Uninsured Deposits
interaction would superficially suggest.
      The most plausible rationale behind the flatness of the schedule is, unsurprisingly,
policy intervention. On Sunday, March 12, 2023, the U.S. Treasury Department, the
Federal Reserve, and the FDIC jointly announced guarantees of all deposits at SVB and
Signature Bank, including those above the FDIC insurance limits. On the same day, the
Federal Reserve also introduced the Bank Term Funding Program, whereby depository
institutions could borrow at par value against eligible securities, removing the forced sale
of assets channel  (Arseneau, Klee, Kotidis, and Siemer, 2025).  Both policies altered the
equilibrium considerations of uninsured depositors at other fragile banks: the signal that
the regulators were prepared to offer depositor protection above the statutory limit for
these specific banks  implied that the costs of holding uninsured deposits were lower at
other fragile banks, while the BTFP meant that an  deposit run would not translate into a
solvency event anymore. In equilibrium, these changes reduce the price discount on
stocks of banks with large amounts of uninsured deposits, resulting in a precise prediction
of our empirical finding. The interpretation is in line with the  classic  government-
guarantee literature on bank fragility (Diamond  and  Dybvig,  1983), which shows that a
credible government guarantee can solve the coordination problem among depositors and
eliminate the run equilibrium without changing the fundamentals of the bank.
      Another interpretation is that the flat schedule is due to fundamental improvements
in bank balance sheets due to deposit inflows, capital injections, or effective balance sheet
management  post-crisis.  While  this  cannot  be  entirely  excluded,  there  are  two
observations in our dataset that are not consistent with such an interpretation. First, the
speed of adjustment,  with the uninsured deposit schedule already flattened in the window
[+50, +200],  is too fast to be consistent with the usual balance sheet adjustment timeline.
Second, the flattening happens precisely in the uninsured deposits dimension while
capital levels, liquidity positions, and profits remain unaffected and are not loaded by any
relevant interactions. This makes an expectations-based explanation driven by the
government backstop more appealing.
     The only marginally significant interaction coefficient Post x Duration x Uninsured
with an estimate of  -0.0131 and significant at the 10% level (Table 8 column 2),  implies
the existence of a residual structural pricing channel. The incomplete nature of post-event
flattening among doubly fragile banks suggests that the stock market applied a run-risk
discount for such banks even following the government guarantee policy. We interpret
this result as a signal that, despite flattening of the  deposit pricing channel, the joint asset-
liability pricing channel formalized by Jiang et al. (2024) remained in place.

   5.4.  Identification,  Endogeneity, and Limitations
      The  study’s  methodology  is based on ex ante  balance-sheet  characteristics  of U.S.
banks.  Therefore, differences in  bank  returns  reflect  investors’ evaluations of the
vulnerabilities that these structures entail. However,  there are some  methodological
concerns  and limitations  that we  must  discuss.

                                                                     29
      The first  potential issue is  omitted variable bias in the CAR regressions  across  the
defined  event  windows.  Banks  with  high uninsured deposit ratios may  also  have
differences in  depositor  industry, depositor  concentration, and business model.  These
factors  may all be  associated  with their perceived fragility and returns. We control for
size, liquidity, profitability,  and  capitalization,  which partially mitigates the omitted
variable bias concern.  Another relief follows  from  our findings in Table 7,  the sample
median split,  which  reveals that the negative market pricing of uninsured deposits is
concentrated  among banks with high uninsured deposit  ratios.  The pricing effect being
the strongest in this location of the distribution  is more consistent  with the  mechanism of
the event  and  the  literature than an omitted variable.
     Another concern is our sample selection. The initial sample comprises around  650
U.S. banks, which  is reduced to  185 after filtering the data. Many of the  removed banks
were smaller and traded over  the  counter. Consequently, our sample  becomes biased
toward larger institutions, which,  by definition,  are  more transparent and  receive  greater
investor  coverage.  Therefore,  this has implications for the  null result on H2  and makes
the failure to reject conservative, as  the banks  that  are less scrutinized by the  market are
excluded from the study.  The CAR regressions  examining H1  do not suffer the same
weakness, as  they  focus on within-sample variation.
      The  third  concern is potential measurement error in the duration exposure proxy,
which  has been  mentioned  in Sections 4.2.2 and 5.2.  It fails to capture  the  actual
heterogeneity  in  banks’ true interest-rate exposure  because  the maturity structure is not
reflected  in  it. A bond that matures in 30 years and a  treasury bill would  yield the same
duration exposure,  according to our proxy. However, their true exposure to interest rates
is  very different.  It also fails to capture hedging methods that banks may  use.  Therefore,
the   insignificance   of   duration   exposure   throughout   this   study   may   arise   from
measurement error, in addition to the conditional pricing  finding  discussed by Jiang et al.
(2024).  The validity of the results can be substantially improved by  having access to
detailed data on the actual security holdings of all banks,  and  by  reducing  reliance on
individual  assumptions when combining multiple noisy estimates to calculate unrealized
losses.
      Finally,  the  ex-post  panel regression  fails to cleanly distinguish the impact of the
policy response from other contemporaneous potential adjustments.  These include
changes in  deposit flows and changes in funding costs, both of which  may affect bank
valuations.  With access to data on deposit flows and unrealized losses  at  the bank  level,
e.g.,  as in Jiang et al. (2024), the analysis  can cover  the mechanisms underlying the
collapse with even greater confidence.
     We additionally note  that Choi et al. (2023) examine aspects of the SVB collapse
that  we do not, particularly bank-size and too-big-to-fail effects,  anticipation  measured
through daily coefficients on 2022 balance-sheet vulnerabilities,  and the role of cash
holdings separate from broader liquidity measures.  We treat these as natural extensions
to our study, given that  we primarily analyze the  market pricing of the  main fragility
mechanisms  around the event itself.

                                                                     30
6.  Conclusion

      The collapse of Silicon Valley Bank provides a natural setting to examine whether
equity markets priced the mechanism underlying the event across U.S. bank stocks. We
use the event to examine whether investors systematically priced uninsured  deposit
funding and duration exposure on a sample of 185 publicly traded U.S. banks before,
during, and after the collapse.
       Focusing on the [0, +3] window during the event, we find  partial  support for H1,
as equity markets penalized banks funded by uninsured deposits. The coefficient on the
uninsured  deposit ratio is  -0.117 and significant at the 1% level. A ten-percentage-point
increase in the uninsured deposit ratio thus implies a 1.17 percentage point more negative
cumulative abnormal return (CAR), and a one-standard-deviation increase implies a 1.85
percentage point  more negative CAR, equivalent to roughly 21% of the  cross-sectional
standard deviation of CARs  during the window. The result is robust to the removal of
outliers, although the coefficient attenuates to  -0.101, still significant at the 1% level. To
test whether the pricing effect is linear, we also split the sample at the median uninsured
deposit ratio of 0.40 and find that the negative effect exists only in the upper part of the
sample: the coefficient in the [0, +3] window is  -0.287 and significant at the 1% level.
Conversely, the lower part of the distribution has a positive coefficient of 0.125 in the [0,
+4] window, significant at the 5% level. There is therefore a critical level beyond which
investors penalize banks for their uninsured  deposit exposure.
       Duration exposure, measured as HTM securities divided by total assets, is
statistically insignificant across all specifications. The interaction term between duration
exposure and uninsured deposits is also insignificant, except for the below-median
estimates in the [0, +2] and [0, +3] windows when the sample is split at the median
uninsured  deposit ratio of 0.40, although we remain cautious regarding this result. The
stand-alone insignificance is consistent with Jiang et al. (2024), who find that asset-side
losses alone could not have caused the collapse of Silicon Valley Bank without the fragile
funding structure. By the same logic, however, their model would predict a significant
interaction term, which we do not observe. The most likely explanation is measurement
error in our duration-exposure proxy, which also applies to its stand-alone insignificance
throughout the study.
        We run panel regressions of daily excess returns over the pre-event window [-213,
-50] trading days relative to the event date and find no statistically significant relationship
with either fragility measure. We therefore fail to reject H2, the ex-ante underpricing of
these vulnerabilities. After the event, we run similar panel regressions over the [+50,
+200] window. The coefficient on uninsured deposits is 0.0015 in our pooled OLS
specification and  -0.0007 with firm fixed effects.  Both are  essentially  zero. The
corresponding interaction coefficient (Post × Uninsured Deposits)  is significant at the
10% level  only in the firm  fixed  effects specification (Table 8).  This is most plausibly

                                                                     31
explained by  the depositor guarantee  and  Bank  Term  Funding  Program  issued by the
Treasury, the Fed, and the FDIC on March 12, which relieved systemic stress and brought
back the ex-ante investor sentiment. Hence, we do not find consistent evidence in support
of  H3.
       These results contribute to the existing literature on the empirical phenomenon of
bank runs. Diamond and Dybvig (1983) provide the theoretical foundations and define
the conditions under which runs emerge, and Goldstein and Pauzner (2005) extend the
framework to obtain a unique threshold equilibrium. Egan, Hortaçsu, and Matvos (2017)
document how uninsured depositors respond to distress through withdrawals while
insured depositors do not.  Choi et al. (2023)  provide the closest comparable empirical
evidence. They find cross-sectional pricing  of uninsured deposits and unrealized HTM
losses on a different sample using long-horizon cumulative excess returns  from  February
1 through March 17 and May 25, 2023.  Our findings establish  similar results to theirs  on
uninsured deposits using a short-event window design that isolates the market reaction
from the March 12 regulatory intervention.  We  provide  additional results  on how equity
markets price  the  mechanisms underlying the collapse  empirically.  Fragility is priced  in
a non-linear pattern  above the threshold but not below,  consistent with the threshold
equilibrium of Goldstein and Pauzner (2005). Compared with Jiang et al. (2024), whose
model has duration exposure and depositor fragility  interactively  producing  risk, our
evidence shows that markets aggressively priced the latter without pricing the former or
the joint interaction. This  is consistent  with the findings of Choi et al. (2023)  who also
document  an  insignificant HTM divided by assets variable as well as its interaction with
the uninsured deposit share.
       Several limitations require attention. Our duration-exposure proxy does not
control for the maturity structure or for hedging strategies banks may have employed to
manage interest-rate risk, introducing potential attenuation bias of uncertain size that
could explain the consistently insignificant results for duration exposure and the
interaction term. The 185 banks in our final sample represent approximately one  quarter
of the initial sample, and the excluded banks have a median log-asset value of 20.7 versus
23 in the final sample. Our results are therefore biased toward larger institutions with
higher stakeholder monitoring pressures, which makes the failure to reject ex-ante
underpricing conservative. Finally, the post-event panel regressions cannot fully  separate
the effect of regulatory intervention from contemporaneous changes in deposit flows and
bank funding costs.
       Three  main extensions follow from our study. First, incorporating security-level
maturity, unrealized  losses, and derivative-position data would allow testing whether the
conditional pricing hypothesis of Jiang et al. (2024) holds once the threat of attenuation
bias   is   removed.   Second,   including   depositor-concentration   measures,   depositor
characteristics, and the social-media exposure measures developed by Cookson et al.
(2026) would help discipline the unobservable coordination channel.  Finally, extending
the analysis to European banks following the emergency takeover of Credit Suisse by

                                                                     32
UBS would test whether the same pricing mechanisms operate under different regulatory
and depositor-protection regimes.

Appendix A: Definitions
      Bank fragility:  The proportion of uninsured deposits of total deposits, duration
exposure ratio as well as the interaction between the two.
      Fragile funding: A bank with a high ratio of deposits being uninsured, which leaves
the bank  more  vulnerable to runs.
     Uninsured deposits:  Deposits  exceeding the FDIC  coverage  limit of USD 250,000
per bank, per depositor.
      Duration exposure:  A banks exposure to interest-rate risk based on the share of
held-to-maturity securities to total assets.
     Bank run: A coordinated withdrawal of deposits by a bank’s depositors that renders
solvency concerns self-fulfilling.

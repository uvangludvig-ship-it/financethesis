The Fund Flow Impact on Stock Returns: A Study on the Impact of Fund Flows on
Stock Returns

Abstract:
   This study examines the relationship between mutual fund flows and stock returns in the
   Swedish equity market. We measure each stock’s exposure to fire-sale risk by examining
   its ownership structure and the share of free float held by mutual funds. Our results yield a
   positive relationship between fund flows and stocks’ return, with a stronger relationship
   for outflows than inflows. We also find evidence of temporary mispricing caused by fund
   flows: stocks subject to flow-driven price pressure tend to reverse as market liquidity
   normalizes. However, our back tested trading strategy designed to exploit this pattern
   underperformed the market, indicating that generating practical, risk-adjusted excess
   returns is difficult.

Keywords: Mutual fund flows, Fire-sale risk, Liquidity shocks, Ownership Structure, Price
Pressure

1. Introduction
A central challenge in asset management is the constant risk of investor redemptions, which
can force rapid and inefficient liquidation of portfolio holdings. The withdrawal can be made
usually within days leaving the fund manager with a limited time of liquidating the
investment in the portfolio. As a result, the fund manager may be forced to sell below the
fundamental value due to the shortage of time (Coval and Stafford, 2007). This poses a fire-
sale risk and explains how the occurrence becomes enhanced when it occurs at scale. As a
result, the occurrence at scale could drive prices down, with reversals occurring as liquidity
returns to the market. This mechanism illustrates how flows can induce a non-fundamental
price (Aragon and Kim, 2023).
   Sweden stands out among other countries when considering fire-sale risk. Firstly, Swedish
households hold around 10% of their total financial assets in investment funds and 7% in
listed equities, placing them among the leading countries in Europe (OECD, 2025).
Moreover, approximately 70% of all Swedes hold private mutual funds, illustrating how
widespread retail investing is in Sweden (Fondbolagens förening, 2024). Secondly, over the
past decade, Swedish households have shifted their investment strategies — moving away
from direct equity ownership towards indirect equity investments through funds, which has
led to a substantial increase in fund ownership (OECD 2025). Thirdly, despite Sweden’s
strong savings culture, the financial knowledge is limited, where for instance 4 out 10 people
believes funds are riskier than stocks (Finansinspektionen, 2023). Lastly, evidence shows that
retail investors tend to liquidate their fund holdings due to financial constraints, suggesting
that many investments are not based on fundamental market valuation (Fondbolagens
förening, 2024). The combination of a broad retail investor base, large capital flows into
mutual funds, and limited understanding of underlying securities raises an important
question: in a relatively small equity market like Sweden’s, could these dynamics amplify the
risk of fire sales and contribute to non-fundamental stock price movements?
   This paper makes three main contributions. Firstly, our research establishes the
relationship between fund flows and stocks with a greater degree of fire-sale exposure.
Earlier research has found a significant relationship in, for instance, the U.S. equity market,
but we provide evidence that the same phenomenon exists within smaller equity markets such
as Sweden (Aragon and Kim 2023). Secondly, we demonstrate that the commonly used
Capital Asset Pricing Model developed by William Sharpe (1964) does not fully capture the
drivers of stock returns. By including a constructed beta that reflects fund-flow sensitivity,
we obtain a more comprehensive picture of the relationship between traditional factors and
the return of a stock. Thirdly, our main finding shows how the inefficiencies associated with
fire-sale risk potentially could be exploited by retail investors. By constructing a practical
trading strategy, we illustrate how retail investors in theory could be able to act on these
inefficiencies in the Swedish equity market.
   While most prior research on fire-sale risk has focused on large and liquid markets such as
the United States, considerably less is known about how these dynamics unfold in smaller,
more concentrated markets. By extending the analysis to Sweden, our study contributes to the
literature by examining how market size and investor composition shape the transmission of
liquidity shocks. This perspective allows for an assessment of whether fire-sale mechanisms
are universal features or if they are particularly pronounced in markets where retail
participation and ownership concentration are high. Moreover, by focusing on Sweden, a
country characterized by a strong savings culture and a large fund-based investment system,
we highlight how even well-developed but relatively small markets can be vulnerable to
systematic flow-induced price distortions.

                                     3
   In summary, our research illustrates how an extensive passive retail investor base often
without detailed knowledge of the underlying holdings can induce non-fundamental price
movements. This finding challenges the efficient market hypothesis by showing that stock
prices are not determined solely by fundamentals, but also by investor flows and the liquidity
dynamics they generate.
   All this in consideration, the aim of the thesis is to answer the following question:

   Do Swedish large- and mid-cap stocks with high mutual fund ownership react more
   strongly, in terms of short-term returns, to mutual fund inflows and outflows than low-
   ownership stocks, and does this create potential arbitrage opportunities possible to use for
   retail investors?

   Earlier research by Greenwood and Thesmar (2011) into the subject has found that a
stock’s fragility, defined as the concentration of ownership among funds exposed to
correlated liquidity shocks, can serve as a strong predictor of future volatility and price
instability. Building on this idea, they examine how stock prices become more fragile when
ownership is concentrated among investors who follow similar trading strategies or face
correlated liquidity needs. The authors show that when many shareholders are likely to sell
simultaneously due to funding shocks or similar risk exposure, a stock’s price becomes more
fragile—that is, more sensitive to small shocks. Thus, the paper highlights that a stock’s
vulnerability depends not only on the company’s own risk profile, but also on the
composition and behavior of its investors.
   Furthermore, Aragon and Kim 2023 extend Greenwod and Thesmar’s research by
quantifying the relationship between fund flows and stock returns. Based on this information
they constructed a portfolio based on investigating mutual fund flows and stocks’ risk
towards fire sales, which earns a positive alpha. However, their paper is rather complex for a
retail investor to realize. Therefore, our research has a more practical view of trying to
replicate their evidence on the Swedish equity market, but also make it possible for retail
investors to utilize this inefficiency. This contribution will not only highlight the inefficiency,
but also provide practical information of how investors can act on the information.
   In our empirical analysis, we classify large- and mid-cap stocks listed on the swedish stock
exchange into decile groups based on mutual fund ownership, where a high mutual fund
owenership equals a greater fire-sale risk, by taking inspiration from Aragon and Kim (2023).
The purpose of this classification is to investigate how exposure to fire-sale risk affects stock
prices across different levels of mutual fund ownership.
   Next an ordinary least square regression is conducted, where individual monthly excess
stock returns are regressed on this paper’s two key predictors, the overall market excess
return and the aggregated individual fund flow for each stock. The result indicates a positive
significant relationship on an aggregated level in line with Aragon and Kim (2023), where
fund flows do impact stock returns, which explains how a large inflow (outflow) of capital to
funds can induce a buying (selling) pressure. As a result, the price pressure causes anomalies
in the market which could be utilized.
   Complementary evidence from prior studies provides a strong rationale for our trading
strategy. Ben-Rephael, Kandel, and Wohl (2011) show that roughly half of the price impact
from aggregate fund flows reverses within ten trading days, indicating that such price shocks
are temporary rather than reflecting fundamental value changes. Similarly, Akbas,
Armstrong, Sorescu, and Subrahmanyam (2015) highlight the behavioral dimension, showing
that retail-driven fund flow imbalances can temporarily amplify anomalies before market
liquidity restores equilibrium. Cella, Ellul, and Giannetti (2013) further document that during
periods of market stress, large stock sales generate temporary price distortions, particularly

                                     4
for stocks heavily held by short-term investors. Taken together, this evidence suggests that
temporary price inefficiencies driven by fund flows exist across markets and time periods.
Building on this research, we also found evidence in our results of a temporary price shock in
the Swedish equity market, with prices reversing as liquidity returns. Building on this
literature and supported by our own findings of a positive relationship between fund flows
and stock returns, we propose a trading strategy designed to exploit these short-term
inefficiencies.
   Finally, we construct a trading strategy based on our earlier findings. The strategy focuses
exclusively on outflows, as we found the strongest relationship between outflows and
expected returns. Our results show that the reversal effect is strongest after two months. This
timing is consistent with practical constraints, since fund flow data are published with a delay
and therefore cannot be traded on immediately. Buy decisions are triggered by an outflow
rule, described in more detail in Section 3.4. In brief, the rule says that a long position is
taken when the theoretical fund flow is negative, and the stock belongs to a decile with higher
exposure to fire-sale risk. Positions are held for one month and then liquidated. The portfolio
uses the market portfolio as its base allocation. When qualifying outflow signals are detected,
50% of the portfolio is allocated to the strategy and 50% to the market portfolio. If no
qualifying signals are present, the portfolio is fully invested (100%) in the market portfolio.
The trading strategy underperformed the market in the sample period 2021 and 2022. In
contrast to earlier research by, for instance, Aragon and Kim (2023), who found an alpha in
their trading strategy. Additionally, the trading strategy contradicts our earlier positive
findings.

2. Data and Methodology
2.1. Data
The empirical analysis focuses on firms listed on the Nasdaq Stockholm Stock Exchange’s
large- and mid-cap list. The delimitation to only incorporate these securities has been made as
a trade-off between incorporating a large enough sample to ensure reliable testing and only
incorporate stocks that are liquid and media-covered enough to believe that the average
investor would be able to obtain information about fundamentals. Too small and illiquid
stocks risk jeopardizing the reliability, as the average retail investor might struggle to find
enough information at reasonable cost and large owners trading might disturb short-term
returns on non-fundamental basis.
   The dataset has been developed by the authors through combing information from four
main data sources manually. Stocks listed on the large- and mid-cap lists and their respective
free floats have been obtained from the Holdings database. The number of stocks on each list
has been changing throughout the time-period due to new IPO:s, mergers and delistings.
November, and December 2022 were the months with the largest sample of stocks, including
219 securities.
   Monthly stock returns are obtained from the Swedish House of Finance Finbas database,
covering monthly adjusted prices, including dividends, of all listed stocks in the Nordic. The
dataset includes only common equity and excludes preferred shares to ensure consistency in
return behavior. To control for aggregate market movements in our regressions, monthly
return data of the OMXS30 index are included as a benchmark measure of the Swedish
equity market. Since FinBas does not provide returns for OMXS30, the data have been
collected from LSEG Workspace for the sample period. The OMXS30 is a suitable reference
given its well-established role as the primary performance indicator of the Swedish equity

                                     5
market. All returns are expressed as total returns, incorporating dividends, to reflect the actual
investor realized return.
   The dataset covers all Swedish open-ended mutual equity funds sometimes invested in a
large- or midcap security between January 2013 and December 2022. Historical data on net
flow and assets under management (AUM) for all Swedish-domiciled equity mutual funds
holding the sample firms are retrieved from the Swedish House of Finance Morningstar
database. Fund flows have been aggregated to a monthly level in line with the sample
frequency. Meanwhile Swedish legislation demands funds to report net flows monthly, the
holding of each fund is only required to be reported quarterly. Hence, changes in fund-
exposure are on quarterly basis.

2.3. Conceptual Framework
To test our hypotheses, we have developed a conceptual framework of security pricing based
on fund flow as one of the factors. Our framework is a further development of the Capital
Asset Pricing Model developed by William Sharpe (1964) where the short-term return of a
security is based on market return, the security’s exposure to market risk and stock specific
percentual theoretical fund flow (PTFF), defined as in equation (2), of the current period. The
decision to use the CAPM rather than alternative asset pricing models is motivated by our
focus on the perspective of retail investors. For instance, estimating Fama–French factor
loadings is considerably more difficult for retail investors in the Swedish equity market.

                                     6
Moreover, when constructing the trading strategy later in the paper, we consistently balance
the benefits of increased model sophistication against the need for the strategy to be
replicable by retail investors. Developing an overly complex strategy would likely result in a
model that works in theory but is impractical to implement in reality. Therefore, we will
employ the most intuitive and fundamental asset pricing model—CAPM.
   We use a rolling regression with time varying betas to estimate the beta for market excess
return and percentual theoretical fund flow. The beta for each variable is estimated based on
the past two-year data. Furthermore, the model assumes that fund flows consist of a two-part
risk that can be divided into one systematic component and one idiosyncratic component, or a
fund-specific component, were we test for the systematic component. This is motivated by
the fact that all stocks in our dataset are held by at least 15 funds, and up to 102 funds, and
that the fund-specific idiosyncratic risk, for example a fund changing manager or adjusting its
management fee, over time on average will be zero across all funds, and hence also across all
stocks.

3. Results
In this section, we report the main findings of the relationship between stock returns and fund
flows. The analysis follows a step-by-step empirical structure where each section builds on
prior results, integrating statistical outputs and contextual reasoning throughout.
3.1. Hygiene tests

holds for our sample of stocks and returns, we run a CAPM regression over an equally

whether there is a possibility to observe a relationship disregarding the size differences

average in the Swedish equity market. Nonetheless, the equally weighted portfolio of our
sample should still follow the market excess return quite closely, as all stocks have a market
capitalization above the Swedish mid-cap threshold of 150 million SEK. Table 1 reports the

   Several key takeaways emerge from these results. PTFF is positively associated with
returns when significant. This supports the idea that price pressure from flows is real, at least
at times. However, our proxy for the net flow is only significant for one of the four time
periods. Thus, it appears as if our calculated theoretical flows only drive stock returns on the
aggregated market at times. The heterogeneity in PTFF impact across sub-periods likely
reflects variation in market regimes and investor behavior. For instance, the beta is

                                     8
statistically significant in 2015 – 2016, but insignificant in 2017 – 2018. One interpretation is
that during low-volatility or low-turnover regimes, flows exert less pressure on prices. But in
periods of turbulence, e.g., pandemic-era liquidity shocks, even moderate net flows may
move prices due to reduced market depth.
   These findings confirm that investor behavior and portfolio composition are significant for
asset pricing, at least at times, particularly in concentrated institutional ownership markets.
Our results, which align with previous work showing that institutional flows can move prices
independently of fundamentals (Aragon and Kim, 2023), suggest that open-ended mutual
funds introduce a systematic demand shock in the Swedish context. This supports the view
that fund flows are not idiosyncratic noise, but rather constitute a systematic return driver for
certain stocks and periods, a factor not captured by traditional risk models.
3.2. Test of Hypotheses
Previous research by Greenwood and Thesmar (2011) has found that the composition and
behavior of its investors affects the stock’s vulnerability. They show how the volatility
increases when ownership is concentrated among investors who follow similar trading
strategies or face correlated liquidity needs. Interestingly, Aragon and Kim (2023) shows that
US securities proportionally owned to a larger extent by mutual funds already has priced in
what can be called the risk of a fund-run, in practice being the risk of retail investors
withdrawing large sums of money in a coordinated way, often due to macro-economic
factors, but not due to a fundamental shift in the outlook for a specific fund or security.
   Furthermore, the research by Akbas, Armstrong, Sorescu, and Subrahmanyam (2015)
looked into the data trying to establish a pattern between flow driven price pressure, in either
way, and a trend that can be described as a reversal back to the fundamental price. The study
found that a pattern on the overall population of listed securities was hard to find, but if one
focuses on solely the securities with a significant flow factor, most of the flow driven price
changes are reversed within 90 days of trading.
   Having established a baseline above, showcasing that there is evidence supporting the fact
that fund flows are impacting stock returns on the whole population, albeit to various degrees
over time, we will try to see if we can find a difference between stocks held by open-ended
mutual fund to different degrees, resulting in our first hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1
       Stocks held to a larger extent by open-ended mutual funds will experience a stronger
        reaction to fund-flows, showcased by a larger beta, and stocks held to a lesser extent
        will neither have a particular large beta, nor will they have a significant relationship.

   However, this hypothesis does not answer the question regarding whether it is possible to
find a pattern of reversal. To do that, we need to see whether stocks held by open-ended
mutual funds experiencing large inflows (outflows) becomes overpriced (underpriced) in the
following period(s) and how long, if ever, it takes for the price to adjust. If the price takes a
long enough time to adjust, giving retail investor time to obtain and analyze the data on fund
flows, stocks should in theory experience abnormal returns in future period before the price
adjusts back to the fundamental value, giving rise to our second hypothesis.

Hypothesis 2:
       The price reversals for funds with a significant relationship between fund flow and
       stock returns take more than one month to reverse back to the fundamental value,
       giving retail investors enough time to obtain and analyze data. In practice, the fund
       flow beta on lagged fund flows has a negative beta in the coming period(s).

   Table 3 shows how the sensitivity of stock returns to fund flows varies across portfolios
formed by mutual fund ownership. The CAPM betas are generally close to one for the lower
ownership deciles but decline for the highest deciles, indicating that stocks with large mutual
fund ownership behave less like the broad market and more like flow-driven assets.
Furthermore, the PTFF beta, or the impact, of fund flows on stock returns increases as mutual
fund exposure increases together with the significance level increasing. Hence, we can see
that only for stocks with a relatively large mutual fund exposure, fund flows have a
significant impact on stock returns. This contrast between low and high ownership deciles
provides support for Hypothesis 1. It confirms that fire-sale risk is not a systematic market-
wide phenomenon, but rather relates to what degree of the ownership is concentrated, which
aligns with previous research of Greenwood and Thesmar’s (2011). Thus, the stock’s fragility
becomes enhanced due to the concentrated ownership structure, which means that the stock
price becomes more volatile to fund flows. Further on, we will thus only work with the
deciles 7-10 as it is this group that appears to be of relevance when using fund flows to
predict short-term returns.

   It is visible in table 4 that the strongest reversal effect seems to happen after 2 months
have passed. Thus, it appears as if it takes approximately 2 months after a large flow-driven
price change for prices to adjust back to the fundamental value of the security. The result
should, however, be interpreted carefully. The lag effect is not calculated based on days and
rather accumulated into months, which means that the reversal effect that is seen in table 4
should not be interpreted as an exact figure, rather an indication that there seems to be a
reversal effect of the stock price around 2 months after a large flow-driven price change. In
comparison, Rephael, Kandel, and Wohl (2011) have found that the reversal effect was
shown already after 10 trading days. This research somewhat contradicts our results, since the
result we have obtained is a significantly longer time period. But the main takeaway from our
result and Rephael, Kandel, and Wohl (2011) is the fact that there is a reversal effect over
several days. The time period could, though, vary.
   The explanation of why there is a lagged effect as we interpret it is due to the fire sale
effect. Thus, the market need time to adjust for the new stock price. Cella, Ellul, and
Giannetti (2013) bring in the other perspective of how the short-term investors act during
market stress where large stock sales generate temporary price distortions. Their findings
show that short-term investors in fact amplify the effects of the market movement, since they

                                     12
follow the same path as the fundholders who tend to have a longer investment horizon in this
case. As a result, the market does not adjust instantly to the price change but rather needs
time to readjust to the liquidity shock of the stock and wait until it has been stabilized.
   To summarize, the results from table 4 support our second hypothesis that there is a
significant relationship between fund flow and stock returns, and it takes more than one
month to reverse back to the fundamental value, giving retail investors enough time to obtain
and analyze data. However, the exact figure of 2 months according to our data shall be
carefully used and should instead be interpreted as within a timeframe of around 2 months
after a large flow-driven price change there will be some sort of reversal effect.
   However, an interesting perspective to consider, which we have left out up until this point,
is the effect of different flows. Although not stated, we have until this point assumed flows to
be symmetrical in pressure on stock prices. In essence, that large inflows pressures prices in
the same way as large outflows. This is necessarily not a clear-cut assumption to make. If a
fund manager faces a redemption, their actions are limited. Either they can meet the
obligation by using assets held as pure cash within the fund or they liquidate assets. If a fund
manager faces a large inflow, the possible way of using this cash are many more. As with a
redemption, the fund manager can increase the pool of liquid cash within the fund or increase
the position of one or several assets held within the fund. However, the manager can also
choose to invest in new assets, spreading the large inflow over a larger share of securities and
thus reducing, or almost eliminating, the pressure put on any individual security. Hence, a
reasonable thought is that large outflows are going to have a more profound impact on short
term returns. To test this, we will run the same regressions as earlier, and as introduced into
our conceptual framework, but have one separate regression for inflows and one separate
regression for outflows.

3.4. Trading Strategy
The first step of developing a trading strategy is to decide what flows to act on. Based on our
knowledge of which fund ownership deciles that are significant overall, deciles seven through
ten, we now also want to establish which flows that actually are driving this level of
significance. The winsorizing test below in figure 1 yields an unexpected result, the top and
bottom flows are not significant. Rather, these decrease the overall significance of our model.
Although surprising, our flows are purely theoretical and a possible explanation could be that

                                     16
the largest positive flows and smallest negative flows actually are not hitting the stocks
prices, but rather are handled in an alternative way, in practice implying that out theoretical
flows are not equaling real flows in the stocks.

Figure 1: Windsorzing test of fund flow regression.

   From figure 1, it is possible to see that a regression with 10% and 20% trimmed flows
have the highest beta and are most significant and as more data is trimmed away, the beta
estimate becomes insignificant once again. If we divide our monthly fund flows in to deciles,
fund flow deciles, not to mix with the deciles used to classify fund ownership, fund
ownership deciles, we see that the most significant flows are the flows that are in flow deciles
2, 3, 8 and 9, which are the flows we will act on in our trading strategy. As we previously
have established that we only can find a significant lag-effect for outflows, the trading
strategy will solely focus on outflows, which likely only will be present in the lower deciles
during normal months.
   Furthermore, when developing a trading strategy, we will make use of our previous
findings in table 7 showcasing that the largest reversal effect for the significant decile groups
is obtained after two months for outflows, and that no significant reversal effect for inflows
can be found. In practice, information about fund flows is not possible to obtain in real time,
but rather with a lag of between ten and fifteen days depending on the type of funds and if
special grants have been given. Hence, it is not possible to trade on information about fund
flows directly, but rather with a delay of at least ten days, which in our dataset with monthly
observations is a delay of one month. As our regressions above suggest that most of the
reversal effects have been obtained after two months, our trading strategy will thus
incorporate a holding time of one month before trades made on outflows signals are
liquidated. Our trading strategy is the following.
   Each month, stocks are assigned to ownership deciles based on what share of the free float
are held by mutual funds. Only firms in deciles seven through ten are included in the strategy
universe as these are the deciles, we have deemed significant. Monthly theoretical fund flows
are measured using the variable percentual theoretical fund flow, PTFF, and sorted into flow

                                     17
deciles based on all previous flows analyzed in the data. An outflow signal, that is an outflow
to act on, is generated when:

the distribution of fund flows, outflows will likely only be found in deciles 2 and 3. Due to
the information delay, the strategy enters a long position at t = 1 if the abovementioned
criteria is fulfilled, holds the stock for one month, which equals two months past the detected
flow, and liquidates the position.

information can thus be said to be too costly to produce for the average retail investor to earn
enough alpha to justify the time invested. However, there is an additional opportunity,
making the information much easier to obtain, namely the aggregated market fund flows on
the Swedish fund market. These are collected and reported by Fondbolagens Förening, and
published on their website with a three-week delay. If possible, a retail investor could in that
case download all the necessary data in just a couple of minutes.

   As could be suspected, an even more narrow version of the general proxy is not enough to
generate significant results. Although decile 8 is significant, the overall portfolio and rest of
the deciles are not. Even though this relationship has been observed on the US equity market,
it is not visible for the period analysed here on the Swedish equity market. A likely

                                     21
explanation is the size of the US equity market. The funds on the US equity market are to a
vast majority invested in US securities, implying that a 1% fund flow in or out of a US fund
often results in a 1% fund flow in or out of the US equity market. On the Swedish equity
market, however, many funds are international ones, investing a substantial share in foreign
markets, and especially the US equity market. As the aggregated flow in or out of funds listed
on the Swedish stock exchange thus flows to a substantial part to foreign equity markets, only
a small share actually impacts the Swedish exchange. Hence, it appears as if the aggregated
flows on the Swedish market thus is too general of a proxy to be used when trying to seek
alpha in trading.

4. Conclusion
We extend the traditional Capital Asset Pricing Model with a fund flow factor to explain the
impact of deposits and withdrawals on mutual funds. By extending the traditional CAPM
with a fund-flow factor, we provide evidence that non-fundamental drivers play a meaningful
role in explaining stocks’ returns in the Swedish equity market.
   Our results show that fund flows have a positive impact on stock returns. Stocks that
experience higher inflows exhibit higher short-term returns, while outflows correspond to
lower returns, even after controlling overall market movements. However, the relationship is
found to be stronger for outflows than inflows. Furthermore, this relationship becomes
substantially stronger for stocks with high mutual fund ownership, supporting the idea that
these securities are more exposed to fire-sale risk and thus more sensitive to liquidity shocks.
   Beyond demonstrating this relationship, our results reveal a reversal pattern. According to
our findings, the strongest reversal occurs approximately two months after the initial flow
shock, suggesting that flow-induced price distortions are temporary and gradually reverse as
liquidity returns to the market. This provides direct evidence of non-fundamental pricing
dynamics in the Swedish equity market and challenges the notion of fully efficient price
formation.
   Finally, the result of the trading strategy is mixed. The strategy underperforms in relation
to the cumulative market return during the same period, which goes against our previous
findings of how mutual fund flows affect the stocks’ prices. Thus, we encourage future
research to test the effect on a greater time period and adjusting the strategy, since our
strategy underperforms, while our earlier findings show a relationship between fund flows
and stocks’ expected returns.
  Overall, our findings highlight that investor flows—rather than fundamentals alone—play
a role in explaining short-term stock price movements. In a market where retail investors
increasingly allocate savings through mutual funds, these flow-induced pressures can become
systematic and meaningful.

Abstract:

This paper investigates the weather effect on the Swedish stock market. It focuses on
how weather conditions impact stock market returns, volatilities and trading volumes
over the period from 1984 to 2024. The analyzed weather variables include temperature,
precipitation, relative humidity, barometric pressure, wind speed, cloud coverage and
sunshine time, and are all collected from the Swedish Meteorological and Hydrological
Institute. Some evidence is found for Stockholm weather impacting stock market
returns and volatilities. However, these effects generally seem to have diminished over
time, perhaps as a result of markets becoming more efficient and globalized. Stronger
evidence is found for weather impacting trading volumes. This study contributes to the
literature by exploring the development of the weather effect over time in a previously
relatively unexplored market.

Ever since the publication of the article “Stock Prices and Wall Street Weather” (Saunders (1993)),
a large body of research has explored the impact of local weather on stock markets. Support has
been found for the idea that weather can affect investor mood and that this can have an aggregate
impact on the behavior of stock markets. We call this phenomenon the weather effect. A lot has,
however, changed since this first paper was published. On the one hand, markets have become
much more digitalized and globalized, with market behavior today being driven to a larger extent
by algorithms and a wide variety of investors from all over the world. This raises questions about
whether local weather can still have the same influence on markets as it has had before. On the
other hand, any weather effect which still exists is likely to be amplified by the increasing amount
of extreme weather the world has experienced over the past decades. Unfortunately, as a result
of climate change, the trend of more frequent and intense extreme weather does not seem to
be reversing anytime soon, and this makes the study of weather’s impact on financial markets
increasingly relevant.
    This study aims not only to investigate whether the weather effect still exists, but also to draw
conclusions regarding its development over time. To do this, over 40 years of stock market data
from Sweden is used together with weather data from Stockholm. By focusing on a longer period
of time than most earlier research, this study contributes to the literature by analyzing potential
changes in the weather effect over time. This study also focuses on several different weather
variables and market variables. The analyzed weather factors include temperature, precipitation,
relative humidity, barometric pressure, wind speed, cloud coverage and sunshine time, while the
analyzed market variables include returns, volatilities and trading volumes. The Swedish weather
effect is a topic which previously has remained relatively unexplored, even though there are several
reasons why Sweden is a suitable setting for this type of research. One major reason is that
the influence of domestic investors on the stock market is comparatively large. As of December
2024, such investors represent around 63% of the ownership in companies listed on the Swedish
marketplace (Statistics Sweden (2024a)). Another reason is that Sweden is a small country and that
weather is relatively constant across regions. This means that Stockholm weather is a relatively
good proxy also for the weather in other major Swedish cities. Finally, the weather in Sweden can
vary a lot, which is perfect when conducting a study of this type.
   Formulating hypotheses regarding the weather effect is no easy task. On the one hand,
through the lens of the Efficient Market Hypothesis (Fama (1970)), weather-induced investor
mood should not influence stock prices. If this were the case, arbitrage opportunities would
arise and sophisticated market participants would exploit them. This suggests that returns and
volatilities should be unaffected by weather and that any indications otherwise could constitute an
inefficiency in the market. On the other hand, it is well-established in psychological research that
weather can influence mood and through this possibly also financial decision-making. Howarth
and Hoffman (1984) studied how weather affects mood and found it to be a significant predictor
of mood changes in a majority of the mood dimensions studied. They found that humidity,
temperature, and hours of sunshine had the greatest impact on mood. High levels of humidity were
associated with lower levels of concentration and increased sleepiness. Lower barometric pressure

was also linked to lower levels of concentration. Higher temperature related to lowered levels of
anxiety and skepticism, while performance was found to decline under uncomfortably hot or cold
conditions. The number of sunshine hours was found to predict optimism scores significantly with
a positive relation. Bassi, Colacito, and Fulghieri (2013) provide experimental evidence suggesting
that sunshine and good weather promote risk-taking behavior while bad weather promotes risk-
aversion.
    Overall, psychological research has led us to the hypothesis that good weather, indicated by
for example more sunshine, will be positively related to returns. The reason is that investors will
accept higher prices of equities when they are optimistic and less anxious. We expect this to be the
case in the beginning of the studied time period, but not in the end, as a result of markets becoming
more efficient. We also hypothesize that bad weather will be related to higher volatility. The reason
is that anxious investors will vary more in what they consider a correct price for an asset and that
this will lead to higher price swings. Once again, we expect this to be the case in the beginning
of the studied time period, but not in the end. Our final hypothesis is that uncomfortable weather
will be positively related to trading volumes. Our reasoning is that investors will be more likely to
stay inside and trade when it for example is rainy and windy. Here we do not expect the effect to
change as much over time, since a relationship between weather and trading volumes could exist
even in a perfectly efficient market.
   To answer our research questions, Stockholm weather data from SMHI (Swedish
Meteorological and Hydrological Institute (2024)) is used together with market data from the
Stockholm Stock Exchange (primarily on the OMXS30 index). However, it is not immediately
obvious exactly what weather data is most suitable to use for the study. Ideally, we would want
all market participants to live closely together in a very small area and then use weather data
from there, but this is not possible in practice. Instead, we choose to primarily use data from the
Observatoriekullen weather station located in the very center of Stockholm, which hopefully gives
a good measure of Stockholm weather and a relatively good measure of the overall weather in
Sweden.
    Several different types of regressions are run to explore potential relationships between weather
variables and market behavior. These are run with all available data in the analyzed time period
between 1984 and 2024, but also separately for four different sub-periods. Weather is assumed
to be relatively random and unrelated to other factors influencing market behavior, but it is very
dependent on time of year, which stock market behavior can be as well. Since these are not the
effects we are interested in capturing, we control for what month it is in all of our regressions.
    The results of this study reveal that weather factors generally are not related to stock market
returns. There are, however, some possible exceptions. The main one is that low temperatures are
related to high stock market returns at a 10% significance level when regressed on all available
data. This effect is consistent over time in the first three analyzed sub-periods, but seems to have
disappeared in the fourth. The observed effect is explained almost exclusively by low temperatures
being related to high returns, meaning it does not seem to matter whether temperatures are medium
or high. There are also some indications of high cloud coverage being related to low returns. This

is the case in the first two analyzed sub-periods (although only in one of them at a 5% significance
level), but not in the last two. The opposite effect is observed for sunshine time, which is not
surprising since cloud coverage and sunshine time to a large extent are inversely correlated. Finally,
there are some indications of low wind speeds being related to low returns, but these results are not
entirely consistent over time.
    When it comes to volatilities, the study mostly finds no clear patterns, despite there being many
significant regression results. Worth mentioning here is that, due to limited availability of intraday
price data, the study’s volatility measure is based entirely on each day’s high and low price for the
OMXS30. The most clear pattern for volatilities is that high temperatures seem to be related to low
volatilities. This is the case for all four analyzed sub-periods. In addition to this, a potential non-
linear relationship is observed suggesting that both high and low barometric pressures are related
to high volatilities. This pattern is, however, not as consistent over time. Finally, there are some
indications of high cloud coverage being related to high volatilities in the first two analyzed time
periods, but not in the last two.
    Trading volume is the market variable for which most of the clear patterns are found. Firstly,
high wind speeds are related to high trading volumes at very significant levels. These results
are consistent across all the analyzed sub-periods. Secondly, high barometric pressures seem to
be related to low trading volumes. These results are also consistent across all the sub-periods
investigated. Finally, there are indications of low temperatures being related to low trading
volumes, but these results are not as consistent over time.
    Overall, this study presents some evidence of certain weather factors being related to stock
market returns and volatilities, but the evidence is not strong enough to establish any of the
observed relationships with complete certainty. The observed effects come mainly from the first
half of the investigated time period, indicating that weather’s impact on stock market returns
and volatilities has decreased over time as a result of markets becoming more globalized and
digitalized. The study finds clearer relationships between weather and trading volumes, which
is not surprising since such relationships can survive even in perfectly efficient markets. A very
clear final conclusion from the study is that changes in weather from one day to the next do not
seem to have an impact on market behavior at all.
    The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section I delves deeper into previous
studies on how weather can impact stock market behavior and expands on the contribution we
make to this literature. Section II provides a detailed overview of the collected data, including
sources and characteristics. Section III outlines the methodology employed to analyze the data,
including regression models and statistical techniques. Section IV presents and interprets the
findings from the analysis. Section V discusses the robustness checks performed to ensure the
validity and reliability of the results. Section VI concludes.

                                      I. Related Literature

One of the earliest works exploring the relationship between stock market performance, weather,
and investor mood is Saunders (1993). The study examines the effects of cloud cover in New York
City on stock returns on the New York exchanges, by regressing daily NYSE and AMEX index
returns against cloud coverage data in New York City from 1962 to 1989. Cloud cover is grouped
into three categories: clear (0-20% cloud cover), partly cloudy (20-80% cloud cover), and cloudy
(80-100% cloud cover). Saunders found a significant relationship, where low cloud cover was
associated with higher returns and high cloud cover was related to lower returns.
    Goetzmann, D. Kim, et al. (2015) provide evidence that weather can induce behavioral biases,
which in turn can have an aggregate impact on the prices of stocks and indices. Their findings
suggest that weather-based mood measures influence investors’ perceptions of market mispricing.
Investor optimism is shown to be associated with lower values of depersonalized cloud cover which
is negatively associated with perceived overpricing of assets. The study used disaggregated trade
data to provide evidence that investor optimism increases propensities to buy. The study also
found that their stock-level investor mood measure explains a significant amount of variation in
daily returns of stocks associated with higher arbitrage costs. They document return co-movement
attributable to investor mood.
   Loughran and Schultz (2004) investigate NASDAQ-listed firms and examine whether local
weather conditions in cities where companies are headquartered affect stock prices. They found
that trading volumes dropped for firms headquartered in cities where blizzards struck, suggesting
that trading is localized and affected by factors such as the ability to drive to work. However, they
found no significant evidence that cloud cover where a firm is headquartered is related to its stock
returns. They did, however, find support that cloud cover in NYC was weakly negatively related to
stock returns on the NYSE, supporting the findings of Saunders (1993). Despite strong evidence
of localized trading, the study concludes that local weather conditions do not provide profitable
trading opportunities.
   Kliger and Levy (2003) used cloudy weather as a proxy for bad mood and clear skies as a
proxy for good mood and found a negative correlation between risk-aversion and mood. Their
results indicate that high cloud cover is associated with higher risk-aversion in financial decisions.
    The weather effects found in earlier studies are visualized in Table 1, 2 and 3. A majority of
the studies use Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regressions where the weather variable is used as
the independent variable and the financial market variable as the dependent variable, which is the
method that will be used in this study as well.
    The results from earlier works exploring the weather effect on returns are visualized in Table 1.
Following the first study by Saunders (1993), the weather effect has been explored in numerous
subsequent studies. Evidence of a cloud cover weather effect on the NYSE has been found by
Goetzmann, D. Kim, et al. (2015), Loughran and Schultz (2004), Goetzmann and Zhu (2005),
and S. Chang et al. (2008). Other stock exchanges and weather variables have also been examined.
Significant effects of weather on returns have been documented in Germany by Muhlack and Soost

developed over time and if it still remains present during the past decade. Additionally, it extends
the research to the Stockholm Stock Exchange, a market that has not yet been thoroughly explored
in this context. Furthermore, we investigate if weather-related effects are observable on not only
returns, but also on volatilities, and trading volumes, across multiple weather variables, which is
yet to be done in Sweden.

                                     II. Data Description

The ideal weather dataset for this study would contain precise geographic locations for all market
participants, paired with local weather conditions at the same locations. However, such precise data
is not available and we instead primarily use weather data from the Observatoriekullen weather
station in Stockholm. Stockholm is the most populated city in Sweden with 1 652 895 residents
in 2023, followed by Gothenburg with 674 529 residents and Malm¨o with 339 316 residents
(Statistics Sweden (2024b)). Given its size and economic centrality, Stockholm likely represents
the geographic base of a large share of domestic investors, making its weather a reasonable proxy
for the prevailing conditions experienced by many market participants.
    The ideal stock market data for this study would contain daily returns, volatilities and trading
volumes for the Stockholm Stock Exchange. We choose to use OMXS30 historical index data for
returns and volatility measures as it represents the 30 largest and most actively traded stocks on the
Stockholm Stock Exchange, covering a significant portion of the trading activity (Nasdaq OMX
(2024)). The available data on this index also stretches relatively far back in time (around four
decades). Due to limited availability of trading volume data at the index level, we collect such data

for all available instruments on the Stockholm Stock Exchange separately and aggregate them into
a single daily measure.

A. Weather Data
The weather data is collected through the API of SMHI (Swedish Meteorological and Hydrological
Institute (2024)). The weather factors collected include temperature (in °C), precipitation (in
mm per day), relative humidity (in %), barometric pressure (in hPa), wind speed (in m/s), cloud
coverage (in %) and sunshine time (in seconds/hour). Many of these weather factors are correlated,
which can be seen in Figure 1, with for example cloud coverage being inversely correlated to
sunshine time. However, since we analyze each weather factor separately, this is not a problem as
long as we are aware of the correlations when interpreting results. The reason for analyzing one
weather factor at a time is to facilitate comparisons with previous literature.

historical data. However, some data is collected from other stations as well. Observatoriekullen A

B. Stock Market Data
Price data on the OMXS30 is collected primarily from FinBas at the Swedish House of Finance
Research Data Center (House of Finance, Stockholm School of Economics (2024a)). The collected
data includes each day’s closing price, its highest price and its lowest price. The FinBas data ends
on 2019-01-30 and therefore the Yahoo Finance API (Yahoo Finance (2024)) is used for collecting
data from that point up until 2024-11-30.
    The collected index data is used to calculate daily returns and daily measures of volatility. The
return on day t is calculated as (Pt − Pt−1)/Pt−1, where Pt is the closing price of the index on
day t. The volatility measure is calculated as the fraction by which the highest price for the day
is higher than the lowest price for the day, according to (Pt,high − Pt,low)/Pt,low. This is of course
not an ideal measure of volatility, but due to limited availability of intraday price data, this is the
approach used.
   Data on trading volumes is, just like the price data for OMXS30, taken from FinBas at the
Swedish House of Finance Research Data Center. However, since no trading volume data is
available at the index level, daily trading volumes in SEK are instead collected for all individual
available instruments on the Stockholm Stock Exchange and summed together into a total daily
value (House of Finance, Stockholm School of Economics (2024b)). The result is a time series
starting on 1994-01-01 and ending on 2023-06-30.
    Table 5 gives descriptions of the market variables used in the study. It also shows the period of
time for each time series. During these time periods, there is data for essentially all days, excluding
weekends and other days when the stock market is closed.

                               III. Empirical Implementation

Various linear regressions are run to analyze the data. Each regression focuses on one weather
factor (temperature, precipitation, relative humidity, barometric pressure, wind speed, cloud
coverage or sunshine time) and one market variable (return, volatility measure or trading volume).
Monthly dummy variables are included in all regressions to control for time of year since seasonal
effects are not what is of interest. The first regression is of the type:

         Market Variable = α + β0 · Weather Variable + β1 · D1 + β2 · D2 + · · · + ε (1)
This regression, and in particular the coefficient β0, gives insights into the relationship between the
weather factor and the market variable. D1, D2, ..., D11 are the monthly dummy variables (only 11
are needed since the regression already includes an intercept α). Both the β0 and the corresponding
p-value are collected and presented in the results. The second regression is of the type:

        Market Variable = α + β0 · ∆Weather Variable + β1 · D1 + β2 · D2 + · · · + ε (2)
The only difference with the first regression is that the change in the weather variable from the
day before is used instead of the actual weather variable. The reason for this is to analyze whether
changes in weather from one day to the next are related to market behavior.
    Both the above regressions assume a linear relationship between the weather factor (or the
change in the weather factor) and the market variable. To investigate the nature of the actual
relationship, the data for each weather factor is divided into three different groups. The first group
consists of the first quartile, the second group consists of the middle two quartiles and the last
group consists of the top quartile. Dummy variables denoting the top and bottom group are created
and a regression of the following type is run:

         Market Variable = α + β0 · Bottom + β1 · Top + β2 · D1 + β3 · D2 + · · · + ε (3)

                                      12
                                 Exploring the Weather Effect

This regression, and in particular the β0 and β1 coefficients, gives insights into potential non-linear
relationships between the variables.
    All of the regressions mentioned so far are run with all available data for the entire analyzed
time period. To investigate the development of the relationships over time, regressions 1 and 3 are
repeated with different sub-periods of the data. The sub-periods used are 1984 to 1993, 1994 to
2003, 2004 to 2013 and 2014 to 2024.
    Finally, it is worth mentioning that all variables in regression 1 and 2 are standardized to
facilitate comparisons of effect sizes between regressions. In regression 3, only the market variable
is standardized while the group variables are kept binary. All the above are run as OLS regressions.

                                     V. Robustness

Several robustness checks are conducted to assess the reliability of the regression results. Firstly,
we carry out tests regarding how well the OLS assumptions are met. Secondly, we re-run all
the regressions using different regression models, which weaken some of the assumptions behind
OLS, and compare them with the already existing results. Finally, we conduct placebo tests to gain
insights into the likelihood of noise impacting our results.
    Durbin-Watson tests, Jarque-Bera tests and residual plots are used to assess the extent to which
the OLS assumptions are satisfied. The results vary between the different regressions, but the
Durbin-Watson statistic is in general somewhere between one and two and sometimes even just
under one. This indicates a general trend of positive autocorrelations and raises some questions
regarding the validity of the OLS results. The Jarque-Bera tests and residual plots also make clear
that the residuals do not entirely follow a normal distribution. Because of all these observations,
other regression models are experimented with as well, which weaken some of the assumptions
behind OLS. All these regressions yield results consistent with the main conclusions of the study,
reinforcing their robustness.

    Placebo tests are also conducted to gain insights into whether the observed effects can be
attributed to chance rather than a true relationship. The placebo tests are done by randomizing
the permutations of all the weather observations and re-running the regressions. This is done 2000
times and the resulting beta distribution is plotted and compared to the actual beta values. Figure 2
and Figure 3 show the results for the regression of temperatures on returns and the regression of
wind speeds on trading volumes. The obtained betas in both these cases fall in the very outer part
of the distribution tails. This strengthens the robustness of these findings.

                                   VI. Conclusions

The aim of this study is to investigate whether there is a weather effect on the Swedish stock
market, and if so, how it has developed over time. The results show some evidence of certain
weather factors being related to stock market returns and volatilities, but not at particularly high
levels of statistical significance. Two of the observed relationships are low temperatures being
related to high returns and cloudiness being related to low returns. These relationships, however,
seem to have diminished over time. Likely reasons for this could be financial markets becoming
more globalized, meaning investors no longer are influenced by local weather to the same extent,
and also more efficient. The evidence for relationships between weather and trading volumes is
stronger than for returns and volatilities, and these results also seem to be more consistent over
time.
    There are some limitations with this study worth noting. Firstly, a large number of regressions
are included, meaning there is a high risk of detecting some statistically significant results even
for relationships which do not actually exist. Because of this, the study focuses on running
different types of regressions and assessing the consistency of the observed relationships over time.
However, further exploring the nature of the relationships could provide additional evidence for

their existence. Secondly, section V gives indications that using more advanced regression models
could be justifiable in some cases. This study experiments with different options, but ideally,
separate assessments should be done for every regression regarding what model to use. Using
more advanced models, both linear and non-linear, is a suggestion for future studies. Finally, it is
important to mention that investor mood is not the only mechanism by which weather could impact
stock market behavior, since certain types of weather potentially could have a direct economic
impact as well.

The  Fast Lane:  Event study of stock price adjustments in conjunction with FDA
accelerated  approval announcements

Abstract:

   Using  traditional event study methods, the abnormal returns of publicly traded small
   pharmaceutical and biotechnology stocks in conjunction with a drug receiving the special
   FDA accelerated approval designation is analyzed. 37  events between  2001  and 2021 are
   included and the announcement is found to lead to significant abnormal returns of
   approximately +11% on the event day and +13% over the analyzed 9-day event window.
   Therefore, the conclusion of this study is that an accelerated approval by the FDA is an
  unexpected and highly valuable event for a small pharmaceutical or biotechnology firm
   significantly effecting its value.

1.  Introduction

This  study  shows  that  accelerated  approvals granted by the  Food and Drug Administration
(here forth  referred to simply as “the FDA”)  to  pharmaceutical and biotechnology firms  are
followed by  positive stock returns.  The  pharmaceutical and biotechnology industry is one of
the largest in the world.  In the United States, it stands for 3.2% of gross domestic product
(Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America, 2017).  The industry carries heavy
research and development expenditures to develop new  drug compounds  that  after  approval
can  enter the market and  target specific diseases  (Bilir, 2020).  The US pharmaceutical and
biotechnology industry is regulated by the  FDA  which is the federal organ handling all drug
applications, approvals and pharmaceutical studies. Receiving FDA approval is crucial for a
firm to be able to reach out and market its drugs to  patients and doctors across the  nation and
thus drive revenue to cover  up the R&D costs and finance future drug development. There are
multiple types of FDA approval, both traditional and accelerated.  However, the effects of
different types of FDA approvals on firm value are not fully understood. This study contributes
to the understanding of the market value of  an  FDA  drug approval.

Using traditional event-study methodology, this  study analyzes 37 FDA accelerated approvals
between 2001 and 2021. Accelerated approval is a special type of approval  granted  to drugs
after they have completed only phase-2 trials. This has been granted to  192 drugs since its
inception in 1992. This can be compared to  traditional  approval for novel drugs which during
the past 5 years  (2017-2021)  has been granted to approximately 50  novel  drugs per year
(Mullard, 2022).  Accelerated approval  is  not  an approval that the firm  applies for but rather
granted  by the FDA is they deem  it important. Due to this, an accelerated approval is a
surprising event for the market.  The accelerated approvals  analyzed in  this study stem from
small pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies.

The effects of public regulatory announcements such as FDA  announcements have previously
been studied by Cohen (2019) which found that announcements originating from the FDA led
to  significantly larger  abnormal returns than announcements originating from the company
itself. However, the magnitude of this effect has not previously been defines. Using an ordinary
least squares (OLS) regression  where stock returns  are  benchmarked against the Russell  2000
index, this  study finds that for  accelerated approvals  granted between 2001 and 2021  to small
pharmaceutical and biotechnology firms with ten or fewer previously approved drugs, the
average abnormal return on the  event day is  an  11%  increase in the stock price  on the event
date.  Over a longer event window  of 9 days,  including  4 days  before and  after approval  has
been granted, the cumulative  average  abnormal return is  a  13%  increase in stock price.  Both
these results are significant at the 1 percent level  and show the value of an FDA accelerated
approval.

1.2 Motivation and aim
In this paper, a quantitative analysis in the form of an event  study is  conducted to research how
investors in  small firms in  the American pharmaceutical and biotechnology industry react to a
novel drug being granted accelerated approval by the FDA. As a consequence of being granted
accelerated approval by the FDA, the drug can be marketed  to the public years before full
approval was anticipated.

Research Question: How does the FDA accelerated approval drug designation affect stock
                returns  of  small pharmaceutical and biotechnology stocks?

2.  Literature and Background
2.1  Literature review
2.1.1  The stock market  and reactions to company events  and innovation
The general effects of company events have  been widely studied during the past fifty years of
financial research. Fama et al. (1969) conducted the first ever event study where they examined
how  stock  prices react to adjust to new information being available on the market. This led to
their formulation of the efficient market hypothesis in 1970 which states that  publicly traded
stocks reflect all relevant publicly available information as soon as it is available to the market.
The  events  studied  in  an  event  study  can  be  of  different  characters.  Firm-specific
announcements can be things such as earnings announcements or new product launches. Sector
specific announcements can be newly imposed regulations or governmental subsidies e.g.
during the coronavirus pandemic  (Goldstein, 2021). Market-wide events  can be fiscal, such as
changes in the federal interest rate, or political, such as public elections.  Generally, firm
specific events occur on different days for different firms even if they are of the same character
(e.g. earnings announcements)  whilst sector and or market events occur on the same day for all
companies  (e.g. elections,  natural disasters).

Market reaction to events differ between type of events and  a number of other external factors.
According to research from DellaVigna (2009), the timing of the market response differs
between days of the week. They explain this as being due to investor inattention being larger
during Fridays than during the rest of the weekdays. The reaction to announcements on publicly
traded companies also depends on company size according to research by Chopra (1992). One

                                     4
reason for this is that smaller companies often have less diversified operations both in regards
to product mix and geographical reach. Therefore they inherently carry more risk compared to
larger companies that often operate through multiple subsidiaries both geographically and in
regards to product offering. Chopra (1992) also discusses the difference in ownership as a
possible explanation for  these overreactions. Smaller firms are to a larger extent owned by
individuals whilst larger companies are predominantly held by institutions. Institutions often
hold stocks over longer time periods than individuals which  makes them less sensitive to new
announcements.

The effects of  company innovation, measured by  research and development (R&D)  spending,
and its effect on stock market returns was studied by Chan et al. (2001). They found that within
a  set of growth stocks, R&D-intensive stocks tend to out-perform stocks with little or no R&D.
Pharmaceutical and biotechnology stocks that do not yet have any product on the market are
examples of R&D heavy growth stocks and the Chan et al. (2001) findings that the market
undervalues these stocks have implications for our study.  Innovation  in firms  was studied by
Bilir et al. (2020). They found that when U.S. firms invested in domestic R&D expenditure,
approximately 80% of the returns associated with the investment were later  realized within the
U.S. and the remaining 20% were realized abroad.  This shows the importance of acquiring
domestic approval for a product.  They also illustrated how the pharmaceutical  sector is  the
most R&D heavy in the world. For the U.S. market, the FDA control the access of novel drugs
and treatments to the U.S. market.  Consequently, this approval opens up the gates  for the firm
to  its largest  revenue stream and thus  has a major effect on total  company value.

2.1.2  Stock market reactions to the FDA
Hassan et al. (2019) studied the relationship between political risk, including certain FDA
announcements, and stock market volatility on both a sector and company level.  They found
that  most of the variation in measured political risk appears to play out at the level of the firm,
thus emphasizing the importance of the individual firm’s characteristics and not just the market
or sector as a whole when it comes to reaction to news events. The financial value of
pharmaceutical company innovation was studied by  Acemoglu et al. (2004) that related the
incidence of new drugs being marketed to changes in demographics. They found that a 1
percent increase in potential market size resulted in a 4 to 6 percent increase in the number of
new drugs within that category.  This shows how traditional drug development is skewed
toward developing drugs where the number of patients  is the largest.

Cohen (2019) studied the case of a change in  FDA regulations regarding infusion pumps. The
difference between stock returns when information was communicated from the company itself
was compared to official FDA announcements and showed that news relating to official
announcements by the FDA caused significantly larger abnormal returns than announcements
originating from the firm itself. Cohen (2019) illustrates the importance and strength of the
FDA when it comes to affecting company returns since they are the regulating party. Since the
medical industry is very technical and many investors lack the ability to assess medical
research,  there is often high uncertainty regarding the possible approval of a product. Cohen
also discusses the fact that investors today have access to an abundance of information which
in theory should make investing easier but sometimes has the opposite effect and makes it more
difficult because all information has to be assessed. This abundance of information further
emphasizes the importance of the FDA since an FDA approval is  clear, unbiased and
straightforward in its communication.

2.2  Pharmaceutical and biotechnology company events
As discussed above, pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies are heavily reliant on
innovation, R&D and regulatory approval to be able to market their products to the public and
consequently make profits. The process from first  idea to marketed drug often takes well over
ten years (Lakdawalla, 2018) and the goal with novel drug development is to gain approval and
patents for the novel drug which will then lead to cash flows over the coming years that cover
the previous R&D costs  and provide substantial positive returns during the coming years to
pay off the risk associated with the drug  development and  approval process.

2.2.1  The FDA Drug Approval Process
The historical context for drug regulation
The pharmaceutical and biotechnology industry  is one of the heaviest regulated industries in
the world. This is due to the potential life-threatening effects if dangerous drugs are approved.
After the Thalidomide scandal during the 1960’s, when West German  pharmaceutical
developer Grünenthal marketed their new drug Thalidomide toward pregnant women and more
than 10,000 babies were born with phocomelia (malformed extremities), regulation quickly
escalated and today all new drugs needs to undergo diligent controls before being marketed to
the public  (Kingsland, 2020).

Novel drug development
When a pharmaceutical company wants to develop a new drug, the first step is drug discovery.
During drug discovery, new molecules are developed and later tested in animal  models, most
commonly on rats or mouses, and in later stages dogs or monkeys. This early drug development
is called pre-clinical drug development. When initiating the first trials on humans, Phase 1-
trials, up to  100  healthy individuals are included and possible toxicity of the drug is  reviewed
and a  possible dose range is found where the drug is safe to administer. Phase 2-trails include
a few hundred participants with a certain disease and aims to review the drugs efficacy and
possible side effects. Phase  3-trials are conducted on larger groups, up to a few thousand
participants, with a specific disease  targeted  and  where  the therapeutic effect of the drug is
reviewed. Phase 4-studies are conducted after the drug has reached the market and aims to
monitor long-term effects of the drug. Approximately 70% of all drugs pass Phase 1-trials, 33%
pass Phase 2-trials and 25% pass Phase 3-trials (FDA). This means that of 100 drugs entering
Phase 1-trials, only 6 of them pass  Phase 3-trials and can apply for marketing approval by the
FDA.

There are some exceptions to the rules stated above, such as those stated  in the Orphan Drug
Act from 1983. The orphan drug act was passed through  Congress as a way to incentivize the
development of drugs to treat rare diseases. Rare diseases are those that affect fewer than  one
in 2000 individuals though many are far more rare than that. Conducting clinical research on
such diseases means more difficulty in recruiting patients and they are therefore given special
treatment by the FDA to keep the process from stagnating.

Another exception to the traditional rules are the FDA rules for the accelerated approval
program. The accelerated approval program was instituted to allow for earlier approval of drugs
to treat serious conditions. This program uses a surrogate endpoint as a  marker for clinical
benefit and considerably shortens the time it takes to receive FDA approval. Accelerated
approval is granted in between the completion of Phase 2-trials and the initiation of Phase-3
trials.  After accelerated approval is granted, the drug can be marketed toward the public in the
same way as traditional fully approved drugs.  Due to the time value of money this has
significant economic effects on the firms financials since the profits from the  drug come

                                     6
considerably earlier than they otherwise would have done.  Of  all  drugs granted accelerated
approval since 1992, only 10,3% were later taken off the market, which demonstrates the
importance and value of an accelerated approval designation.  An accelerated approval is not
applied for by the company itself but chosen by the FDA upon the completion of a Phase-2
trial. Since the start of the program in 1992, 192 drugs have been granted accelerated approval.

2.3  Theoretical assumptions
Studying abnormal stock returns caused by specific events can be done through multiple well-
defined event study measures. Some of  these are described by Brown and Warner (1985).
When examining  stock returns in conjunction to company specific events, it is essential to
make some theoretical assumptions for the research to be meaningful. This study relies on the
central assumptions listed below:

Market efficiency
To study the impact of an event  on publicly traded asset prices, the assumption has to be made
that security prices quickly adjust to fully reflect the value of the new information (Brown and
Warner, 1985). The efficient market hypothesis formulated by Fama (1970) is the basis for
modern  financial theory and states that share prices reflect all publicly available relevant
information as soon as it is available. This event study is conducted on daily stock returns and
thus assume that the stocks are sufficiently traded  within one day  to allow  for a  quick
adjustment in security prices.

Unforeseeable timing of the event
Pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies are open and quick when communicating the
phases of their different  studies. However, the timing of a specific FDA  accelerated  approval
can never be known since  companies themselves do not apply for accelerated approval but the
FDA  itself chooses to grant it to a drug upon completion of Phase-2 studies  (FDA, 2020).
Accelerated approvals therefore come years before full approval was expected by the market.
Simply put, the announcement of accelerated approval is not something that  can be  anticipated
by  the market beforehand in the same way that other company events can be known and
expected before they occur.

                                     7
Absence of other significant events
In order to be able to draw any correct conclusions about an individual event’s impact on asset
prices  it is essential  that no other major events or news emerged during the same time period.
If the abnormal returns relate to another event than the studied  event it would be impossible to
firmly relate any results to the specific event.  To adjust for this risk, press releases from all
companies around the event dates are reviewed so as to make sure that no other major company
news such as the publication of quarterly earnings or annual report occurred during our event
window.

2.4 Hypothesis
The hypothesis explored in this paper is whether the event of an accelerated approval being
granted to a pharmaceutical or biotechnology company leads to abnormal returns  for the
affected companies.

H1: Publicly traded pharmaceutical and biotechnology stocks experience abnormal returns
                       after being granted accelerated approval by the FDA.

3. Methodology

To test the hypothesis, the event study framework from Campbell  et al.  (1997)  is used.

3.2  Defining the estimation and event window
The events of interest in this study is the public FDA announcement that a novel drug has been
granted accelerated  approval. These announcements are delivered on all weekdays in the
United States with the exception for public holidays. Approvals can be delivered during any
time of day, both before, during and after market close. Hamill (2018) studied the timing of
FDA announcements and found that a majority of announcements are released after market
close. To adjust for this, data from the FDA database with announcement dates is cross-checked
with the corresponding company press releases. In cases where the announcement has been
delivered after market close, the event  date is adjusted so as to make the day after the
announcement the event date (𝑡  =  0). This is the day when we expect the market to react to
the news.  Since only American stocks are included, we do not need to adjust  the data for time
differences. The opening hours for the New York Stock Exchange and the NASDAQ are 09:30
a.m. to 04:00 p.m. Eastern Time (ET) and in cases where press releases are timestamped
Central Time or Pacific Time they are adjusted accordingly.

The regression window is defined as the timeline leading up to the event. The regression
window is divided into two sub-windows, the first being the estimation window, i.e. the period
before the event window and the second being the event window itself. The length of the
estimation and event windows can differ between event studies.  An event window can vary
from only a few minutes or hours when conducting research of momentary stock returns  to
multiple weeks. The shortest event windows are used when examining momentary responses
to new market information, for example  the daily financial news report on a major American
tv-channel (Busse, 2002). Long event windows are used when the researcher wants to be
certain that no information spillage has occurred prior  to the event,  Felimban (2018) used a 40
day event window when studying the impact of dividend announcements on stock returns. The
main benefit and reason for them using a long event window is that it accounts for leaked
insider information from the Board of Directors between the vote and the announcement. The
main issue with using a long event window is that it increases the risk of other events
influencing the stock returns. In the theoretical assumptions above, the importance of not
having any other significant events influence the stock price is emphasized and therefore, an

                                     9
event window of  one day before and after the event  is chosen for this study. This is to  make
sure that on days when the FDA announcement is given during market hours, the investors
have time to react to the announcement.  In addition to this, an event window consisting of four
days before and after the event day is also examined so as to  assess  if any information spillage
occurs and whether the change in company value in conjunction to the event remains for more
than one day.

Similarly to event windows, estimation windows, i.e. the number of days before the event
window that is chosen for our regression, can also differ between studies. Peterson (1989)
writes that the typical estimation  window is usually between 100 and 300 days for daily event
studies. Some studies choose to include trading days that occur after the event date but that is
not done in this study since the FDA approval announcement fundamentally changes the
company’s future outlook. The risk with having a too long estimation window is that its  stock
returns that far back in time may be inaccurate in describing how the stock should have behaved
on the event date in the absence of the event. The risk with having too few datapoints is that
one may not be able to model the stock returns correctly. In this study  100 days is chosen as
the estimation window. In addition to this, an alternative event window of 120 days and another
alternative event window of 80 days  is  also  analyzed as  with the objective to check the
robustness of our obtained results.  The estimation and event windows are visualized in the

4.  Empirical Data
4.1  Sampled events
The starting sample for this study consisted of the  192  FDA accelerated approvals that have
been awarded  to American pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies  since the beginning
of the accelerated approval program in  June  1992  and  up until December 2021.  Accelerated
approvals have through the years been granted to  companies of varying sizes, ownership
structures and geographical headquarters. Firm characteristics can be found in Appendix Table
2.  The exclusion criteria for this study began with companies  that were private on the event
date studied  (n=5). Afterwards,  all  major pharmaceutical companies were removed (n=102).
These were defined as companies with more than 10 drugs on the market.  The rationale for
excluding companies  with more than 10 approvals  is that for them an accelerated approval

likely will not be an event with major effect on the stock price since the majority of their future
earnings and business comes from products that are already well-established. Of the excluded
events, 37 from Merck  Sharp & Dohme, 18 from Janssen and  17 were from  Bristol Myers
Squibb. Thereafter, (n=35) events were removed because they stemmed from companies that
were subsidiaries to larger firms, for example EMD Serono which is a subsidiary to Merck and

(n=12) events were removed due to being a duplicate drug of another accelerated approval.
Some drugs are first granted accelerated approval for one single indication but thereafter
granted a second accelerated approval for an unrelated indication. Since the first accelerated
approval is the  most  unexpected news, only the first approval was included in this study. All
company websites were  thereafter  screened for other news released on the date of the
accelerated approval. Another (n=1) event was removed due to other major news occurring on
the event date.

4.2 Stock Data
The stock dataset consists of the daily closing prices for all 37 selected companies for all trading
days 125 days before the event date and  4  days after the event date. All days where no trading

The results from the regression presented above shows that on the event date, that is the date

initial company value when the drug was expected to go through the traditional and much
slower phases of drug approval. The surrounding days show no abnormal returns.  This
indicates that the market corrects the stock price for these assets  within the first trading day
and  that no information spillage occurs in  conjunction with the FDA communication of the
granted accelerated approval.

The benefits of obtaining an accelerated approval for the firm are multiple. First of all, the
revenue stream from the drug reaches the firm years earlier than it would have in  the case of
traditional drug approval. Secondly, the costs of Phase-3 trials and Phase-4 trials can be
lowered since these are conducted simultaneously and uses the same scientific research. It is
not the exact same thing as Phase-3 and -4 trials being completely removed but the total number
of required research subjects is lower and the total timeline of drug research becomes shorter,
consequently lowering the costs associated with it. One challenge with accelerated approval
may be that a company does not have a sufficient sales and marketing staff in place to maximize
profits from the drug immediately since this had been planned for a much later phase of drug

                                     15
development. However, companies are often capable of quickly ramping up these processes
once accelerated approval has been granted.

Summarized, the null hypothesis of zero abnormal returns on the event day can be rejected due
to the findings  that  the event results in significant abnormal returns  (a positive return of 11%
at the 1 percent  significance  level)  for the pharmaceutical and biotechnology stocks  on the
event date.

For our regression, one of the central assumptions was the market efficiency assumption stating
that the current  share price of a publicly traded stock reflects all of the publicly available
information regarding the company. This includes the prediction of future cash flows that will
reach the shareholder  as owner of stakes in the company. The other assumption was that the
timing of the event was unforeseeable and thus could not be known beforehand. Given these

                                     16
assumptions, the empirical results show that the announcement was in fact unexpected since
there  were significant abnormal returns  following the event  and that the market expects the
present value of the company’s future cash flows to be  larger since the cash flows from sales
of the drug will be arriving much earlier and that the risk that the drug will not receive approval
has been substantially lowered. As touched upon earlier, approximately 90% of all drugs
receiving accelerated approval  after Phase-2 studies  continue to be sold on the market whilst
in the traditional drug approval process only 25%  of all drugs that have passed Phase-2 trials
also pass Phase-3 trials.  This is also in line with the research by Cohen (2019) in that it
emphasizes the importance of announcements communicated by the FDA.  The market thus
views an accelerated approval as a  positive sign from the FDA and from  previous knowledge
they can also know that the risk of drug failure has been lowered compared to if the drug would
have undergone traditional approval.

6. Concluding remarks
6.1  Limitations  and  further research
One limitation to this study is the  fact that only 37  out of 292 accelerated approvals were
included. Ideally, a study would be conducted where the value of an accelerated approval could
be quantified for all involved companies. However, this is not currently feasible due to the
varying characteristics of companies included in the study.  Another limitation  of this study is
that it does not quantify the plausible parameters that may affect stock returns such as market
capitalization, annual revenue, liquidity or number of drugs already approved but only uses
number of drugs as a cut-off point for inclusion. The study also only covers a small time period.
This means that it does not capture the long-term effects of an accelerated approval on stock
returns  and how the market reaction to subsequent events is altered.

A  possible  future research question could  address the  relationship between R&D expenditure
and market returns to FDA announcements. One hypothesis could be that higher R&D
expenditure increases the chance of accelerated approval and therefore leads to  less of a
positive stock price chock upon approval. However, it would also be possible to imagine that

                                     19
higher R&D expenditure  leads to  more positive stock  price chocks upon approval since the
expected net incomes  previously  have been weighted down by R&D  and now becomes
positive. Perhaps  there are  also  differences  in stock performance depending on the possible
population size that will use the drug or the  price they will be able to charge for the drug.  Such
research would provide more insight into the research of  Acemoglu et al. (2004)  looking at the
combination of  financial effects of drug market size and how vast the drug offering is
depending on the market size.  Another interesting question could be to compare the American
pharmaceutical and biotechnology market with its European counterpart. American drug prices
are noticeably higher than their European counterparts which raises the question if an approval
in the US is worth more for the stock market than a European approval even if the total potential
target population may be smaller  in the US  than  it is  in Europe.

6.2  Conclusion
This  study  investigates the effect of  FDA accelerated approvals granted to publicly traded
pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies  by observing abnormal returns. The abnormal
returns are computed using a standard event study methodology  based on the market model to
test  the abnormal returns hypothesis.  The average abnormal returns on the event day and the
cumulative abnormal return over the event window are found to be approximately 11% and
13%  respectively,  significant at the 1 percent level. This result indicates that the accelerated
approval of a drug is  perceived as  a highly valuable event for the affected firm  by investors.
The markets pro-reactiveness to the event also indicates that the event was unexpected and not
already factored into stock prices.

On the basis of this research, the effect of accelerated approvals granted to companies that are
larger than the ones included in this study cannot be concluded.  In addition to this, the impact
of accelerated approval on private company valuations cannot be concluded either.  However,
the findings from this study may be useful for the internal controlling of a large pharmaceutical
or biotechnology company when placing value on their product pipeline.  The results can also
provide insight when conducting a valuation of a private pharmaceutical company having
received accelerated approval.  This study contributes to the understanding of  regulatory
announcements, specifically FDA announcements, and their effect on stock market returns and
valuation of small pharmaceutical  and biotechnology companies.

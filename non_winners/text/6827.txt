Abstract:

    The TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle, documented by Fleckenstein, Longstaff, and Lustig
     (2014), describes a persistent and economically significant violation of the no-arbitrage
     relationship between U.S. nominal Treasury securities, Treasury Inflation-Protected
     Securities (TIPS), and inflation swaps. We empirically test an explanation that has been
     suggested but not yet studied directly: that the convenience yield of nominal Treasury
    securities accounts for the mispricing. Following van Binsbergen, Diamond, and Grotteria
    (2022), we measure the convenience yield as the spread between box rates implied by
    S&P 500 options and nominal Treasury yields, and we incorporate it into the no-arbitrage
     relationship. Using daily data from June 2007 to August 2025 at 1-, 2-, and 3-year tenors,
     we find that adjusting for the convenience yield reduces the average absolute deviation
    from no-arbitrage conditions by 5 to 10 basis points per annum and eliminates the
     directional bias previously documented in the literature. The convenience yield does not,
     however, explain time-series variation in the mispricing outside of the Great Financial
     Crisis. We interpret these findings as evidence that the convenience yield resolves the
     puzzle’s most distinctive feature – its consistent direction – but acts primarily as a level
     shifter rather than a driver of high-frequency variation. Our results support including
     convenience yields alongside TIPS liquidity premia, Treasury credit risk, and
     intermediation frictions in any complete account of the puzzle.

Keywords:

     TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle; convenience yield; box rates; inflation swaps; risk-free rate

                                   I. Introduction

   In finance, few principles are held in as high regard as no-arbitrage. While researchers
tend to accept small deviations from this principle, we sometimes uncover deviations so large
that we must raise questions. One such case is what Fleckenstein et al. (2014) have termed
“the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle”. They find consistent violations of a no-arbitrage relationship
between nominal U.S. Treasury securities, U.S. inflation swaps – securities that give investors
an inflation-indexed interest rate in exchange for a pre-determined, fixed interest rate –, and
U.S. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) – bonds that protect investors from inflation
through having their principal indexed to the level of the Consumer Price Index (CPI). Haubrich,
                                                                                                  1
Pennacchi, and Ritchken (2012) express the no-arbitrage relationship in terms of yields :

Treasury securities, and rt,n is the real yield of TIPS, for continuously compounded zero-
coupon interest rates of tenor n observed at time t.
    Violations of this relationship are economically significant – averaging circa 50 basis points
per annum – and consistent in direction, such that the spread between nominal Treasury yields
and TIPS yields is consistently too small relative to inflation swap rates (Fleckenstein et al.,
2014). With evidence that it is not mispricing of inflation swaps that drives the arbitrage
(Fleckenstein et al., 2014; Ashton, 2008), one of two conditions must be true: TIPS are severely
underpriced or nominal U.S. Treasuries are severely overpriced, to the tune of billions of dollars
of total mispricing (Fleckenstein et al., 2014). It also means that there must exist a bias in
at least one of the two most frequently used measures of U.S. risk-free rates – either the TIPS
yield is a biased measure of the real risk-free rate, or the nominal Treasury yield is a biased
measure of the nominal risk-free rate. A resolution to the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle can thus
enhance our understanding of how safe assets are priced and how we should measure risk-free
rates in the American economy.
   Researchers have searched for market frictions that can explain the TIPS-Treasury bond
puzzle, such as liquidity premia in TIPS (D’Amico, Kim, and Wei, 2018; Andreasen, Chris-
tensen, and Riddell, 2021; Driessen, Nijman, and Simon, 2017; Pflueger and Viceira, 2016),
credit risk in the U.S. Treasury (Dittmar, Hsu, Roussellet, and Simasek, 2026), intermediary
frictions, and marking-to-market concerns (Ahn and Ahn, 2023). This paper investigates an
explanation that existing literature has suggested, but that has not yet been rigorously empiri-
cally studied, namely that the convenience yield of nominal Treasury securities can explain the
mispricing (Fleckenstein et al., 2014; Pflueger and Viceira, 2016). We investigate the following
research questions:

    1. Does the level of arbitrage violations between nominal Treasury securities, TIPS, and
    inflation swaps decrease when accounting for the convenience yield of nominal Treasury
     securities?

  2. Can variation in the arbitrage between nominal Treasury securities, TIPS, and inflation
     swaps be explained by variation in the convenience yield of nominal Treasury securities?

   The concept of convenience yields, brought to prominence by Krishnamurthy and Vissing-
Jorgensen (2012), is the finding that highly liquid safe assets like nominal U.S. Treasury secu-
rities have non-pecuniary benefits beyond the value of their cash flows – such as being widely
accepted collateral and allowing financial institutions to satisfy capital requirements. Investors
are willing to pay an additional price for these special properties, which lowers their yield below
the true nominal risk-free rate. The purpose of this study is to test whether accounting for the
price of these non-pecuniary benefits resolves the frequently documented TIPS-Treasury bond
puzzle.
   We draw on a different body of literature that measures the convenience yield in nominal
Treasury securities by comparing their yields to the interest rate that options markets use
to price options contracts, called the box rate. Following van Binsbergen et al. (2022) and
Diamond and van Tassel (2022), we define the convenience yield as the spread between box rates
and nominal Treasury yields. With this, we define two measures of the mispricing: First, the
non-adjusted mispricing, which is the inflation swap rate less the spread between the nominal
Treasury yield and the TIPS yield. Second, the mispricing adjusted for convenience yields,
                                                                        2
which is simply the non-adjusted mispricing less the convenience yield.  To investigate whether
the level of arbitrage violations can be reduced by accounting for the convenience yield, we test
whether the absolute value of the mispricing is smaller after adjusting for the convenience yield
on average. Since the mispricing should be zero in a no-arbitrage market, any deviation from
zero constitutes a mispricing. Further, we investigate whether variation in the convenience yield
can explain variation in the mispricing, we estimate an ordinary least squares (OLS) regression
with the non-adjusted mispricing as the dependent variable and the convenience yield as the
independent variable. We conduct these tests both over our entire sample period spanning from
2007 to 2025, and over a subsample excluding the Great Financial Crisis (GFC) to see if our
results are persistent over time or simply driven by unique dynamics in crisis periods.
   We find that adjusting for the convenience yield statistically significantly decreases the
average absolute deviation from no-arbitrage conditions by between 5 and 10 basis points per
annum in the full sample and between 4 and 7 basis points in the subsample excluding the
GFC, bringing markets significantly closer to satisfying no-arbitrage conditions. Further, the
previously documented consistent overpricing of Treasury securities relative to TIPS no longer
exists after adjusting for convenience yields. Further, while we find that the convenience yield
explains between 18 and 35 percent of the variation in the mispricing over our entire study
period, we note that this explanatory power is practically entirely driven by the GFC and that
the explanatory power does not remain after excluding it.
    Our results consistently show that the convenience yield is of the right size and direction to
resolve much of the observed mispricing in the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle, but that it is not
a meaningful predictor of the mispricing outside of crisis episodes. Our interpretation is that
when the convenience yield varies strongly, such as during the GFC, its effect on the TIPS-
Treasury bond puzzle can be detected. During normal times, the convenience yield is small in

                              II. Related Literature

   This paper mainly contributes to two bodies of literature. We most directly contribute to
the literature on the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle (Fleckenstein et al., 2014; Andreasen et al.,
2021; Driessen et al., 2017; Pflueger and Viceira, 2016; Dittmar et al., 2026; Ahn and Ahn,
2023). Most existing empirical analyses have argued that the explanation for the arbitrage
between nominal Treasury securities, TIPS, and inflation swaps is the relative illiquidity of
TIPS compared to nominal Treasury securities (D’Amico et al., 2018; Andreasen et al., 2021;
Driessen et al., 2017; Pflueger and Viceira, 2016). This research is generally in agreement that
TIPS liquidity explains a meaningful part, but not all of the mispricing, with Driessen et al.
(2017) providing a reasonable range of between 29 percent and 63 percent being explained by
TIPS illiquidity. Newer research finds different drivers of the mispricing. Dittmar et al. (2026)
find that U.S. Treasury credit risk explains between 46 percent and 78 percent of the mispricing
depending on tenor. Ahn and Ahn (2023) find that marking-to-market concerns explain around
53 percent of the mispricing and that marking-to-market concerns and intermediation frictions
combined explain around 70 percent. Despite these advances, no single channel has emerged as
a definitive explanation. We contribute to this literature by providing the first direct empirical
test of the conjecture, originally proposed by Fleckenstein et al. (2014) and tentatively echoed
by Pflueger and Viceira (2016), that the convenience yield in nominal Treasury securities is
responsible for the arbitrage. We find that accounting for convenience yields consistently brings
the mispricing closer to zero, consistent with their hypothesis, but that this effect operates
primarily as a level adjustment: The average convenience yield is of approximately the right
size to neutralize the documented directional bias, while variation in the convenience yield is
only informative about variation in the mispricing during crisis episodes.
   Further, we contribute to the body of literature that tries to identify the optimal reference
rate to measure convenience yield with. A large body of literature uses a variety of ways to
measure the convenience yield, including corporate bond spreads (Krishnamurthy and Vissing-
Jorgensen, 2012), near money assets (Nagel, 2016), Refcorp bonds (Longstaff, 2004), CIP-
implied foreign benchmarks (Du, Im, and Schreger, 2018), and box rates (van Binsbergen et

                                         4
                     Stockholm School of Economics

al., 2022; Diamond and van Tassel, 2022). Although the literature agrees that an economically
significant convenience yield in nominal Treasury securities exists, there is a lack of consensus
around the best way to measure this convenience yield with. van Binsbergen et al. (2022)
and Diamond and van Tassel (2022) provide a compelling theoretical argument that the box
rate is the best reference rate since it is the only rate that is both risk-free and carries no
convenience yield. A good way to test if the box rate is a valuable reference rate is to test if
using it can explain known anomalies in finance like covered interest parity (CIP) deviations
(van Binsbergen et al., 2022; Diamond and van Tassel, 2022). This paper contributes to the
field by finding evidence that box rates can indeed be used to resolve some part of the TIPS-
Treasury bond puzzle, suggesting their economic validity as a reference point with which to
measure the convenience yield. This being said we also clearly display the main limitation of
using box rates, namely substantial noise and volatility in the estimated interest rates.

                             III. Convenience Yields

A. What is the Convenience Yield?

   The convenience yield is the non-pecuniary value that investors derive from holding certain
safe assets, such as the safety, liquidity, and money-like services the safe asset provides. It
manifests as a yield discount: investors accept a lower financial return on convenient assets
because they value the implicit service flow. This convenience yield is especially pronounced in
nominal Treasury securities – the nominal Treasury rate is consistently below benchmarks of
other ‘pure’ risk-free rates (Krishnamurthy and Vissing-Jorgensen, 2012; van Binsbergen et al.,
2022). Further, the convenience yield is persistent and cannot be arbitraged away by shorting
the nominal Treasury security and going long the ‘pure’ risk-free asset since the non-pecuniary
benefits of the Treasury security cannot be replicated by the synthetic long leg. The lender of
the Treasury temporarily loses the asset’s convenience and subsequently demands compensation
for the loss of non-pecuniary value, thus eliminating the arbitrage opportunity in equilibrium
(Krishnamurthy, 2002).

B. How Should the Convenience Yield Enter the TIPS-Treasury Bond Puzzle?

   The convenience yield becomes directly relevant for Haubrich et al.’s (2012) no-arbitrage
relationship between the yields of nominal Treasury securities, TIPS, and inflation swaps. This
relationship does not account for the fact that investors are willing to pay for non-pecuniary
benefits. While TIPS and inflation swaps can be used to replicate the cash flows of a nominal
Treasury security, they cannot replicate the convenience of said Treasury security. Equivalently
expressed, TIPS and inflation swaps can only be used to replicate the cash flows of a risk-
free asset with no convenience. Haubrich et al.’s (2012) yield-based relationship can thus be
respecified as:
                                              inf      risk f ree     T IP S
                                 rt,n = rt,n − rt,n (2)

           risk f ree

free of any convenience yield.

   Although equation (2) and (3) are equivalent under our assumptions, we prefer the speci-
fication which makes explicit the convenience yield instead of using equation (2) directly, for
two reasons: (i) to highlight the role of extra-monetary factors in resolving the TIPS-Treasury
bond puzzle, and (ii) to make the time-varying structure of the convenience yield and its effect
on the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle directly observable.

C. Why the Spread Between the Box Rate and the Nominal Treasury Yield is an
   Adequate Measure of the Convenience Yield

   A box trade on European options is risk-free, providing a guaranteed future payoff in ex-
change for a fixed price today. At the same time, options contracts are risky assets, meaning
they do not carry a convenience yield. Thus, the interest rate one can gain through a box trade
– the so-called box rate – is a risk-free interest rate that is free of any convenience yield. This
can be exploited to measure the convenience yield of nominal Treasury securities as the spread
between the box rate and the yield of nominal Treasury securities. Such a measure is supe-
rior to other often-used measures of the convenience yield in two ways: (i) it avoids measuring
spreads between two safe assets, in which case we would only get a measure of the difference in
convenience yields, and (ii) it avoids measuring spreads using assets with credit risk, in which
case the convenience yield measure is confounded by a credit-risk premium (van Binsbergen et
al., 2022; Diamond and van Tassel, 2022).
   There are three main concerns that could render this measurement of the convenience yield
invalid: A spread in credit risk between the issuer of European SPX options and the U.S.
Treasury, a spread in liquidity premia between box trades and U.S. Treasury securities, and
additional cost of capital in the box rate imposed by margin requirements for the box trade.
   Credit risk in European SPX options is considered negligible by the precedent literature,
due to the large institutional protections that exist on options markets for the following reasons
(van Binsbergen et al., 2022): Any investor in SPX options must post daily variation margin
to the Chicago Board Options Exchange (CBOE). Next, CBOE posts additional margin to
the Options Clearing Corporation (OCC), which is designated a “Systematically Important
Financial Market Utility” and subsequently has access to emergency liquidity support from
the United States Federal Reserve. Thus, a default of an options contract would require (i) a
default of the individual trader counterparty, (ii) a default of CBOE, and at the same time (iii)
a default of OCC, with the United States Federal Reserve liquidity support being insufficient to
avoid default. In any default event, options contracts are considered “super-senior”, meaning
traders have an immediate claim to the value of their contract before any debtor is paid.

                                         6
                     Stockholm School of Economics

   Regarding liquidity premia in SPX options, research is very limited. However, research has
found that there is a liquidity premium in exchange-traded equity options (Choy and Wei, 2020;
Christoffersen, Goyenko, Jacobs, and Karoui 2018). In light of the fact that SPX options are
substantially more liquid than equity options (Schwartz, 2026), the liquidity premium in SPX
options should be a substantially smaller concern; however, we can not state with confidence that
the effects of this are negligible. In Section VII.A we find that our box rates are meaningfully
different to box rates constructed from options on a different index where contracts are less
liquid. A more thorough investigation of this and its effects on estimated box rates is left for
future research.
   Lastly, it is worth noting that CBOE acknowledges the box trade as a riskless trade, elim-
inating margin constraints that otherwise could increase the cost of capital of executing a box
trade to harvest the box rate (van Binsbergen et al., 2022).
   For the Treasury security side: Credit risk in the U.S. Treasury is remote but non-zero, with
a notable technical default event in 1979. Augustin, Chernov, Schmid, and Song (2021) argue
that the credit-risk component of U.S. nominal Treasury yields is non-negligible in affecting
benchmark-rate interpretations. Liquidity in the U.S. nominal Treasury security market is, in
normal times, among the highest of any bond market globally, particularly for on-the-run securi-
ties (Fleming, 2003; Fleming, Mizrach, and Nguyen, 2018). Nonetheless, Treasury yields embed
a measurable liquidity premium: Longstaff (2004) compares Treasuries to credit-equivalent Re-
fcorp bonds and finds an economically significant flight-to-liquidity premium in stress periods,
while Hu, Pan, and Wang (2013) document persistent yield-curve fitting noise that proxies for
market-wide illiquidity. Thus, there may be factors beyond convenience that drive the yield of
nominal Treasury securities to differ from the true risk-free rate.
   However, deviations on the side of nominal Treasury securities should not devalue the model
we suggest: If box rates are accepted as the true risk-free rate, then any wedge to U.S. Treasury
bond rates – which we denote “the convenience yield” but which could also be composed of
other factors – must be adjusted for. One can imagine that the spread between box rates and
U.S. Treasury bond yields is composed of (i) the convenience yield, (ii) a liquidity premium, and
(iii) a credit risk premium, but in such a case, the no-arbitrage relationship we study should
also be adjusted for both (i), (ii) and (iii). Thus, the concern of unobservable factors biasing
the nominal Treasury yield should only weaken the interpretation of our results, not the validity
of our model. Our model can thus only be invalidated by discrepancies between box rates and
the true risk-free rate.

   The data used for this study were gathered from three sources: London Stock Exchange
Group (LSEG) Workspace, Wharton Research Data Services (WRDS), and publicly available
information from the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (commonly known as
the Federal Reserve Board). All data in our sample for our main analysis run from the first
date our inflation swap data are available, June 18, 2007, to the last date that our options data
are available, August 29, 2025.

A. Nominal Treasury, TIPS, and Inflation Swap Data

   For the construction of the basic mispricing time series, we first collect a daily multivari-
ate time series of zero-coupon nominal U.S. Treasury yields from the Gürkaynak et al. (2007)
dataset, maintained by the Federal Reserve Board. This dataset contains continuously com-
pounded annual yields at 1-, 2-, and 3-year tenors. The yields are derived from an NSS curve
fitted to off-the-run Treasury securities. Second, we retrieve a daily multivariate time series
of zero-coupon TIPS yields from the Gürkaynak, Sack, and Wright (2010) dataset, maintained
by the Federal Reserve Board. This dataset contains continuously compounded annual yields
at 2- and 3-year tenors. Similar to the nominal yield data, the yields are derived from an
NSS-estimation. The dataset also contains the estimated NSS-parameters which we use to ex-
trapolate the 1-year tenor yield. It is, however, important to note that Gürkaynak et al. (2010)
actively chose to not extrapolate that far since their initial dataset of TIPS prices only included
tenors above 18 months. We thus note that interpretations at 1-year maturities must be made
with caution. Lastly, we gathered zero-coupon U.S. inflation swap data from LSEG at 1-, 2-,
and 3-year tenors. They provide aggregated daily time series of annually compounded annual
swap rates based on data from a multitude of contributors, which we convert to a continu-
ously compounded basis. The inflation swaps use CPI-U as the underlying series to match the
measure used for indexation of TIPS.

   The observed seasonality in the mispricings is an artefact of the NSS-estimation of TIPS
yields. The NSS-parameters are estimated by fitting an NSS-model to observations of individ-
ual TIPS, which do not necessarily have integer-year tenors (Gürkaynak et al., 2010). Thus,
the individual TIPS that underlie the NSS-estimation do not cover entire inflation-seasonality
cycles. On the other hand, the inflation swap rates are based on actual observations of integer-
year tenored inflation swaps, which do cover entire inflation-seasonality cycles. The result is
a mismatch in the degree of seasonality that is priced by the inflation swap contra the TIPS
bond. This means that the two inflation-indexed securities no longer cancel out their respec-
tive inflation-seasonality, ultimately producing seasonality in the mispricings. This difference
in seasonality between inflation swaps and TIPS bonds is confirmed through direct observation
in appendix B.
   This is not an invalidation of the no-arbitrage condition. If we could observe true integer-
year tenored TIPS yields, they would exhibit the same degree of seasonality as the inflation
swaps. In this case, since the TIPS yields are estimated including TIPS with a non-standard
number of days left until maturity, the estimated TIPS yields incorrectly carry a different degree
of seasonality than the inflation swaps do.

C. Exclusion of the Great Financial Crisis

   The convenience yield of nominal Treasury securities is documented to exhibit severely dif-
ferent behaviors during crisis episodes, compared to during non-crisis episodes (Krishnamurthy
and Vissing-Jorgensen, 2012). It is likely that investors value the non-pecuniary conveniences
of safety, liquidity and collateral higher during times of market stress. As such, it is reasonable
to expect the economic significance of the convenience yield to be larger during major crises,
such as the GFC. A question of interest is then whether the convenience yield is still econom-

                                        19
                     Stockholm School of Economics

ically significant in the context of the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle during non-crisis episodes.
To investigate this, we also execute all tests on a sub-sample which excludes the GFC.
   Following the timeline laid out by Brunnermeier (2009), we treat August 2007 as the onset
of the financial crisis and define the crisis period as August 2007 to June 2009, consistent with
the NBER recession trough (NBER, 2010). All our main results are estimated and presented
on both our full sample and our subsample excluding the GFC.

                                 VI. Results

A. Research Question 1

   There are two parts of this question that have been highlighted by researchers: (i) Market
prices of TIPS and Treasury securities consistently deviate from no-arbitrage conditions, such
that the mispricing defined in equation (4) is almost always non-zero, and (ii) these deviations
are consistent in direction, such that the mispricing defined in equation (4) is consistently
positive.
   We find that adjusting for the convenience yield performs very well in resolving (ii), and
is useful, although not exhaustive, in resolving (i). After adjusting for the convenience yield
in accordance with equation (3), the average absolute level of deviations from no-arbitrage
conditions is decreased. Still, deviations from zero-mispricing remain after accounting for the
convenience yield. Adjusting for the convenience yield can thus decrease the average absolute
level of arbitrage that exists in the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle, but cannot remove all no-
arbitrage violations entirely. While some absolute level of arbitrage remains, the convenience
yield is able to bring the mean mispricing much closer to zero, such that the mispricing is no
longer consistent in direction. While this is evidence that the convenience yield is of the right
size and direction to account for nominal Treasury securities being systematically rich relative
to TIPS-plus-inflation-swaps, it is not evidence that the convenience yield actually drives this
mispricing.

B. Research Question 2

   We find that the convenience yield is a predictor that can explain up to 35 percent of the
variation in the mispricing between TIPS, nominal Treasury securities, and inflation swaps
over our full sample with slope estimates that align well in both direction and magnitude to
the economic expectation. However, this effect is almost entirely carried by the GFC. Despite
the GFC generating much of the in-sample fit, its high autocorrelation means it contributes
far less information than its raw observation count suggests. Thus, we see that the effect is
                                                                                2
not statistically significant at 1- and 2-year tenors despite the high R . When excluding the
                                              2
GFC, we find negative slope estimates and R values below 0.03, with our slope estimate being
statistically insignificant at 2- and 3-year tenors.
   Together with the results from Section VI.A, a reasonable interpretation is that the conve-
nience yield does act as a constant shifter in the no-arbitrage relationship, but that the conve-
nience yield does not vary in an economically meaningful way outside of severe crisis episodes. If
economic variations of the convenience yield are small during “normal times”, noise introduced
by the box-rate estimation is likely to dominate the convenience yield signal, making it a poor
statistical predictor of the mispricing. A more skeptical interpretation is that the convenience
yield is not a significant factor driving the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle, invalidating the hypoth-
esis laid out by Fleckenstein et al (2014), but that since the convenience yield is positive on
                                    1

tenor may be an artefact of the higher uncertainty in the TIPS extrapolation to the 1-year
                                                                                                         1
tenor as explained in Section IV. The slopes of the convenience yield on the mispricing A
are not statistically significant across the 1-year and 2-year tenors, but the magnitude in slope
coefficients over tenors reveals two interesting phenomena. A 1 percentage point increase in
                                                                                        1
convenience yield should lead to a 1 percentage point increase in the mispricing A  over all
tenors, i.e. the slope coefficients should be strictly 1 – if convenience yields are exogenous from
other predictors of mispricing. A possible explanation for the fact that the slope estimate is
larger than 1 for the 1-, and 2-year tenors is that the convenience yield co-moves with other
predictors of mispricing, such as a liquidity premium in TIPS. This makes economic sense:
liquidity premia and the convenience yield, which are both “flight-to-safety” premia, are higher
in times of high macroeconomic stress. Indeed, D’Amico et al. (2018) document a peak in their
estimated TIPS liquidity premium around the GFC, during which we and van Binsbergen et al.
(2022) also document a peak in convenience yields. An explanation of the observed decrease
in slope coefficient over tenors is that random measurement error in the regressor – in this
case noise in convenience yields induced by NSS-estimation – grows larger with longer tenors,
causing greater attenuation of the estimated effect.
   The same results do not hold when excluding the GFC. The intercept is statistically signif-

                                        25
                     Stockholm School of Economics

icantly positive across all tenors, suggesting that an average mispricing of 20-31 basis points
remains even when the convenience yield is zero. Further, the estimated coefficients of the
                                        1
convenience yield on the mispricing A  are negative, contradicting the economic expectation
outlined in Section III.B. This finding is statistically significant at the 1% level at the 1-year
tenor. An economically sensible interpretation is that, outside of crisis episodes, the conve-
nience yield negatively co-moves with a positive unobservable predictor or positively co-moves
with a negative unobservable predictor of the mispricing. Together with the full-sample results,
an interpretation is that the convenience yield co-moves with other market-stress factors, for
example a TIPS liquidity premium, during crisis episodes, but does not co-move or co-moves
negatively with the same factors during non-crisis episodes. These interpretations should be
taken with caution, however, as they are largely based on statistically insignificant estimates of
slope coefficients.

                               VIII. Discussion

   We empirically test an explanation for the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle that has been sug-
gested in the literature but not yet rigorously studied: that the convenience yield of nominal
Treasury securities accounts for the observed deviations from no-arbitrage conditions between
TIPS, nominal Treasuries, and inflation swaps. The average raw mispricing in our sample is
between 25 and 31 basis points per annum, with an average absolute mispricing between 25
and 33 basis points. After adjusting for the convenience yield using box rates (van Binsbergen
et al., 2022), the average mispricing falls to between −5 and +5 basis points, and the average
absolute mispricing falls to between 17 and 28 basis points. The directional bias previously
documented in the literature – where nominal Treasury securities are systematically expensive
relative to TIPS-plus-inflation-swaps – disappears. However, from our regression of the mispric-
ing on convenience yields, we find that variation in the convenience yield cannot consistently
explain variation in the mispricing.

A. Interpretation of Results

   Our headline finding is best framed as a directional result, not a variation result. Two
distinct empirical exercises in the paper speak to different aspects of the convenience yield
channel, and they yield different conclusions.

                                        34
                     Stockholm School of Economics

   The first exercise, in Section VI.A, asks whether the average convenience yield is of the
right size to account for the average mispricing. The answer is yes. The average convenience
yield in our sample is approximately the same magnitude as the average puzzle, and adjusting
for it brings the mean mispricing close to zero across all tenors, while reducing the average
absolute deviation from no-arbitrage conditions by 5 to 10 basis points. This is the level result.
We emphasize that this is a magnitude-consistency check rather than a causal identification:
                    2      1     conv
by construction, A = A − r   , so subtracting a positive quantity from a positively biased
series will always pull the series toward zero. The non-trivial finding is that the magnitudes
line up: the average convenience yield is neither far too small nor far too large to neutralize the
directional bias.
   The economic interpretation of the level result is straightforward and arguably the most
important contribution of this paper. While much of the existing literature has framed the
puzzle as evidence that TIPS are systematically too cheap (typically attributing this to TIPS
illiquidity), our results suggest a complementary framing: nominal Treasuries are systematically
too expensive, and the reason is that they confer a convenience that TIPS and inflation swaps
cannot replicate. A synthetic Treasury constructed from TIPS and inflation swaps replicates
the cash flows of a nominal Treasury but not the safety, liquidity, and money-like services that
investors derive from holding the nominal bond directly. The wedge between the two should
reflect this difference in non-pecuniary value, and the size of the wedge in our sample is consistent
with such a story.
   The second exercise, in Section VI.B, asks the stronger question of whether variation in
the convenience yield explains variation in the mispricing. Here the evidence is meaningfully
weaker. In the full sample, the convenience yield explains 18 to 35 percent of the variation in the
mispricing, but the slope coefficients are statistically insignificant at the 1- and 2-year tenors.
We further find that the intercept is statistically indistinguishable from zero at the 1- and 2-year
tenors in the full sample, which can be interpreted as the average mispricing after controlling
for the convenience yield not being significantly different from zero, providing further support
to the level result.
   When we exclude the GFC, the relationship between the convenience yield and the mispricing
weakens substantially. The slope coefficients become small and negative, significantly so at the
1-year tenor, and the intercept becomes significantly positive at 20-31 basis points across all
tenors. This pattern is not what the convenience yield channel alone would predict. We see two
readings of this finding, neither of which we can fully rule out with our data.
   The first reading is that the convenience yield acts as a near-constant level shifter outside
of crises. Under this reading, the convenience yield is real and is the right size on average
to neutralize the directional bias, but it does not vary enough during normal times to drive
day-to-day movements in the mispricing. Other factors, such as TIPS liquidity premia, in-
termediary frictions, and credit risk drive the high-frequency variation, and the convenience
yield contributes mostly through its mean. This reading is consistent with Krishnamurthy and
Vissing-Jorgensen’s (2012) finding that the convenience yield exhibits sharply different behavior
in crisis versus non-crisis episodes.
   The second reading is more skeptical. It is that variation in the convenience yield is gen-

                                        35
                     Stockholm School of Economics

uinely uninformative about the mispricing outside of crises, and that the strong full-sample
co-movement is essentially a single-event phenomenon driven by the joint behavior of these
series during 2007–2009. Under this reading, the convenience yield is not a meaningful driver
of the puzzle’s time-series properties, and the level match in Section VI.A is closer to a useful
accident than to evidence of a structural mechanism. We do not think this reading is the most
natural one. The magnitude match is informative, and the structural argument in Section III is
independent of the empirics, but this reading is consistent with the data and we cannot reject
it.
   A third possibility, which we view as more likely than either reading above but cannot
directly test, is that box-rate measurement error dominates true convenience yield variation
outside of crises. NSS estimation noise, residual put-call parity violations, and the use of mid
prices as proxies for transaction prices all introduce noise into the constructed convenience yield
series. During the GFC, when true convenience yield variation was large, this noise was small
relative to signal. During calm periods, the signal-to-noise ratio likely deteriorates substantially,
which would attenuate slope coefficients toward zero and could plausibly flip their sign in the
presence of any negatively co-moving omitted variable.
   We also note that the full-sample slope coefficient at the 1-year tenor exceeds one, which
is informative under our structural specification. Equation (3) implies that if the convenience
                                                    1     conv
yield were the only relevant friction, the slope of A  on r    should equal one exactly. A slope
                           conv
above one indicates that r    co-moves positively with another driver of the mispricing – most
plausibly a TIPS liquidity premium, which D’Amico et al. (2018) document as peaking during
the GFC, the same period during which we and van Binsbergen et al. (2022) document a peak in
convenience yields. This positive co-movement during crises is consistent with both convenience
yields and TIPS liquidity premia rising together as flight-to-safety phenomena, and it suggests
that the full-sample regression captures a combination of the convenience yield channel and
other crisis-sensitive factors that we cannot separately identify.

B. Relation to existing literature

   Our results contribute to a body of literature that has progressively decomposed the TIPS-
Treasury bond puzzle into constituent frictions. Driessen et al. (2017) attribute between 29 and
63 percent of the mispricing to TIPS liquidity, Dittmar et al. (2026) attribute between 46 and
78 percent to U.S. Treasury credit risk, and Ahn and Ahn (2023) attribute around 70 percent
to marking-to-market concerns and intermediation frictions combined. Our finding that the
convenience yield resolves a substantial portion of the mispricing, particularly its directional
component, adds another factor to this list, and one that has been suggested in the literature
(Fleckenstein et al., 2014; Pflueger and Viceira, 2016) but not previously tested directly.
   The collective explanatory shares in this literature far exceed 100 percent, which means
these factors must materially co-move. They are not competing explanations; they are likely
partial views of overlapping economic phenomena. Convenience yields rise during flights to
safety, as do TIPS liquidity premia and intermediary funding constraints. Treasury credit risk
perceptions move with broader macroeconomic stress. Any single-factor regression, including
ours, picks up a combination of these correlated drivers and cannot cleanly identify the unique

                                        36
                     Stockholm School of Economics

contribution of any one. A natural next step for this literature is a joint test of these factors in a
unified specification. Our paper contributes the framework and the measurement infrastructure
to include the convenience yield in such a specification.
   Our paper also contributes to the literature on how convenience yields should be measured.
van Binsbergen et al. (2022) and Diamond and van Tassel (2022) propose box rates as the
optimal reference rate for measuring convenience yields, on the grounds that they are simulta-
neously risk-free and free of convenience yield. By showing that convenience yields measured
this way can resolve the directional component of an entirely different anomaly – one not pre-
viously studied by this literature – we provide out-of-sample support for box rates as a useful
reference rate. We note, however, that our results also expose a meaningful limitation of the
method: the noise in box-rate-based convenience yields is substantial enough that meaningful
variation outside of crisis episodes is difficult to detect. Whether this reflects a genuine property
of convenience yields (low variation in normal times) or a measurement limitation of box rates
is not something we can resolve here, but the question is itself a contribution to the literature
on optimal reference rates.

C. Theoretical Implications

   Our results suggest that the standard framing of the TIPS-Treasury puzzle as evidence that
TIPS are too cheap is incomplete. Reframing the puzzle as evidence that nominal Treasuries
are too expensive shifts the locus of the anomaly from the inflation-protected leg of the spread
to the nominal leg, and points naturally to convenience yields as a candidate explanation. More
broadly, the result implies that empirical work using nominal Treasury yields as a measure of
the nominal risk-free rate may carry a systematic downward bias of the order of 25–30 basis
points at short tenors. For applications where this bias matters, such as breakeven inflation
decompositions, term premium estimation, or any work that subtracts the nominal risk-free rate
from another rate, using box rates rather than Treasury yields as the reference rate is likely to
produce more economically interpretable results.

D. Practical Implications

   For practitioners attempting to harvest the TIPS-Treasury arbitrage, our results imply that
the apparent arbitrage is smaller than it appears once the convenience yield foregone by holding
the synthetic position is accounted for. The remaining mispricing after this adjustment, while
still on average non-zero, is substantially smaller, and is comparable to or smaller than typical
bid-ask spreads in TIPS and inflation swap markets. This may help explain why the apparent
arbitrage has persisted: a meaningful share of it is not arbitrage at all, but compensation for
the convenience that the synthetic position fails to provide.
   That being said, accounting for the convenience yield does not eliminate the puzzle. Sub-
stantial mispricing remains after accounting for the convenience yield, implying that there are
other factors that explain the mispricing. Our work does not contradict previous research in
the field. We argue that the convenience yield is one of a multitude of factors explaining the
mispricing.

E. Limitations and the Boundary of our Claims

   Several limitations bound the interpretation of our results. First, our sample is limited to
1-, 2-, and 3-year tenors, with noted limitations to the extrapolation required for the 1-year
TIPS yield. The 1-year results consistently show the largest standard errors and the most
volatile coefficient estimates across specifications, which aligns with the literature’s view that
1-year TIPS yields are too sensitive to indexation-lags to be wholly interpretable. The 2- and
3-year results are the most reliable, and we view them as carrying most of the weight of our
conclusions.
   Second, our convenience yield measure is constructed exclusively from SPX options. As
shown in Section VII.A, box rates derived from RUT options differ from SPX-derived box
rates by approximately 30 basis points on average, a magnitude comparable to the average
convenience yield itself. We follow the precedent of van Binsbergen et al. (2022) in using SPX
options because of their dominant liquidity, but we cannot rule out that a different choice of
underlying would meaningfully alter our results.
   Third, we cannot identify the convenience yield channel cleanly from other crisis-sensitive
factors. The full-sample regression results in Section VI.B are consistent with convenience yields
driving the mispricing during crises, but they are equally consistent with the convenience yield
acting as a proxy for a broader stress factor that includes TIPS liquidity premia, intermediary
frictions, or credit risk. A joint specification including these factors is needed to make sharper
claims, and we view this as the most natural extension of our work.
   Fourth, accounting for the convenience yield does not eliminate the puzzle. A statistically
significant absolute deviation from no-arbitrage conditions remains in all specifications, and
the unexplained portion of the mispricing remains substantial. Our results should be read as
identifying the convenience yield as one factor among several, with a particular role in explaining
the puzzle’s directional component, rather than as a complete resolution of the anomaly.
   Taken together, our findings support a calibrated claim: the convenience yield in nominal
Treasury securities is real, is approximately the right size to account for why nominal Treasuries
are systematically expensive relative to TIPS-plus-inflation-swaps, and belongs in any complete
account of the TIPS-Treasury puzzle. Whether convenience yields also drive high-frequency
variation in the puzzle outside of crisis episodes is a question on which our evidence is weaker,
and which we leave to future work.

                              IX. Conclusion

   This paper investigates whether the convenience yield of nominal Treasury securities can
resolve the TIPS-Treasury bond puzzle documented by Fleckenstein et al. (2014). Following
van Binsbergen et al. (2022), we measure the convenience yield as the spread between box rates
implied by SPX options and nominal Treasury yields, and we test whether incorporating it into
the no-arbitrage relationship between TIPS, nominal Treasuries, and inflation swaps reduces
the observed mispricing.
   We find that adjusting for the convenience yield reduces the average absolute mispricing by
5 to 10 basis points and eliminates the directional bias previously documented in the literature.

                                        38
                     Stockholm School of Economics

The average convenience yield is of approximately the right size to account for why nominal
Treasuries are systematically expensive relative to TIPS-plus-inflation-swaps. We interpret this
as evidence that the puzzle is, in part, not a puzzle: a meaningful share of the wedge reflects
compensation for the convenience that a synthetic Treasury constructed from TIPS and inflation
swaps cannot replicate.
   The convenience yield does not, however, explain time-series variation in the mispricing
outside of crisis episodes. Our regression results are dominated by the Great Financial Crisis,
and excluding it leaves the relationship between the convenience yield and the mispricing weak
and wrong-signed. We read this as consistent with the convenience yield acting primarily as a
level shifter in normal times, with measurement noise in box rates plausibly obscuring whatever
variation exists.
   Our contribution is therefore narrower than a full resolution of the puzzle but specific in
what it adds: convenience yields belong in the conversation alongside TIPS liquidity premia,
Treasury credit risk, and intermediation frictions, and they specifically account for the puzzle’s
most distinctive feature, its consistent direction. A natural next step is to test these factors
jointly, since their individually estimated explanatory shares far exceed 100 percent and they
almost certainly co-move. We leave this to future work.

Appendix A: The Process of NSS-Estimation of Yield Curves

NSS-estimation is used in two places: to fit a daily box-rate yield curve from the panel of tenor-
specific box rates obtained from SPX options, and to observe synthetic 1-, 2-, and 3-year TIPS
yields from the daily NSS parameters published by the Federal Reserve. Both applications rely
on the same parametric form, which we review briefly here. The model by Nelson and Siegel
(1987) specifies the zero-coupon yield at tenor n as a four-parameter function:
                       (        )    (              )
                                        −n/τ1              −n/τ1
                              1 − e            1 − e        −n/τ1

                                     n/τ1               n/τ1

The three terms admit a standard economic interpretation: β0 governs the long-run level, β1
the short-end slope (its loading decays monotonically from one to zero as n grows), and β2
a medium-term hump whose location is controlled by the decay parameter τ1 > 0. Svensson
(1994) extends the model by adding a second curvature term with its own decay parameter τ2:
             (        )    (              )    (              )
                          −n/τ1              −n/τ1                       −n/τ2

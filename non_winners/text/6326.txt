Intergenerational wealth mobility: How policy changes affect intergenerational
wealth mobility in Sweden

Abstract:

   We use a life-cycle simulation model based on Swedish administrative data to quantify
   how wealth taxation affects intergenerational wealth mobility. We find that a 1
   percentage point increase in wealth tax reduces the mean wealth gap between the Very
   Rich and the Non-Rich by 6.2%. However, when we assign counterfactual behavioral
   parameters, risk aversion, time preference, and bequest motives, from the Non-Rich
   group to all socioeconomic groups, the effect of the tax drops to 0.2%. This shows that
   persistent wealth accumulation is primarily driven by inherited behavioral traits rather
   than inherited wealth alone and that the effect of wealth tax on wealth accumulation
   gaps is limited. Even under a flat 5% wealth tax, wealth accumulation among the Very
   Rich remains significantly higher. This suggests that while wealth taxation can reduce
   absolute disparities, long-run wealth persistence is primarily driven by behavioral
   differences rather than taxation alone.

Keywords:

   Intergenerational wealth mobility, Wealth taxes, Behavioral heterogeneity, Life-
   cycle simulation, Wealth accumulation

1. Introduction

Wealth disparities are a longstanding characteristic of modern economies, with
significant implications for social mobility and economic policy. Intergenerational wealth
mobility plays a crucial role in determining long-term economic opportunity and
inequality. The aim of this paper is to evaluate how policy changes regarding taxation
influences intergenerational wealth mobility, using a structurally calibrated life-cycle
model grounded in Swedish data.

While Sweden is often recognized as having relatively high intergenerational mobility
compared to other countries (Black and Devereux, 2011), wealth persistence remains
substantial. Estimates suggest that Sweden’s intergenerational wealth elasticity is
approximately 0.37, meaning that 37% of parental wealth differences persist into the next
generation (Ebrahimian and Sodini, 2024). Although some degree of mobility exists,
more than 20% of children from the lowest wealth quintile move to the top two quintiles,
while 25% of children from wealthy parents fall into the lowest two quintiles, there is
evidence that Sweden’s wealth concentration has increased in recent decades (Ebrahimian
and Sodini, 2024; Roine and Waldenström, 2008).

One driver of this trend is the shift in tax policy over the past 30 years. The abolition of
Sweden’s wealth tax in 2007 has been linked to rising wealth inequality, with the wealth-
to-income ratio increasing to 6.85, higher than Germany (6.16) and Finland (5.11) (Black,
Devereux, Landaud, and Salvanes, 2022). This trend aligns with broader international
patterns, where wealthier households consistently earn higher returns on investment,
exacerbating wealth disparities over time (Benhabib, Bisin, and Luo, 2015). Notably,
research has shown that capital income risk and differences in asset returns contribute
significantly to wealth concentration at the top, reinforcing long-term persistence
(Gabaix, Lasry, Lions, and Moll, 2016).

Despite extensive research on wealth inequality, most analyses focus on wealth
concentration rather than mobility. While intergenerational mobility has been explored in
various national contexts, particularly in comparative studies between the United States,
the United Kingdom, and the Nordic countries, there is limited research on the specific
relationship between intergenerational wealth mobility and tax policy in Sweden. Black
and Devereux (2011) highlight the role of inheritances, investment behavior, and tax
structures in wealth transmission, yet the mechanisms through which tax policies
influence intergenerational wealth persistence remain insufficiently explored.
Furthermore, studies on wealth accumulation suggest that labor income, rather than
inheritance, is the dominant driver of wealth for most individuals, raising questions about
the relative importance of taxation in shaping wealth mobility (Black et al., 2023).

To address this gap, we build on the empirically calibrated framework by Ebrahimian and
Sodini (2024), which is itself grounded in the heterogeneous-agent model developed by
Benhabib, Bisin, and Luo (2011). We use this structure to simulate how counterfactual
wealth tax regimes affect long-run wealth persistence in Sweden. The model incorporates
key drivers of wealth accumulation (labor income, bequests, and returns on investment)
and accounts for heterogeneous preferences across socioeconomic groups, including

                                   2
differences in risk aversion, time preference, and bequest motives. This life-cycle
simulation framework allows us to explore how tax policy interacts with behavioral traits
to shape intergenerational mobility outcomes. In doing so, we aim to contribute to the
limited body of research that explicitly links tax structures to intergenerational wealth
dynamics in a Swedish context.

The model also incorporates portfolio choice across multiple asset classes, distinguishing
between risky and risk-free investments, with returns calibrated to reflect both systematic
and idiosyncratic risk. Labor income profiles and initial endowments are allowed to vary
by background group, capturing the influence of human capital investment and early life
transfers. Wealth accumulation is thus endogenously determined by a combination of
individual decisions, inherited financial resources, and behavioral traits shaped by
socioeconomic background.

Wealth evolution in our model is shaped by individual consumption-behavior, risk-taking
and savings attitudes, portfolio allocation, and capital returns. These individual-level
dynamics are aggregated to assess group-level outcomes, such as intergenerational wealth
elasticity and mobility. Using the empirically calibrated framework developed by
Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024), we simulate counterfactual wealth tax scenarios to
examine how taxation may influence the persistence of wealth across generations in
Sweden.

To put our work into a relevant context we will provide a short history of wealth and tax
policy in Sweden. Sweden’s historical trajectory in wealth distribution and taxation
makes it a compelling case for studying intergenerational wealth mobility. Once among
the most unequal countries in Europe, Sweden had a hierarchical society where wealth
dictated political influence through property-based voting rights (Piketty, 2014).

During the 20th century, the Swedish Social Democratic Party leveraged wealth data to
implement progressive taxation, significantly reducing inequality (Piketty, 2014).
However, economic turmoil in the 1970s, driven in part by the oil crises, prompted a shift
toward neoliberal policies inspired by Milton Friedman. This led to deregulation,
privatization, and reduced state intervention (Schön, 2010). The shift accelerated in the
1990s and early 2000s, resulting in key tax policy changes:

    • 1990/91: Separation of capital gains tax from labor income tax.
    • 1995: Introduction of the high-income surtax (värnskatt).
    • 2004: Abolition of the inheritance tax.
    • 2007: Abolition of the wealth tax.
    • 2008: Replacement of real estate tax with a lower municipal property fee.

These reforms marked a shift toward capital-friendly policies, contributing to rising
wealth concentration. Empirical evidence suggests a U-shaped trend in inequality, with
declining wealth disparities in the mid-20th century followed by a reversal in recent
decades (Roine and Waldenström, 2008). Given this background, this paper examines the
impact of tax policy changes on intergenerational wealth mobility in Sweden.

                                   3
We find that wealth taxes have a moderating effect on the wealth gap between the Very
Rich and the Non-Rich, as well as between the Rich and the Non-Rich. Wealth gap
elasticity appears to be inversely related to tax rates, where a one percentage point
increase in the wealth tax reduces the wealth gap by approximately 6.2% and 1.4% for
the respective socioeconomic groups. This can be interpreted as an increase in flexibility
within socioeconomic segmentation, leading to improved wealth mobility.

When testing counterfactual values for the behavioral parameters, γ (risk aversion), χ
(bequest motives), and ρ (time preference), we assign them the values of the Non-Rich
group. The resulting drop in the wealth gap is significant, reinforcing the conclusion by
Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024): it is not only the level of inherited wealth that matters, but
also the behavioral traits that drive persistent wealth accumulation.

While our model simulates clear mechanisms for wealth mobility, it also underscores the
persistent challenge of addressing the wealth gap. From a policy perspective, it is difficult
to directly influence the behavioral drivers of wealth accumulation. Therefore, tools such
as wealth taxation may present a more feasible solution. That said, the debate remains as
to whether efforts should focus on reducing wealth at the very top or instead on improving
the capacity of the lower-wealth groups to accumulate wealth.

Finally, our simulations show that even under hypothetically high wealth tax rates of 5%,
which are unrealistic in an empirical context, the wealth gap continues to widen over a
25-year period. This underscores the powerful compounding effect of wealth
accumulation among the Very Rich.

2. Related Works

Our primary empirical foundation is Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024), who estimate
preference heterogeneity using Swedish administrative data. Their model captures how
inherited attitudes toward saving and risk influence long-run wealth accumulation and
intergenerational persistence. We replicate and extend their framework by incorporating
wealth tax policy changes into their model. In doing so, our contribution lies in adapting
their preference-driven model to evaluate how taxation reshapes the distributional
trajectory of wealth across generations.

An important empirical complement is Black et al. (2023, who decompose individual
wealth accumulation into labor income, capital gains, and inheritances. Their findings
suggest that while inherited wealth plays a role, it is far from the dominant factor,
particularly for most of the population. This is consistent with Black et al. (2022), who
demonstrate that the importance of inheritances for explaining the observed wealth
distribution is often overstated. Instead, both papers point toward the need to understand
behavioral and structural mechanisms behind lifetime accumulation. Our study
contributes to this literature by structurally modeling the implications of such behavioral
mechanisms, captured through preference heterogeneity, and analyzing how they interact
with tax policy to shape long-run wealth dynamics. Black and Devereux (2011) offer a
broader overview of the empirical literature on intergenerational mobility and its
persistence across time and countries. Black et al. (2023) focus on Norway, a country
which shares many structural and institutional similarities with Sweden, including a
comprehensive welfare state, detailed administrative data, and relatively equal
opportunity structures, making it a strong empirical reference point for our Swedish
setting. Our contribution builds on these insights by applying a similar empirical
foundation to Sweden and exploring how institutional design, in particular, taxation,
shapes wealth mobility outcomes.

On the methodological side, our paper connects to the simulation-based literature that
models wealth dynamics through structurally calibrated life-cycle models. Hubmer,
Krusell, and Smith (2021) build a structural model to analyze the evolution of U.S. wealth
inequality over time, incorporating heterogeneity in saving behavior, investment returns,
and income risk. Their work supports our approach of using preference heterogeneity,
estimated from real-world data to simulate the long-run distributional outcomes of
different policy settings. Our contribution lies in applying this approach to a Swedish
context, using preference parameters drawn from Swedish administrative data, rather than
assumed or estimated within the model. Furthermore, our data source originates from the
Swedish administrative register data first used in Calvet, Campbell, and Sodini (2007),
who provide evidence of systematic heterogeneity in household investment behavior and
risk exposure. Their findings justify the use of type-specific parameters in our model to
capture differences in financial behavior across population subgroups. We extend this by
examining how these differences affect intergenerational wealth dynamics under varying
policy regimes.

Finally, our work contributes to the literature on taxation and wealth mobility. Benhabib,
Bisin, and Luo (2019) develop a quantitative model of wealth distribution and social

                                   5
mobility, emphasizing the role of stochastic returns and saving behavior in generating
persistent inequality. Their model demonstrates that small differences in risk profiles and
saving behavior can lead to large differences in mobility outcomes, highlighting the
importance of incorporating behavioral heterogeneity in structural models of wealth
accumulation, an approach we adopt in our own simulation framework. In addition,
Benhabib, Bisin, and Zhu (2011) analytically characterize how fiscal policies, particularly
capital income and estate taxation, impact the stationary distribution of wealth. We adopt
a similar framework to simulate counterfactual policy environments and evaluate how tax
changes affect the intergenerational wealth mobility under heterogeneous preferences.
Our addition lies in applying this logic to a calibrated Swedish model developed by
Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024) and evaluating how a range of realistic tax reforms
influence wealth persistence across generations.

3. Data

This paper adopts the wealth accumulation model and utilizes the estimated parameters
presented by Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024). Their work, which uses Swedish
administrative register data to recover individual preferences and other model
components, forms the direct empirical basis for our simulations. We do not access or
manipulate the underlying microdata ourselves; instead, we use their complete model
specification, and the summarized parameter estimates and data moments derived from
their analysis as inputs for our simulation model. The methods Ebrahimian and Sodini
(2024) used to obtain these parameters and data moments are summarized below, for a
comprehensive explanation, readers are referred to their original paper. Our empirical
method, which utilizes these parameters, is detailed in Section 4.

The data used by Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024) originates from several linked
administrative registers maintained by Statistics Sweden. They have used the Wealth,
Income and Demographic Registers (which contain annual information on financial
assets, real estate, liabilities, labor and capital income, and household composition) and
the Multi-Generation Register (which enables linkages between individuals and their
biological parents, facilitating intergenerational analysis). Their study is limited to the
1999–2007 period, during which detailed, asset-level wealth data are available for the full
population. In addition, income and demographic variables are observed from 1983 to
2016, allowing for long-run assessments of income potential, labor market attachment,
and background characteristics.

The sample comprises more than 4 million individuals and spans multiple birth cohorts.
During the observation period, Sweden maintained a wealth tax that required detailed and
mandatory asset reporting, including securities, which improves the accuracy of measured
household wealth. To capture intergenerational heterogeneity, individuals are classified
into 108 socio-economic subgroups based on three background characteristics: Parental
wealth background, parental industry sector and parental education level.

This paper focuses on variation by parental wealth background, which is constructed by
ranking parents into three groups according to taxable net wealth when their children are
in early adulthood. The three groups are defined as:

    • Non-rich: This group consists of individuals whose parents were not subject to the
        wealth tax (approximately 91% of the sample).
   • Rich: This category includes individuals whose parents were among the 75%
        lowest wealth-tax-paying households (approximately 6.8% of the sample).
    • Very-rich: This group comprises individuals whose parents fell within the top
        25% of wealth-tax-paying households (approximately 2.2% of the sample).

The estimation of behavioral preferences is conducted using a minimum distance
estimation approach. The authors simulate life-cycle behavior (savings, consumption, and
investment) under different values of the preference parameters and select the set that
minimizes the distance between simulated outcomes and observed group-level moments.
The empirical moments used for identification include:

                                   7
    • The average share of risky assets in household portfolios, used to identify risk
         aversion.
    • The consumption-to-wealth ratio in early adulthood, used to identify the time
         discount factor.
    • The consumption-to-wealth ratio in later adulthood, used to identify the strength
       of bequest motives.

thoroughly documented and designed to ensure internal consistency between the
structural model and the observed data.

One limitation of relying on summarized data moments is that it constrains the scope for
model validation and sensitivity analysis. Specifically, we cannot perform robustness
checks based on alternative specifications or subsamples. However, this limitation is
mitigated by the breadth and precision of the underlying data, as well as the
methodological rigor of the estimation procedure. By running simulations on these
empirically grounded parameters, we aim to capture key features of wealth dynamics and
generate policy-relevant insights on intergenerational mobility.

In our simulation model, individuals make forward-looking decisions based on their
expectations about the future. The vector of expected excess returns (μ) on different risky
asset classes is a key parameter influencing their optimal portfolio choices and,
consequently, their consumption-saving decisions (at). However, the actual wealth
accumulated in any given period depends on the realized aggregate excess return (ert) for
that period, which reflects the actual market performance above the risk-free rate and
includes unpredictable shocks. Thus, while μ guides optimal behavior, ert (further
individualized with idiosyncratic shocks in our simulation) drives the period-by-period
changes in wealth. For the complete mathematical derivations of these relationships and
the underlying continuous-time model, readers are referred to Ebrahimian and Sodini
(2024, particularly Sections 2.5, 4, and Appendix B).

The model categorizes the population into three socioeconomic groups: Non-rich, Rich,

the number of individuals in that group, scaled to reflect millions of individuals. These
group weights are used to simulate many individual agents within each group, thereby
capturing heterogeneity and preserving representativeness of the Swedish wealth
distribution.

As Bastani and Waldenström (2023) discuss, it is unrealistic to assume that 100% of an

5. Results

This section presents the outcomes of the simulation model introduced above. The
analysis focuses on average final wealth levels, absolute wealth gaps between groups, and
the extent to which wealth taxes affect these. All results are reported for the three
population groups, Non-Rich, Rich, and Very Rich, over a fixed time horizon of 25 years
and across tax rates ranging from 0% to 5%.

The central finding of our simulation is that increasing the wealth tax rate leads to a non-
trivial reduction in the final wealth gap between socioeconomic groups. As illustrated in
Figure 1, the mean absolute wealth gap between the Very Rich and the Non-Rich declines
from approximately 4.7 MSEK to just under 3.5 MSEK as the tax rate increases from 0%
to 5%. Similarly, the Rich–Non-Rich gap falls slightly from above 1 MSEK to just under
1 MSEK. These trends are smooth and monotonic across all group comparisons,
reflecting a clear inverse relationship between the wealth tax rate and overall wealth
dispersion at the top of the distribution. A vertical reference line at 1.5% marks Sweden’s
pre-2007 wealth tax level. Notably, even at this relatively moderate rate, the absolute
wealth gaps between groups are already reduced. For illustrative visuals of the wealth gap
on a relative basis, see Figures A1 and A2 in the appendix.

threshold of 1.5 MSEK, as their average wealth remains well below this cutoff across all
tax rates. This insulates them from the impact of the policy and explains why their average

final wealth remains virtually unchanged regardless of the level of taxation. In contrast,
the Rich group accumulates average wealth levels narrowly below the threshold, hovering
between 1.2 and 1.3 MSEK. Although they are close to the threshold, their exposure to
the tax remains limited, resulting in only a modest decline in accumulated wealth.

Furthermore, our model indicates that even with a flat wealth tax applied above 1.5
MSEK and set at a rate of 1.5%, wealth gaps between groups continue to diverge over
time. However, as demonstrated in Appendix Figure A3, the pace of divergence is
significantly slower under higher tax regimes. This pattern points to a clear moderating
effect of tax policy on the long-run concentration of wealth at the top.

A closer look at Figure 4 reveals that in year 0, all three groups begin with negative net
wealth due to initial indebtedness. This reflects realistic starting conditions, where
individuals often incur debt early in life, such as student loans or mortgages. Interestingly,
the Rich group starts with a lower net wealth than the Non-rich. One possible explanation
is that the Rich have greater expected future income, which enables them to take on more
debt to invest in education, housing, or other forms of human capital, but this would
require further investigation to be confirmed.

While the analysis above primarily focuses on the effects of wealth taxation on absolute
wealth levels and group-level disparities, the simulated wealth trajectories also offer
insight into economic mobility over the life cycle. Specifically, the results suggest that

                                  17
although the wealth tax compresses the absolute gap between groups, it does not
substantially alter the relative positioning of individuals across the distribution over time.

The Very Rich continue to accumulate wealth at a significantly faster pace than both the
Rich and the Non-Rich, even under higher tax rates. For an overview of outcomes under
varying tax levels, see Figure A3 in the appendix. This reflects a low degree of upward
mobility among lower-wealth groups, as the gap in final outcomes persists despite policy
interventions. The Non-Rich, in particular, see virtually no improvement in their average
final wealth, indicating limited opportunity for meaningful economic advancement within
the simulated time horizon.

These patterns are closely tied to the behavioral parameters embedded in the model. The
differences in risk aversion (γ), bequest motives (χ) and time preference (ρ) play a key
role in shaping long-run accumulation, strengthening the results of Ebrahimian and Sodini
(2024). Individuals with lower risk aversion, greater bequest motives and patience, traits
concentrated among the Very Rich in the simulation, are more likely to benefit from
compounding returns over time. As a result, wealth positions tend to remain stable,
reinforcing persistence at the top and limiting catch-up dynamics for those below.

Taken together, the results point to a broader implication: while wealth taxation above a
threshold can slow the pace of wealth concentration and reduce disparities in absolute
terms, it does not, on its own, generate high levels of mobility across the wealth
distribution. Additional mechanisms, such as policies aimed at improving access to
capital, education, or investment opportunities, may be necessary to facilitate more
dynamic movement between economic positions over time.

6. Extensions and Robustness

In this section, we outline the implications of extending the model by focusing on
counterfactual scenarios involving parameter and threshold variations. Drawing from the
findings of Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024), wealth accumulation is influenced not only by
the level of inherited wealth but also by inherited behavior. Accordingly, we test the
elasticity of the wealth gap in response to wealth taxes across different socioeconomic
groups, applying the logic that "less favorable" accumulation conditions, i.e., Non-Rich
group parameter values may alter the effects for especially the Very-Rich category.

6.1. Counterfactual Parameters

As shown in Table 4, setting the risk aversion parameter (γ) to the Non-Rich group's level
results in a substantially smaller wealth gap elasticity. Specifically, we observe a wealth
gap elasticity of 2.9%, meaning the wealth gap decreases by 2.9% following a one
percentage point increase in the wealth tax, compared to a 6.9% reduction in the base
case. This suggests that higher risk aversion among the Very Rich reduces the potential
for wealth accumulation, thus compressing the overall wealth gap and limiting the
marginal effect of taxation.

Turning to the bequest motive parameter (χ), we observe a similar but less pronounced
effect. When setting bequest motive to Non-Rich level for all wealth groups we observe
a decrease in the relative wealth gap elasticity between Very Rich-Non-Rich to 5.1%
compared to the benchmark value of 6.2%. The wealth tax still yields a marginal impact
on the wealth gap when bequest motives are aligned across groups. A comparable pattern
is seen for the time preference parameter (ρ): in the counterfactual scenario with uniform
time preference parameter, wealth taxes continue to have a non-trivial effect. Here, the
elasticity of the wealth gap between the Very Rich and Non-Rich is 4% per percentage
point increase in wealth tax.

Finally, when all parameters are simultaneously set to Non-Rich levels, we find a minimal
effect of wealth taxes on the gap between the Very Rich and the Non-Rich. In this
scenario, the elasticity drops to just 0.2%, underscoring the critical role of behavioral
parameters in shaping wealth inequality. These findings reinforce the conclusions of
Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024) regarding the importance of inherited behavior in driving
wealth dynamics.

This highlights the role of behavioral parameters in driving heterogeneity in the wealth
accumulation process across groups. In particular, the risk aversion parameter, γ, plays a
central role in shaping wealth trajectories. When combined with the intertemporal
preference parameter, ρ, these factors lead to more pronounced fluctuations in wealth
among the Very Rich. This group exhibits both higher risk tolerance and greater patience,
enabling them to pursue more aggressive investment strategies and defer consumption,
which in turn results in a steeper accumulation path. These dynamics align with the
findings of Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024), who emphasize the importance of
heterogeneous investment behavior in explaining long-term wealth mobility.

     6.3. Robustness test

      To reinforce our results, we conduct a robustness test on the calibrated parameter in our
      extended model, σ, representing shocks in excess returns. By testing various values of σ,
     we anticipate similar trends, which are confirmed in Table 6. Our baseline model uses σ
      = 0.2. As expected, the results show that the lower the value of σ, the smaller the impact
      of wealth taxes on wealth gap elasticity. This outcome is driven by the reduced presence
      of outliers in the right tail of the wealth distribution, which are otherwise
      disproportionately affected by higher taxes. The effect becomes especially clear in the
      extreme case of σ = 1, a value that is not realistic but used here for illustrative purposes
      to highlight the trend. As can be observed in Figure A4 the relative developments of
      wealth gaps follow the same overall trend as our original model no matter the adjustments
      to σ. This in turn indicates that our model is robust across a wide range of return shock
      assumptions. While the magnitude of the tax impact varies with σ, the relationship
      between tax policy and wealth concentration remains consistent. This supports the
      internal validity of our findings and suggests that our conclusions regarding the role of
      taxation in shaping intergenerational wealth mobility are not unduly sensitive to the
      assumed level of return volatility.

     6.4. Further Research

     A promising avenue for future research involves refining the simulation framework to
      distinguish more clearly between total wealth and taxable wealth. This could be achieved
      by incorporating estimated discounts or exemptions based on historical Swedish wealth
      tax rules. Drawing on comparative data from countries with existing wealth taxation, such
      as Norway (Thoresen et al., 2022), researchers could approximate a more realistic taxable
      wealth base.

                                       22
Introducing this distinction would likely reduce the quantitative magnitude of simulated
wealth tax effects, as the tax would apply to a narrower subset of total wealth. However,
the qualitative direction of the results is expected to remain stable. Even when applied to
a reduced base, wealth taxes would continue to primarily affect high-wealth individuals.
As such, the core finding, that wealth taxation contributes to lowering wealth inequality,
would likely hold.

Another interesting direction for future research would be to simulate a broader set of tax
policies to compare their effects. This could include real estate taxes, inheritance taxes,
and various forms of progressive income taxation. Additionally, exploring combinations
of tax policies, such as lowering income taxes while increasing capital or wealth taxes,
could provide valuable insights into the trade-offs and synergies of different fiscal
strategies.

7. Conclusion

We conclude that wealth taxes have a non-trivial effect on intergenerational wealth
accumulation and can contribute to increased economic mobility. However, this effect is
significantly determined by key behavioral parameters (risk aversion, time preference,
and bequest motives) which shape the underlying dynamics of wealth accumulation.
While wealth can be inherited, the persistence of wealth across generations is primarily
driven by the behavioral characteristics of heirs.

From a policy perspective, this suggests that although wealth taxation can play a role in
increasing mobility, it is not a comprehensive solution. More impactful long-term
outcomes may be achieved by investing in human capital and the knowledge that
facilitates wealth creation. Rather than solely focusing on reducing the wealth of the
richest, policies that empower the Non-Rich to accumulate wealth more effectively may
offer a sustainable path toward greater mobility. That said, implementing such measures
poses its own set of complex challenges.

In light of the research conducted prior to this paper, our findings align with and further
strengthen the conclusions of Ebrahimian and Sodini (2024), particularly their conclusion
that: ”it is not just about the money that individuals receive from their parents, but also
what they do with it... ”

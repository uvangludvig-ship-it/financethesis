     The Index Effect in an Isolated Environment

          – A Review of the Market Cap Segment Changes on the Nordic Exchanges

                                David Persson*, Jacob Wiström**

Abstract

Since 2006, the NASDAQ OMX Nordic Exchanges are divided into three segments based on market capitalization:
Small-, Mid- and Large Cap. This paper examines the up- and downgrades between these segments in the
framework of the index effect, that is, abnormal returns in connection with index inclusions or exclusions. The
nature of the market cap segments invalidates some commonly proposed explanations for the index effect, such as
price pressure and information signaling. This enables investigation of other proposed theories in an isolated setting.
To overcome clustering, a portfolio approach is applied using the event study dummy variable model. Significant
and permanent positive price effects are found for the upgrades, especially for the Small Cap to Mid Cap upgrades
where a mean CAR of 14.7% is reported. Abnormal volumes are found for all types of changes. These effects are in
line with the awareness hypothesis, suggesting upgraded firms experience increased market values thanks to more
investors becoming aware of the stock. This also explains why effects are larger for the smaller companies upgraded
– since smaller companies have an initially smaller investor base they gain relatively more from the increased
investor awareness.

Keywords: index effect, index changes, awareness hypothesis, information cost hypothesis, liquidity hypothesis,
abnormal returns, liquidity, event study, clustering, NASDAQ OMX Nordic

Since the creation of the NASDAQ OMX Nordic Exchanges in 2006, Swedish, Finnish, Danish, and
Icelandic companies are jointly presented in three different market cap segments based on total market
capitalization. These segments each form a separate index, but the most attention these segments get is
not in the form of indices. Rather, the higher segments are an important marker for larger, more stable
companies, and the segmentation also decides how the stocks are presented in for example the business
press. Companies are regularly reviewed based on public, non-discretionary market capitalization criteria,
and if a company exceed or fall below these certain thresholds, it is either up- or downgraded to a higher
or lower market cap segment. This differs from other frequently studied indices such as the S&P 500,
which are reviewed for inclusions or exclusions on ambiguous, sometimes non-public criteria. The main
difference to regular indices is however the market cap segments’ non-investability, which has meant they
are not at all tracked by index funds (Hage and Karlsson, 2013). These characterstics caught our attention;
what are the effects for individual stocks of switching from one market cap segment to another? Are the
effects similar to those observed on regular indices or can aberrant effects be found?
        These effects are also interesting in a broader scope as part of the studies of the ‘index effect’ –
the widely studied topic investigating why abnormal returns are consistently found around index
composition changes.
        Our study contributes with its unique opportunity to study some of the proposed explanations for
the index effect in an isolated setting. Due to the nature of the market cap segments (the public, automatic
inclusion criteria and the absence of index tracking) we are able to study the awareness and information
cost / liquidity hypotheses as explanations of the index effect isolated from the often proposed theories
based on information signaling due to ambiguous inclusion criteria and price pressure due to index
tracking. The index effect is on an even grander scale interesting to study in the framework of the
Efficient-Market Hypothesis (EMH). If an index inclusion is regarded as an information free event, the
abnormal returns associated with it might constitute a violation of the EMH since prices should only react
to new information.

3. Previous literature

3.1. Theoretical framework

Studies of the index effect go a long way back. The first documented effects were published by Shleifer
(1986) and Harris and Gurel (1986), both finding positive price effects of stocks included into the S&P
500. Since then, the finance community has debated the index effect through numerous new papers. The
effect was subsequently researched through new studies of the S&P 500 but eventually the studies
expanded to European and Asian indices, some proposing new explanations and theories for the findings
and some supporting old ones with new research. A definite conclusion is yet to be found and it is
doubtful one will ever be – more likely is that the index effect can be explained by a variety of factors
depending on time and nature of the index.
        To discuss the index effect in a broader theoretical framework, we have to go further back than
1986. In 1970, Fama refined and presented the widely supported and discussed Efficient-Market
Hypothesis (EMH), which in its semi-strong form states that current stock prices reflect all current
publicly available information. If this is true, we should not see a price effect at all of a stock index
inclusion or exclusion, given that no new information is conveyed to the market with the index addition
(deletion). So why, then, do we consistently find abnormal returns in connection with index revisions?
One theory which explains the index effect without violating the EMH is the information signaling
hypothesis, brought forward initially by Mikkelson (1981) and Harris and Raviv (1985) and applied to the
index effect by Jain (1987) and Dhillon and Johnson (1991). This theory states that the index inclusion
(exclusion) actually does convey new information about the firm to the market. In the latter cases the
information relates to the scrutiny of Standard & Poor’s when making index selections – the authors
propose that Standard & Poor’s have access to information not publicly available. Depending on the index
administrators and their inclusion criteria, different types of information could possibly be conveyed.
        Two related, often proposed hypotheses do however discard the EMH: the imperfect substitutes
hypothesis and price pressure hypothesis. The EMH requires that investors consider that there are close

                                                                                                       6
substitutes for all the stocks on the stock market. This means a temporary or permanent change in demand
or supply should not affect the prices of stocks, and that the demand curve is almost perfectly elastic
(horizontal). Otherwise the EMH would not hold, since the prices of stocks would then change without
any new information having been presented about the stock.

                                    9
       The imperfect substitutes  hypothesis was discussed by Kraus and Stoll (1972). It holds that
investors actually do not consider stocks as close substitutes to one another, and hence the demand curves
for the stocks slope downwards in the long run. This means that an increase in demand for a stock
(following for example an index inclusion and fund managers subsequent need to rebalance index funds
or funds benchmarked against the index) will increase the price of the stock as the increased demand
changes the equilibrium to a new, permanently higher price. This theory was applied to the index effect
by Shleifer (1986) in his above mentioned study of the S&P 500, and explained the permanent price
appreciations he found. In terms of volume changes, the theory is however inconclusive.
       The price pressure hypothesis was discussed by Scholes (1972) and supported by Harris and
Gurel (1986). It is related to the imperfect substitutes hypothesis in the fact that it also argues that demand
changes can affect the prices of stocks, although in a slightly different way. According to the price
pressure hypothesis, long-term demand for stocks is perfectly elastic but in the short-term the demand

                                             10
curve is actually downward sloping  . This is because investors on the receiving end of demand- or supply
increases need to be compensated for transaction costs and portfolio risks inferred by sales or purchases
they otherwise would not have made. Hence, these investors will sell at temporary higher prices and buy
at temporary lower prices. In the long-term, prices will return to the “correct” full-information prices and
thus said investors will have been compensated with excess return. During this temporary demand shift,
volumes are expected to increase.
        Another relevant theory, the information cost / liquidity hypothesis, was introduced by Barry and
Brown (1985) and applied to the index effect by Beneish and Gardner (1995). It is stated that with an
index exclusion comes decreased liquidity and it is argued that investors demand higher expected returns
for stocks with less liquidity and less available information. This is due to the fact that the less liquid
stocks will incur more trading costs for the investor (because of higher bid-ask spreads), and stocks with
less available information are seen as riskier. There are also greater costs involved with acquiring the
required information for these stocks, which further justifies the higher expected returns. Stocks included
into an index, on the other hand, will be followed by financial journalists and analysts to a higher degree

       A related theory, the awareness hypothesis, is based on work by Merton (1987), who developed a
model for market equilibrium with incomplete information. Merton argues and incorporates into the
model that investors do not have knowledge about all available stocks on the market due to various
information costs. The model establishes that expected returns for better-known firms, holding everything
else constant, is lower. The model is based on the fact that initially each investor is only aware of a subset
of stocks. Companies which are held by investors not completely diversified (because they are not aware
of all stocks) bear a shadow cost. This shadow cost represent the extra return demanded by investors for
not being completely diversified (and thereby holding idiosyncratic risk). As a company increases its
investor base, the new investors will be more diversified, and will thus demand less of a premium for not
being completely diversified, hence the shadow cost falls. By increasing the knowledge of the firm and
thereby increasing its potential investor base, a company can decrease its shadow cost and thus lower its
cost of capital and increase its market value. Merton also establishes that this increase in knowledge could
happen in ways outside control of the company, such as through publicity in a frequently read newspaper.
Polonchek and Krehbiel (1995) apply this theory to the index effect by studying and comparing the Dow
Jones Industrial Average (DJIA) and the Dow Jones Transportation Average (DJTA). None of them are
followed by index funds, but the DJIA receives much more media attention than the DJTA. In line with
Merton’s model, they find abnormal returns only for the DJIA inclusions. Chen et al. (2004) also support
the hypothesis; they find effects from inclusions but not from exclusions on the S&P 500. This
asymmetric finding is in line with the hypothesis since investors will not “forget” excluded stocks once
included. For the same reason, the investor base will increase permanently following inclusions.
Therefore, no negative effects should be seen for exclusions and the effects from inclusions should be
permanent. Merton does not make any assumption regarding trading volumes, but one could expect an
increased investor base should lead to increased trading, at least temporarily.

3.2.1. Small Cap literature
As our study is a unique opportunity to study the index effect on small companies compared to the effect
on larger companies, a few papers documenting the index effect on Small Cap indices are relevant. The
first major study on the index effect on smaller companies was done by Biktimirov et al. (2004), where
they study the effects of additions and deletions to the American Small Cap Index Russell 2000. They
distinguish between “pure” additions and deletions and those taking place as upgrades to or downgrades
from the Russell 1000 index (next in line upwards in the index-hierarchy). The Russell indices are more
similar to the indices in our study than the S&P 500 since they are based on market capitalization, and not
discretionary selection. For the sample of pure additions (deletions), they find highly significant positive
(negative) abnormal returns of about 5% in the period up to and including the effective date, but these
effects are effectively reversed in the following days. These results thus give support for the price
pressure hypothesis. Interesting for us is however the slightly differing effects they find for the additions
and deletions taking place as up- or downgrades from the index above in the hierarchy. For these stocks
they find smaller (CARs of about 3% and –3% for stocks shifting index upwards and downwards,
respectively) but permanent effects, albeit at slightly lower significance, possibly because of a smaller
sample. For all stocks they document significant positive abnormal volumes around the event date and for
the stocks shifting index upwards they found positive abnormal volumes for all examined days.
        Shankar and Miller (2006) also investigate the index effect on Small Cap stocks, but on the S&P
SmallCap 600 index. The S&P SmallCap 600 differs from the Russell 2000 in that it is more seldomly
used as a benchmark or for index tracking, which the Russell 2000 frequently is. The paper finds effects
largely consistent with the price pressure hypothesis (temporary abnormal returns and volumes, reversed
in the period after the event) and also notes that price effects are larger for smaller firms in the sample.
        A recent study by Becker-Blease and Paul (2010) study additions to both the S&P 600 SmallCap
and the S&P 400 MidCap indices. They find a permanent value increase of 2% and 3% for SmallCap and
MidCap additions, respectively. They also attempt to explain these price appreciations by looking at
proxies for liquidity, capital intensity and investor awareness. They find the primary explanation to be an
increased liquidity in the stocks, which they also connect to increased capital intensity as investment
opportunities increase with the lowered cost of capital that follows increased liquidity (from lower
expected returns). They also find investor recognition to be an explanatory factor, and also here link it to

an increased capital intensity  . In line with Merton (1987), they argue increased investor recognition
leads to lowered cost of capital, which increases investment opportunities and the capital intensity.

3.2.2. Index tracking literature
We are focusing on market cap segments and indices which are explicitly non-investable and not tracked
by index funds. Therefore, it is relevant to look at some important articles concerning the price pressure
from institutional investors in connection with index revisions, an over the years much cited explanation

                                           13
for the index effect. Already Shleifer   (1986) and Harris and Gurel (1986) attributed the price
appreciations of the index effect to the purchases of index funds trying to replicate the performance of the
S&P 500. This is further confirmed by Pruitt and Wei (1989), who show a positive correlation between
changes in institutional holdings and index additions and deletions. Shleifer also links the over the years
increasing index effect to the growth of index funds.
       As already mentioned, a highly relevant paper is Beneish and Garnder (1995), who study the
Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJIA). They find no price effects for additions to the index, only negative
effects for deletions. They explain these results by a lack of fund rebalancing, stating that because index

                                                            14
funds mimic the S&P 500 and not the DJIA , both the price pressure and the imperfect substitutes
hypotheses are unlikely explanations. Finding a decline in liquidity and available information for deleted
firms, Beneish and Gardner instead argue for the information cost / liquidity hypothesis.

4. Expected findings and hypotheses

4.1. Expected findings

As established, the indices of the market cap segments which we study are not tracked by index or mutual
funds, at least not to any significant extent. Because of this, we do not expect to find indications of the

                                                   15
price pressure hypothesis in our results . For the same reason, we do not expect to see evidence for the
imperfect substitutes hypothesis. Another theory we can rule out is the information signaling hypothesis,
since the selection criteria for the market cap segments are publicly specified and based on information
accessible to all market participants. This means no new information is revealed through the
announcement of the changes, in fact, changes can be anticipated even before the announcements.
       Instead, we expect to find results in line with the awareness hypothesis. As discussed, the main
implication of a market cap segment change is medial presentation and increased or decreased exposure;
Large Cap (and Mid Cap) stocks are more frequently featured in the media and an upgrade is seen as a
sign things are going well for the company. For stocks upgraded to a higher market cap segment, we also

4.2. Hypotheses

From our review of the theoretical framework and the related literature, we have developed five testable
hypotheses. These will be used to interpret the results from our study, and help us to distinguish between
the different theories proposed in the previous literature and to see if the results match our expected
findings.
        Initially, we need to establish that there actually is some form of index effect in connection with
the market cap segment changes on the Nordic Exchanges. The most basic level of index effect, and the
basis for all proposed explanations to it, are the price appreciations in connection with index inclusions.
Given the special characteristics of the market cap segments, we can discard some of the previously
proposed theories with high confidence. This means simply establishing the effect for the market cap
segments would have significant implications. Therefore, our first hypothesis is:

        H1: There are positive abnormal returns for stocks upgraded from one market cap segment to
        another.

When it comes to exclusions, the two theories we intend to test differ slightly. The awareness hypothesis
suggest we should not see any price depreciations following downgrades, whilst the information cost /
liquidity hypothesis states we should. Thus we split our second testable hypothesis into two alternative
hypotheses:

        H2A: There are negative abnormal returns for stocks downgraded from one market cap segment
        to another.

       H2B: There are no negative abnormal returns for stocks downgraded from one market cap
        segment to another.

Of importance in differentiating between the previously proposed theories is the longevity of the price
effects. Are they transitory or permanent? This is also interesting for institutional and private investors on
the Nordic Stock Exchanges contemplating on how to handle and possibly exploit the index effect. Since

                                                                                                     11
we expect findings in line with the awareness and the information cost / liquidity hypotheses, we expect
any effects to be permanent for both upgrades and downgrades (since the awareness hypothesis does not
implicate any effects for downgrades, our expected finding for longevity on downgrade-effects is based
on the information cost / liquidity hypothesis). Thus our third hypothesis is:

       H3: There are no price reversals in the periods after the effective date of stock up- or
       downgrades.

Our study represents a unique opportunity to check for differences in effects between small and larger
firms. As we suggest the awareness hypothesis as a significant explanatory theory, we expect price
effects to be larger for the smaller firms, since larger firms are more likely to already have been noticed

                                                                                      16
by investors. Such an effect is noticed by Shankar and Miller (2006)  . This leads to our forth hypothesis:

        H4: Price effects are larger for Small Cap to Mid Cap upgrades than for Mid Cap to Large Cap
       upgrades.

To further distinguish between the previously proposed theories and to see what effects are taking place in
our sample, we also want to look at volume effects associated with the market cap segment changes. As
the awareness hypothesis does not make explicit predictions about trading volume (although one might
assume a slight increase in trading volume for upgrades from the hypothesis), we base our hypothesis on
the implications of the information cost / liquidity hypothesis. This means our fifth and final hypothesis
is:

        H5: There are permanent positive abnormal trading volumes for stocks upgraded and permanent
        negative abnormal trading volumes for stocks downgraded.

bias. Analogous with the reasoning in Mase (2007), stocks upgraded to a higher market cap segment are
likely to have achieved relatively superior return in the period before being upgraded, whereas the reverse
would apply for stocks being downgraded. Given a potential selection bias, the model parameters
estimates would not be independent of the event itself using a pre-event estimation window. Because the
independence assumption therefore would not hold, using a pre-event estimation window would cause a
bias in the estimated abnormal returns.

days E+31 to E+210, similar to Denis et al. (2003) and Mase (2007). The length of the estimation is
window is in line with most other studies, which uses about half a trading year as their estimation window

6.1. Price effects for stocks upgraded to a higher market cap segment

7. Discussion

7.1 Hypotheses discussion

From our achieved results we will now go through our hypotheses to see if they match our expected
findings. If they do not, we will attempt to explain why.
        To establish that there is some kind of index effect in connection with the market cap segment
changes on the Nordic Exchanges, we start with the first hypothesis H1:“There are positive abnormal
returns for stocks upgraded from one market cap segment to another”. Both the results presented in the
graphs and the tables confirm considerable index effects are present in our sample, in fact our results are
very strong with high abnormal returns for both types of upgrades. For the upgrades from Small Cap to
Mid Cap, the results are exceptionally strong with high coefficients at high significances. These results
are fully consistent with an index effect, and we can conclude that despite the uniqueness of the market
cap segments compared to regular indices, they do exhibit strong signs of an index effect.
       Next, we consider our second, divided hypothesis H2A(B): “There are (no) negative abnormal
returns for stocks downgraded from one market cap segment to another”. Here, results are more
inconclusive; effects are smaller, contradictive, and less significant. As the results in this case differ
between the graphs and the portfolio-based tables, we follow the results presented in the tables, as those
are the results which are most statistically relevant. In the portfolio study (see Table 7), the results differ
considerably between the Mid Cap to Small Cap- and the Large Cap to Mid Cap downgrades. For the Mid
Cap to Small Cap downgrades, we do find negative abnormal returns for the period before and around the
segment downgrades, supporting our H2A hypothesis. For the Large Cap to Mid Cap-downgrades,
however, the CARs are more ambiguous in the period before the changes. For these changes, we instead
observe a very significant positive abnormal return around the effective date. This effect is contrary to our
H2A hypothesis and a result which none of the theories in the previous literature can explain. One could
speculate that since the effect is only seen for Large Cap to Mid Cap downgrades, it might be explained
by some funds which are indexing or benchmarking against specifically the Mid Cap segment. If there
were any such funds, the effect could be expected to be considerable since stocks downgraded to the Mid
Cap will immediately constitute a relatively large part of a value-weighted Mid Cap index. We have no

                                                                                                     33
indication there are any considerable amount of such funds, but there could definitely be private funds
with such strategies active on the Nordic Exchanges. There is thus no clear evidence for either H2A or
H2B, although our sample of Mid Cap to Small Cap downgrades support the H2A hypothesis.
        We continue by looking at the longevity of the results, using our third hypothesis H3 as starting
point: “There are no price reversals in the periods after the effective date of stock up- or downgrades”. If
we begin by looking at the upgrades, we can confirm the hypothesis; the price effects are permanent. For
the Small Cap to Mid Cap upgrades, we barely find any negative abnormal returns at any point
throughout the event window. For the Mid Cap to Large Cap upgrades, the indicative graph (Figure 3)
hints of a slight reversal in prices. However, examining the corresponding table (Table 6), we find these
effects to be very small and insignificant. For the downgrades, we see strong and significant positive
abnormal returns for the period after the effective date. For the Mid Cap to Small Cap downgrades,
considering the negative abnormal returns in the period before the effective date, this means there are
considerable reversal effects (although not complete). For the Large Cap to Mid Cap downgrades, this
cannot be classified as reversals however, since there in this case were no negative effects to begin with.
Thus we can only confirm the hypothesis for the upgrade-effects. For the downgrades, we see signs of
reversals. Such reversals are usually associated with a price pressure effect, but given the mentioned lack
of indexing to the market cap segments this is not a satisfactory explanation for the effects, especially
since we do not experience any reversals for the upgrades.
        Moving on to our forth hypothesis H4, we consider effect differences between small and larger
firms: “Price effects are larger for Small Cap to Mid Cap upgrades than for Mid Cap to Large Cap
upgrades”. This hypothesis is confirmed both in the indicative graphs and from the more statistically
relevant tables. Throughout the event window, with only slight exceptions, the CARs are larger for the
Small Cap to Mid Cap upgrades. This means our hypothesis can be confirmed. We consider the difference
in magnitude of effects to be a strong indicator of the awareness hypothesis. This is because we assume
the relative increase of a stock’s investor base due to attention to be higher for small stocks, as their
investor base is initially much smaller. Thus small stocks would be relatively more favored by attention
stemming from market cap segment upgrades than larger companies.
       For the volume study we look at the results with the fifth hypothesis H5 as starting point: “There
are permanent positive abnormal trading volumes for stocks upgraded and permanent negative abnormal
trading volumes for stocks downgraded”. To begin, we look at the volumes around the upgrades. Here,
the results are consistent across the indicative graphs and the portfolio-based tables. Overall, for both
types of upgrades, we see that there are positive abnormal volumes averaging about 14% over the
complete event window. The effects however seem to be decreasing toward the end of the event window
and are actually negative (although insignificantly so) for the Small Cap to Mid Cap upgrades, signaling

                                                                                                     34
that they are not permanent. For the downgrades, the effects differ between the Mid Cap to Small Cap
downgrades and the Large Cap to Mid Cap downgrades. While both show positive abnormal volumes
over the complete event window, the abnormal volumes are much lower and concentrated before the
effective date for the Large Cap to Mid Cap downgrades. As volumes in this case decrease from the index
downgrade, this finding thus supports the information cost / liquidity hypothesis. For the smaller
companies in the Mid Cap to Small Cap downgrades, the effects are as mentioned different. The highly
positive abnormal volumes are constant throughout the event window, with a huge spike in the interval
between announcement and effective date. These effects cannot be said to be in line with our hypothesis.
Instead, they are more consistent with a price pressure effect due to rebalancing, but again this
explanation is not plausible. Clearly, the evidence supporting our fifth hypothesis is weak and we can
only confirm it for downgrades from Large Cap to Mid Cap.

7.2 Analysis

Overall then, what do our results say about the index effect on the Nordic Exchanges? From Table 10, we
notice the awareness hypothesis is supported in almost all cases. The only exception is hypothesis H2A
regarding price effects for downgrades. Here, our results are not in line with the awareness hypothesis,
since we for the Mid Cap to Small Cap downgrades see significant negative abnormal returns in the
period before the event; the awareness hypothesis does not predict any abnormal returns for downgrades,
since investors will not “forget” about a stock just because it is downgraded. Of interest is also the
reported volume effects. While the awareness hypothesis does not explicitly predict any abnormal
volumes, the reported positive abnormal volumes for upgraded stocks are intuitively in line with the
companies gaining a larger investor base.
        When it comes to our other proposed theory, the information cost / liquidity hypothesis, we only
find some support for it, and the arguably most important hypothesis for this theory, H5, is not supported
to a large degree.
       Overall, our study’s results strongly support the awareness hypothesis as being the primary
explanation for the effects we encounter in connection with the market cap segment changes on the
Nordic Exchanges.
        Lastly, it is interesting to note that considerable parts of the results we find are from the period
before the announcement of the changes. This indicates we do have anticipatory trading, which is
intuitive given that the changes actually can be fully anticipated before the announcements.

7.3 Robustness checks

As our results are based on data from three different countries (Sweden, Finland, and Denmark), we want
to check for the possibility that the results differ significantly across the different countries. For this
reason, we have produced individual indicative graphs for the results split by country to check the
robustness of our results. This means we can check how much the results varies and also check for the
possibility that our results are driven by a single country’s results. The graphs (Figure 10-21 and 24-35
for the standard return- and volume study, respectively) are presented in the appendix. Overall, the effects
in both the return- and volume studies are consistent across countries. However, some differing results are
worth mentioning. For the Mid Cap to Large Cap upgrades and Large Cap to Mid Cap downgrades in the
return study, the results for the Finnish stocks differ from the rest. In both cases, Finnish stocks show
weak effects, with CARs close to zero for the complete event window. Swedish and Danish results are
however consistent with the consolidated results. This means the consolidated results are driven by the
Swedish and Danish results for these cases. Since the Finnish results for these cases not are contradictive,
rather they are inconclusive, this is not seen as a large issue. For the Mid Cap to Small Cap downgrades in
the return study, the Swedish and Finnish results are actually more in line with our hypotheses but the
consolidated results seem to be skewed by the Danish results. Again, this is not a major problem for our
results, as the individual results (excluding Denmark) actually match our hypotheses more closely. The
volume results are largely consistent across countries. Given the inherent volatility of trading volumes,
they naturally differ somewhat, but in our opinion not to a degree which hurts our results.

        In total and from what we can conclude, our results thus seem to be robust for the factors we have
checked for.

8. Conclusions and implications

We have studied the market cap segments on the NASDAQ OMX Nordic Exchanges for signs of the
internationally widely reported index effect of abnormal returns around index inclusions and exclusions.
We have found these segment changes to very intriguing to study because of the segments’ special
characteristics compared to regular indices. Our study is unique since it is the first one to our knowledge
to study the index effect on indices which are composed automatically by completely public and non-
discretionary criteria and are also not tracked by index funds. This means our study contributes to existing
literature by studying the awareness and information cost / liquidity hypotheses isolated from the other
hypotheses often proposed for the index effect. The study also provides a great opportunity to study the
difference in effects of relevant hypotheses between larger and smaller companies.
        In our studies of the price effect around the market cap segment changes, we found significant
abnormal returns for the upgrades. The Small Cap to Mid Cap upgrades show the largest and most
remarkable effect. Here, the mean CAR is 14.7% over the complete event window. The effects are also
found to be permanent, as no strong signs of reversals can be found.
        For the downgrades, the price effects are inconclusive; in the period before the event, there are
negative abnormal returns for the Mid Cap to Small Cap downgrades whereas abnormal returns for the
Large Cap to Mid Cap downgrades are ambiguous. However, both types of downgrades exhibit positive

abnormal returns in the period after the event, meaning effects are reversed for the Mid Cap to Small Cap
downgrades. For the Large Cap to Mid Cap downgrades, these post-event effects are harder to explain.
        In our studies of the volume effect around the market cap segment changes, we found significant
abnormal volumes for all types of segment changes. For the upgrades, however, abnormal volumes are
lower after the event, signaling they are not permanent.
        Overall, in the absence of price pressure or imperfect substitutes effects from fund rebalancing
and of information signaling effects by the nature of our studied index, the evidence presented in this
study is in line with the awareness hypothesis. Given the index characteristics, another possible
explanation would have been the information cost / liquidity hypothesis, but our results does not point in
this direction, especially volume effects are inconsistent with this hypothesis. The awareness hypothesis
also provides an excellent explanation for the fact that we find larger effects for the smaller company
upgrades; as smaller companies have an initially smaller investor base they therefore benefit more from
the increase in awareness and thereby experience a greater reduction in Merton’s shadow cost.
        For private investors on the Nordic Exchanges, our findings have some implications. The most
important is that we have potentially identified an anomaly that is possible to exploit. Since the change in
market cap segments can be anticipated well in advance and much of our reported effects actually happen
after the effective date it would be possible to design a trading strategy which over time should
outperform the market. The investor would simply buy shares about to be upgraded at the first trading day
in December (when the average market cap for November can be reliably calculated) and hold them until
the upgrades become effective and the following 30 days. As reported, the positive abnormal returns are
largest for the upgrades of the smaller stocks. Thus a weakness of the strategy is the relatively low
volumes available for trading, which means it might be problematic to exploit the anomaly on a large
scale. Another weakness is the proven possibility that NASDAQ OMX might overrule market cap
segment changes, as they did for the changes that should have taken place in January 2009.
        A further implication of our findings is that private investors should not sell stocks about to be
downgraded – while this might seem a reasonable trading strategy, it is not supported by our results.
        In a broader sense, our findings implicates strong support for the presence of an investor
awareness effect explaining abnormal returns around index inclusions in general. However, it must be
noted that we cannot discard other proposed explanations for the index effect such as price pressure and
information signaling – the market cap segment changes on the Nordic Exchanges is simply not a
satisfactory laboratory to study such effects. Actually such effects may very well also be present on the

                                                                                                  25
Nordic Exchanges, but for the more investable indices such as OMXS30 . Our study does however
present slight evidence against the information cost / liquidity hypothesis. In our case this is a likely

finding given the non-investable character of the market cap segment indices. Thus it should not be taken

9. Further research

Although the topic of index effects is already widely studied, our study provides some ideas for where
further studies could be made. With this study as a starting point, it would be interesting to examine the
slightly different effects across the Nordic countries – are the segment changes given varying degrees of
attention? To further examine the awareness hypothesis and to confirm the effects of investor awareness
on the Nordic Exchanges, we suggest two related topics. First, it would be interesting to separate stocks
being upgraded to a higher segment for the first time from “returners”. Following the awareness
hypothesis, we would expect to see stronger effects for “first-timers”. Second, it would be interesting to
conduct a more extensive study where changes in Merton’s shadow cost are computed for stocks
changing segments, similar to the study performed by Chen et al. (2004). It would also be interesting to
use another measure for studying the liquidity effects. Here, bid-ask spreads seem to be the most suitable
and intuitive measure to use as a proxy for stock liquidity.
        In a broader perspective, it would be interesting to study similar market cap segments (or other
indices with comparable characteristics) on other stock exchanges than the Nordic ones, and possibly over
a longer time span if available, to see if the results are consistent with our findings.

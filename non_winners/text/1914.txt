                             Abstract

In this thesis, return data on the US stock and corporate bond markets together with gold and WTI-
Crude Oil prices over the period 1987 – 2012 were used to examine the time-varying correlations
and benefits from cross-asset class diversification. It concludes that correlations vary distinctly over
time and an all-equity investor benefit from having positions in the bond market, as investment
grade bonds have shown a generally low or negative correlation with the stock market during crises.
For an investor, it could therefore be costly to ignore the benefits that the bond markets provide.
Gold is shown to be a good source for diversification during severe financial crises, even if the
volatility in returns are higher than for investment grade bonds.

1. Introduction
The idea of diversification is an old one and the phrase “don’t put all your eggs in one basket”
existed long before modern finance theory. The principles of diversification in the financial markets
was first published by Markowitz (1952) and earned him the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1990.
According to Modern Portfolio Theory (MPT), an investor is only concerned about risk-return
relationships, and that by diversifying into assets with a less than perfect correlation, the overall risk
of the portfolio will be lower. However this theory rest on the assumption that correlation is in fact
constant, leading to possible disasters from an investment management perspective if changes in
correlation are not taken into consideration (Bodie et al 2008).
       Several studies have been conducted to examine the changes in correlation between
international stock markets over time and during extreme conditions, see Longin and Solnik (2001)
and Campbell et al (2002). Coaker (2006) analyses how the return correlations between different
asset classes varies over time, but little has been written about how the correlation varies during and
between specific crises, in order to understand how the actual diversification benefits changes and
what the underlying reasons behind these changes are.
        This study takes the viewpoint of an equity investor and analyses the historical benefits of
diversification by investigating how correlations have changed over times of crises, more specifically
the Dotcom-crisis in the early 2000’s, the Subprime-crisis in 2007-08 and the Sovereign debt crisis in
2011-12. The asset classes studied against the stock market are corporate bonds (both investment
grade and high yield bonds) together with two of the most commonly traded commodities: WTI-
Crude Oil and gold.
       To reach the conclusions, a top-down approach will be taken by first confirming previous
research that correlation is in fact time-varying, followed by an analysis on how the different assets’
correlations with the stock market have changed before, during and after the specific crises. This will
be followed by a case study that aims to quantify the benefits of diversification over the past 25
years, linking the correlation changes to investment management. As will be presented in Section 2,
a rational investor is only concerned about returns in relation to its risk, and the risk of a portfolio is
the sum of its covariances. This is in turn directly determined by the assets correlation: An increase
in correlation implies an increased risk. To capture this, the case study compares the Sharpe ratio of
a wide set of different portfolios over time, as it is a function of both the portfolio’s risk and return,
and thus provides an indication of the actual benefits of diversification for investors.

2. Theoretical framework
Bodie et al (2008) outlines the fundamental steps in a portfolio manager’s investment process: (i)
Capital allocation between a risky portfolio and a risk-free asset, (ii) Asset allocation between different
asset classes and (iii) Security selection within each asset class. This process is derived from ‘Modern
Portfolio Theory’ (MPT) that was developed in the 1950s originating with Markowitz (1952), giving
rise for the “Markowitz Portfolio Selection Model” which will be presented in greater detail in this
section, after the readers is provided with an intuitive understanding about the benefits of
diversification. An important point is the fact that the MPT assumes constant correlation between
different assets (Bodie et al, 2008).

3. Previous literature

The vast majority of the literature on how correlations vary over time is focused on the international
stock markets, and how correlations vary conditional on extreme returns. In this section these
findings are presented, along with an extensive cross-asset class correlation study done by Coaker
(2006). To support the case study, an overview of some literature on passive vs. active portfolio
management, performance evaluation and momentum strategy is also be presented, and together the
results from these papers are used to put this thesis into perspective.

3.1 International stock markets correlation
Longin and Solnik (1995) investigated the correlation between international stock markets during the
years 1960 – 1990, using monthly excess return data and found that correlations had generally
increased over time and also that they tended to increase in times of high volatility. This acted as the
basis for their next paper: Longin and Solnik (2001), where they apply a bivariate framework to
study the US stock market separately against four international stock markets during the period 1959
– 1996. They find that correlations are dependent on market trends and not market volatility per se,
and conclude that correlation increases during bear markets, but not during bull markets. To reach this

                                                   1
conclusion the extreme value theory (EVT)  is used to study the dependence structure of the
different equity indices and estimate the conditional correlation on increasing, pre-determined
threshold levels over a multivariate return distribution. These threshold levels are determined as ±X
percentage points away from the empirical mean for each country. Using their approach, as the
threshold levels increases, the correlation of large returns should asymptotically go to zero. They
show that correlation of large negative returns does not converge to zero, but tend to increase with
the threshold level, while for positive returns the correlation decreases and converges to zero as the
threshold levels increases. Thus, the assumption of multivariate normality can be rejected when
returns are negative, but not when returns are positive. This provides evidence that correlation
increases during financial stress.
       Campbell, Koedjick and Kofman (2002) reached a different conclusion and state that even if
correlation is time-varying, it does not imply that correlations depend on the size of market
movements, and that correlation studies conditional on size, such as Longin and Solnik (2001)
presented above, along with Ramchand and Susmel (1998), Boyer, et al. (1997) and Lorethan and
English (2000) will all suffer from a theoretical estimation bias. This bias will arise from the choice

of extreme returns, (i.e. the nature of the conditioning on the size of the joint return) but also on the
assumed underlying joint return distribution. Boyer et al. and Lorethan and English condition their
correlation estimates on one component of the bivariate return distribution, and then estimate the
truncated conditional correlation for bivariate return observations in the cases where one of the
returns exceed a certain threshold level. Campbell et al (2002) criticise this approach, arguing that
truncating a joint return distribution with constant correlation leads to a downward bias for
increasing threshold levels, with the implication of an increase in how often the assumption of
constant correlation can be rejected. Longin and Solnik’s (1995 and 2001) methodology is slightly
different as they only consider the cases where both return series exceed pre-determined threshold
levels, creating a “quadrant” of relevant return observations. When moving further into the bivariate
tail, the conditional correlations will as previously discussed converge to zero. However, Campbell et
al (2002) claim that this approach gives rise to a theoretical bias similar to that of Boyer et al. and
Lorethan and English.
        Instead of adjusting the conditioned correlation estimates for this theoretical bias (from
either a truncated or quadrant conditioning approach) Campbell et al (2002) take a portfolio
managers viewpoint and condition their correlation measure in a way consistent with the portfolio
Value-at-risk (VaR). As described by Linsmeier and Pearson (2000), VaR is a statistical measure of
possible portfolio losses resulting from ‘normal’ market movements, such that the correlation
estimates are conditioned on portfolio returns falling below a pre-specified worst-case portfolio
quantile, instead of being conditioned on the returns on either one or both assets falling below a
particular threshold level. Through the VaR methodology, estimations can be made on the
probability of a portfolio’s return falling below a threshold VaR-return with a specified confidence
level. If constant correlation is assumed over the joint return distribution, the probability of the
portfolio return falling below the VaR-level equals the weighted average of the individual asset’s
probability of falling below the same VaR-level. As confidence level increases, return moves further
into the tails (of the joint portfolio distribution), and the VaR increases. If at the same time, the
correlation between the assets’ return were to increase – the portfolios VaR will exceed the weighted
average VaR of the individual assets, and the correlation changes can be proved. Using the same
equity markets as Longin and Solnik (2001), with more frequent sampling but during a shorter time
period, May 1990 – December 1999, Linsmeier and Pearson (2000) observe an increase in the
conditional quantile correlation during both bull markets and bear markets. This goes against the findings

                                                                                   7
of Longin and Solnik (2001) who could only observe an increased correlation in bear markets. This
indicates that diversification benefits erode when they are needed the most.

3.2 Stock markets’ correlation with bond markets
Coaker (2006) studies the volatility of correlation and its implications for asset allocation decisions
by analysing the simple rolling correlation of 15 different asset classes against the S&P 500 in one-,
three-, five-, and ten-year time series over a 35-year period (1970-2004), using monthly return data.
He also analyses the diversification benefits in years when the S&P 500 declines. He finds that the
correlation of investment grade corporate bonds with the S&P 500 varies distinctly over time, but
that they have provided high diversification benefits. In all of the eight years within the sample
where the S&P 500 declined, the bonds earned positive returns, with a 35-year correlation of 0.23
and a high of 0.57 for any 5-year period. During the lifetime of the high yield index used, the
correlation between high yield bonds and the S&P 500 is 0.50, and out of the four times the S&P
500 has declined the high yield bonds have declined three times. The correlation between the S&P
500 and high yield bonds is generally stronger and less volatile over time than the correlation
between the S&P 500 and investment grade bonds, indicating that there are greater diversification
benefits from investment grade than high yield bonds. This conclusion is consistent with the
findings of Reilly and Wright (2001) who conclude that there are even stronger correlations between
high yield bonds and the S&P 500 than between high yield and investment grade bond markets.
       Following Reilly and Wright (2001) and Coaker (2006) it is shown that investment grade
bonds is a better source for diversification that high yield bonds. This might be explained by the
phenomenon “Flight-to-quality” as studied by Baur and Lucey (2010), which is the flow from stocks
into investment grade bonds when there is turmoil in the financial markets. However the researchers
only studied short periods of up to 20 trading days following a stock market downturn (an example

                             th
being the September 11  terrorist attacks). Together the research implies a potential benefit from
diversification from the bond markets.

3.4 Active vs. passive portfolio management and performance evaluation
William F. Sharpe (1991) discusses the arithmetic of active management and argues that the return
on actively managed portfolios must on average equal the passive portfolio; that is the market
portfolio. This statement can also be scaled down to a particular asset class. The logic is very simple,
and at first two investor types are classified: ‘Passive’ that hold the market portfolio, and ‘Active’
whose holdings differ from the market portfolio. Over any specified time period, the market return
must equal the weighted average return on all securities in the market, and as the passive investor
will obtain precisely that return, it is impossible for active investors in aggregate to deviate from the
market return. As the first two returns are the same, the third must be also. That leads to his first
assertion: “Before costs, the return on the average actively managed dollar will equal the return on
the average passively managed dollar”. This directly implies his second assertion: “After costs, the
return on the average actively managed dollar will be less than the return on the average passively
managed dollar”. Furthermore he suggests that the best way to evaluate the performance of an
active portfolio is by comparing it with a feasible passive alternative.
       In another paper, Sharpe and Perold (1995) examines and compares a set of dynamic
strategies for asset allocation: ‘Buy-and-Hold’, ‘Constant-Mix’, ‘Constant proportion portfolio
insurance’ and ‘Option based portfolio insurance’. This thesis shall focus on the first two strategies.
A ‘Buy-and Hold’ strategy is a passive strategy characterized by an initial mix of assets that are
bought and held without rebalancing, in contrast to the ‘Constant-Mix’ strategy that is active in the
sense that it maintains the same proportion of assets over time. Each strategy’s performance is

                                                                                   9
evaluated in the case of flat markets, bull/bear markets and in the case of volatile/less volatile
markets. A ‘Constant-Mix’ strategy implies selling assets as they appreciate in value, and buying them
as they depreciate, to keep the ratios constant over time. In volatile markets without any trend, this
strategy will outperform as it takes advantage of “selling high and buying low”. However if there is a
bull or a bear market, the ‘Buy-and-Hold’ strategy will be more successful as reversals are small and
relatively infrequent, and the marginal buy or sell calls in the ‘Constant-Mix’ portfolio will often turn
out to be poorly timed. Which strategy is then the best? The answer is that no strategy can be said to
the best for everyone, as it depends on the strategy’s expected payoff compared with the investor’s
risk tolerance and expectations.
        There is much literature on what the appropriate performance evaluation measure is for
predicting portfolio returns: Zakamouline & Koekebakker (2009) acknowledge the common use of
the Sharpe ratio based on the Mean-Variance theory (as presented in Section 2), but also state that it
is a valid prediction measure only when there can be an accurate measurement of the standard deviation
of returns, together with a normal return distribution. It is further discussed how different research
replaces the standard deviation with other measures of the portfolios risk, such as downside
deviation or the concept of VaR. Another example is Forinelli et al (2008) who use different
performance measures accounting for non-normality in returns in order to find the optimal measure
to predict returns.
        For the purpose of the case study: It is not intended to prove the prediction ability of the
Sharpe ratio on portfolio performance, but instead how different portfolios have performed
according to the Sharpe ratio over time. Hence, the Sharpe ratio will not be complemented by other
performance measures. Further there is a distinction made whether the portfolios are ‘Active’ or
‘Passive’, with all ‘Active’ portfolios being of the nature ‘Constant-Mix’ following Sharpe and Perold
(1995).

3.5 Momentum in asset returns
Following the paper by Debondt and Thaler (1985) discussing the stock markets overreaction to
new information, Jagadeesh and Titman (1993) used stock market data to document strong
momentum effects, for example as the winners in the past 6 months outperformed the market by an
average cumulative return of 9.5 % p.a. between the years 1965 – 1989. Three years later, Fama and
French (1996) found support for the fact that short-term winners tend to outperform the market.
An extension of this that is highly relevant to the case study are the results of Blitz and Vliet (2008)
who investigate momentum effects in terms of returns and Sharpe ratios across asset classes. It is

                                                                                  10
claimed that the application of momentum to global tactical asset allocation across 12 asset classes
(including US investment grade and high yield bonds) delivers statistically significant abnormal
returns. It is also shown that a 12 minus 1 month momentum strategy (investing in the best
performing portfolios in the past 12 months excluding the most recent) yields on average a return of
7.9 % p.a. This approach resembles that of the active portfolio in the case study, which is presented
in greater detail in Section 6.4.

5. Hypotheses

This thesis uses a top down approach, where the correlation stability in returns on the S&P 500 and
the bond indices and the return on the S&P 500 and the commodities are first analysed over the
entire sample period, 1986 – 2013, to determine whether the first hypothesis can be rejected:

Hypothesis 1: The correlation in returns between the stock market and the specified asset
classes is constant through 1986 – 2013.

Following a rejection of Hypothesis 1, a closer examination is made of the stability of this
correlation during periods of crisis, as defined in Section 4.5.

 Hypothesis 2: The correlation in returns between the stock market and the specified asset
classes is not affected over crisis periods.

Following Hypothesis 2, the Dotcom and Subprime-crises will be analysed in more detail in order to
investigate if any of the factors defined in Section 4.4 can explain potential changes in correlation
during times of crisis. This leads to the next hypothesis:

Hypothesis 3: The correlation in returns between the stock market and the specified asset
classes is not affected by market liquidity or changes in market volatility.

       These three hypotheses are followed by a case study, which aims to quantify the actual
benefits of cross-asset class diversification during the period 1987 – 2012. As specified, an increase
in the correlation of asset returns will directly increases the risk of the portfolio. From Section 2.1 it
follows that an investor’s objective is to maximize his Sharpe ratio (slope of the CAL) that is returns
in relation to risk. If the return correlations were constant, the Sharpe ratio of different portfolios
would be predictable, and therefore it would be possible to see a clear pattern on when to rebalance
the portfolio. The case study will evaluate the performance of the individual asset classes compared
to a set of active portfolios in terms of their yearly, realized Sharpe ratios, in order to quantify the
historical diversification benefits of the different assets over time in general, and during crises in
particular.

6. Methodology

In this section, how the different hypotheses are tested and how the case study is performed is
presented in greater detail. To get a better understanding of how the different regressions are run, a
description will first be presented of the way in which autocorrelation amongst the error terms was
resolved.

6.1 Newey-West standard errors
As will be presented later in this section, moving and rolling estimation windows are used for the
return correlations, which give rise to the problem of autocorrelation, that is that the correlation in
period N is affected by the correlation in period N-1. A method to reduce this effect was developed
by Newey and West (1987), which uses a positive, semi-definite, heteroskedasticity and
autocorrelation-consistent covariance matrix to reduce the autocorrelation from an ordinary-least-
square (OLS) regression. The OLS-regression rests on several assumptions, which can be partially
resolved by using this alternative approach. The Newey-West standard errors are calculated
dependant on a choice of maximum lag, which is calculated from a distributed lag of the OLS-
residuals, and this is chosen to match the actual window length when performing the tests. The use
of Newey-West standard errors will be robust to arbitrary departures from homoskedasticity, which
is one underlying assumption in OLS-regressions. This will in fact bring higher standard errors to
the regressions; however the results will be more reliable.

6.2 Testing for constant correlation over time, 1986 – 2012
Zivot and Wang (2003) discuss the common use of rolling analysis of time-series data, and assert
that if parameters are truly constant, that the estimates over a fixed size rolling window should then
not be too different. This method is used by Coaker (2006) to study correlation volatility over time,
and will be used in this study. Another method discussed as common in the analysis of financial
time-series data in particular is the use of a moving window (or moving average window), where the
window is symmetric around the particular observation, and where the estimate is the aggregate
correlation within the window. To reach a stronger conclusion, both non-overlapping rolling
windows of 12 months, as used by Coaker (2006), and moving window correlation estimations are
graphed and analysed. As the size of the window has a strong impact of the results, particularly in
the moving window correlation estimations, three different window sizes are used: 6, 24 and 60
months, to highlight the differences; however the main focus will be on the 24 month window.

6.5 Case study: Assessing the benefits of diversification
The aim of this study is to show what asset classes have provided the best diversification benefits
over the 25 year period 1985 – 2012. As shown in Section 2, the correlation between assets
ultimately determines the risk of the portfolio, and if investors are assumed to act rationally, they are
only concerned about return in relation to risk. The Sharpe ratio measures this relationship, and is
chosen to evaluate the performance of the set of portfolios, to understand which portfolios that
provided the best return in relation to risk on a yearly basis. The results are analysed through the
perspective of the results from Hypothesis 1 – 3, to conclude the actual diversification benefits from
diversification as correlation changes over time, and during particular crisis periods. This measure
was chosen based on the assumption of investors’ rationality (above), and it is one of the measures
used by Blitz and Vliet (2008) to evaluate the performance of momentum strategies across asset
classes.
       This study compares the performance of a portfolio that is actively rebalanced every year,
based on the combination of asset classes that performed the best in the previous year, which is a

                                                                                  18
modified but still similar approach to that of Blitz and Vliet (2008), with the following set of passive
[P] and active ‘Constant-Mix’ [CM] portfolios, classified on the basis of Sharpe and Perold (1995):

    i) Diversified portfolio with equal weights (20%) in stocks, investment grade and high yield
           bonds, oil and gold [CM]
    ii) Bond portfolio (50 % in investment grade/high yield bond portfolio respectively) [CM]
    iii) Stock portfolio (100 % in the S&P 500) [P]
    iv) Stock and bond portfolio (50 % in the Stock and Bond portfolio respectively) [CM]
    v) Investment grade bond portfolio (100 %) [P]
    vi) High yield bond portfolio (100 %) [P]
    vii) Gold portfolio (100 %) [P]
    viii) Oil portfolio (100 %) [P]
    ix) Average optimal portfolio (34 % in the investment grade/high yield bond portfolios
          respectively, 9 % in the stock portfolio and 12 % in the Gold and Oil portfolios
           respectively) [CM]

The case study is carried out in Excel, and uses the monthly return data as specified in Sections 4.1,
4.2 and 4.3. As Sharpe ratios are evaluated on a yearly basis, the monthly return is compounded to
get the yearly returns from each asset class, and a covariance matrix is constructed on a yearly basis,
based on the return data in each year (that is 25 matrices in total). The study starts out by finding the
optimal weights in each asset class every year to produce the highest possible annual Sharpe ratio.
For this purpose, a second ‘weighted covariance matrix’ is set up for each year, based on the former
covariance matrix, and the weights in each asset class. The sum of this weighted covariance matrix is
the portfolio variance, and the standard deviation is the square root of the portfolio variance. The
return of the portfolio is the simple weighted average of the asset returns, calculated from the yearly
returns and the weights in each asset class. As the Sharpe ratio uses excess returns, the average risk-
free rate was calculated on a yearly basis (specified in Section 4.4), and subtracted from the portfolio
return, thus creating portfolio excess returns on a yearly basis. The Sharpe ratio is calculated by
dividing this portfolio excess return with its standard deviation. To find the optimal portfolio, the
Solver function is used to maximize the Sharpe ratio directly, by changing the portfolio weights, as
they ultimately determine both the standard deviation and return on the portfolio. A constraint was

                                                                                  19
put on the weights to be in the range of 0 % – 100 %, and that the total weights had to sum up to
100 %, thus implementing a short-sale and borrowing constraint for the portfolio.
        To find the Sharpe ratios of portfolios: i, ii, iv and ix, the same procedure with a weighted
covariance matrix is constructed on a yearly basis, however holding the weights in each asset class
fixed (hence a ‘Constant-Mix portfolio). The sum of the covariance matrices constitutes the variance
on each portfolio, and the Sharpe ratio is similarly the weighted average of the yearly returns less the
average yearly risk-free rate, divided by the square root of the portfolio variance.
        The Sharpe ratios for portfolios ii, iii, v, vi, vii) and viii, is the yearly excess returns, divided
by the yearly standard deviations of returns.
       To measure the performance of the active portfolio rebalanced each year based on the
optimal portfolio, a weighted covariance matrix is set up for each year, using the weights from year
N-1, and the excess return is calculated using the same optimal weights from year N-1, and the
return and risk-free rate in year N.

As the study is performed on a yearly basis, there is a need to define entire years as crisis periods, as
well as pre- and post-crisis periods, in order to analyse how the Sharpe ratios are affected over the
crises cycles. The new, broader definitions are presented below:

    a) Pre Dotcom: 1998 and 1999
    b) Dotcom crisis: 2000, 2001and 2002
    c) Post Dotcom: 2003 and 2004
    d) Pre Subprime: 2005 and 2006
    e) Subprime crisis: 2007 and 2008
    f) Post Subprime: 2009 and 2010
    g) Sovereign debt crisis: 2011 and 2012

In order to illustrate the best diversification benefits, the monthly return on the portfolios with the
highest Sharpe ratios during the specific crises are graphed.

6.7 Problematization
The first obvious problem with the methodology described above is that of autocorrelation. This
problem is tackled as described in Section 6.1 by the use of Newey-West standard errors. However
the problem is not completely resolved. There also exist some problems with endogeneity since
there might be a number of parameters in the error term of especially the liquidity and volatility data.
It can also be assumed that a lot of other factors correlate with these two parameters in times of
crisis.
       The choice of Sharpe ratio as the risk-adjusted return measure for the purpose of the case
study can also be questioned, and several other measures exist, such as the Treynor ratio and the
Modigliani risk-adjusted performance framework (RAP). Sharpe ratio was chosen over the Treynor
ratio since the latter only takes into account market risk (P ). The RAP-framework has some
advantages over the Sharpe ratio, mainly that it quantifies the risk-adjusted return as percentages,
however it was rejected since the Sharpe ratio has a stronger connection with the theoretical
framework presented. Furthermore, the RAP-framework is partially derived from the Sharpe ratio.
        Another criticism of the Sharpe ratio is the scepticism of the existence of a risk-free rate.
However, finance theory relies on the existence of a risk-free rate and this problem will always be
present no matter which risk-adjusted measure that is chosen.

7.4.2 The portfolios’ performance during crises in general
As the case study was carried out on a yearly basis, the crisis definitions follow the specification in

bonds during the Dotcom crisis in the optimal portfolio (see Table 8).

Sharpe ratio of 3.19, followed by the average optimal portfolio and the equally weighted bond

this recovery period.

markets were underperforming compared to the stock market. During the crisis period, this
relationship is reversed and the investment grade bond market is the best performer. This is

market and investment grade bonds. Hence, this correlation decrease is proven to directly affect the
performance of the portfolios. As was also shown in the results for Hypothesis 2, the high yield
bond market correlation with the stock market increases during a crisis, which holds true also in this
study as the Sharpe ratios remain below 0. This shows that investment grade bonds were the best
possible diversification during the Dotcom crisis in terms of reward-to-risk. The period after the
crisis is a time where the high yield bond market takes off, even more than the stock market, again in

8. Analysis
In this Section, the results presented in Section 7 are analysed and discussed. The aim is to put the
findings from this thesis into perspective by connecting the results with previous literature, and to
discuss the implications for investment management, as well as suggesting areas for further research.
        At first this study aimed to challenge the assumption of the MPT that correlation is constant
over time. This was proven to be wrong by the first tests where it was possible to see that
correlations vary distinctly over time; volatility can be observed both graphically and from the
regression results. This supports the findings of Coaker (2006), and as correlation does vary over
time, this brings both opportunities and significant risks to investors. If correlation between asset
classes were constant, it would be possible to hold an optimal risky portfolio. It proves that it can be
profitable to diversify and it could be crucial in times of crisis, but the question remains as to how?
       It was shown with statistical significance that investment grade bonds have provided good
diversification benefits in times of crises, as correlation between the stock market and investment
grade bonds tends to decrease during crises, as proven by the regression results in Table 3 and
Figures 6 and 8. This indicates that there is a “flight-to-quality” effect as described in the findings of
Baur and Lucey (2010). This is the nature of investment grade bonds: they provide a smaller
predictable return every year compared to stocks and they are subject to different risks (see Figures
10 and 11). It was also shown with statistical significance that correlation kept decreasing in the
period after the crisis; this could reflect that as confidence returns to the financial markets, there is a
reversed flight-to-quality effect, leading to a sell-off in the safer bond markets, and a return to riskier
assets.
       Gold shows a decrease in correlation with the stock market during the Subprime and
Sovereign Debt crises, indicating that gold is still considered a safe investment when the financial
markets are unstable. Büyükşahin et al (2008) concluded that diversification benefits from
commodities eroded during the Subprime crisis, however they did not study the individual
commodities, but instead a broad commodity index. It might well be that gold is a good
diversification in times of severe financial crises, but commodities in general are not.
       It is concluded that the high yield bond market provides a weak alternative for
diversification, as it tends to increase its correlation with the stock market in times of crises,
particularly seen for the Subprime and Sovereign debt crises, which is line with Coaker (2006) and
Reilly and Wright (2001). However it can be seen in the case study that high yield bonds provide a
good alternative for equity investors, as it has outperformed the stock market in terms of higher

                                                                                  29
Sharpe ratios during the chosen time span. However it is important to take transaction costs and
market access into account when it comes to the high yield bond market. Access to the market can
be difficult, but it has been more accessible due to a number of ETFs in recent years. However this
is something that further studies needs to examine.
       The next part of the study investigated if changes in any of the market factors liquidity
and/or volatility had any explanatory effects on the correlation between the stock market and the
investment grade bonds, and the correlation between the stock market and gold. This is interesting
as they reflect the climate of the financial markets and might provide an indication for investors
when it is time to rebalance their portfolios. During the Dotcom crisis the liquidity premium
affected the correlation between the stock- and investment grade bond market negatively, which
supports the “flight-to-safety” effect, however this effect was slightly reversed during the Subprime
crisis, as it became slightly positive. As previously discussed, this should be due to the different
nature of the Subprime crisis as compared to the Dotcom crisis. The change in volatility levels had
little impact during the Dotcom crisis; however it is the dominant factor during the Subprime crisis,
where volatility reached unprecedented levels. This negatively affects both assets’ correlations with
the stock market. The negative asset correlations indicates a flight to safety since the return on the
chosen assets stay positive due to higher demand, however the effect appears to come from
volatility, and not as much from liquidity.
        In an attempt to quantify the actual benefits of diversification over the past 25 years, a case
study was conducted that compared the Sharpe ratio of an all-equity investor with the Sharpe ratio
for a set of diverse active ‘Constant-Mix’ portfolios, passive portfolios and an active portfolio that
rebalances every year according to previous year’s optimal portfolio. The choice of the Sharpe ratio
was based on its sound theoretical framework, linking returns to risk effectively determined by the
assets’ correlation, its common usage in the industry and as it was used by Blitz and Vliet (2008),
whose results were key in motivating our research.
        The price for an all-equity investor to ignore diversification has been high, as the Sharpe
ratio for that portfolio is significantly lower on average than any diversified portfolio or an all-bond
portfolio. This study shows that the highest average Sharpe ratios occurred for the bond portfolios,
and it confirms the finding that investment grade bonds provide high diversification benefits, as it is
the optimal portfolio during the Dotcom crisis, and one of the best portfolios during the Subprime
crisis. Another interesting finding on investment grade bonds is that they performed worst in the
period before crises: this could imply that the bond market is an indicator that the stock market is

                                                                                  30
about to tumble, however this is an area this study does not address and may indeed be due to
several factors. One possible explanation is that interest levels tend to be higher before crises in
order to cool down the economy, but this is an area which we strongly recommend further research.
        The high yield bond portfolio was the best performer on average, but it also had the highest
variability and its strong correlation with the stock market lead to its worst performance occurring
during crises periods: thus it is not a good alternative for diversification, but given the on average
high Sharpe ratios it remains an interesting investment alternative.
        Gold proved to be the best diversification vehicle during the Subprime crisis, with a Sharpe
ratio far exceeding its average over the 25-year period. On average, the performance of the
commodities is lowest, mainly due to high standard deviations in their returns. The benefits from
diversifying into gold during the Subprime crisis could be due to the nature of the crisis as such; it
was not only a stock market crash, the entire financial system was threatened. As the financial
system is threatened, this is bound to affect the bond market, but not necessarily gold (or other
physical assets). Even though commodities on their own do not have attractive Sharpe ratios, a risk-
averse investor would still have benefitted from diversifying into commodities, as the variability is
the lowest in the portfolios that was the most diversified across all the asset classes.
        Perhaps surprisingly, the overall performance of the equally diversified portfolio did not
match those of the bond portfolios, even though variability was less. The actively rebalanced
portfolio based on last year’s optimal portfolio performed rather poorly compared to the others,
and when taking transaction costs into account, this would have been a costly strategy, not providing
the returns one might have expected following the reasoning behind momentum strategies,
especially drawing from Blitz and Vliet (2008), who found a significant momentum effect in cross-
asset class investments. However, they used actual returns to rank the highest yielding assets,
whether this portfolio selected the assets that created the optimal Sharpe ratio, and there is
apparently a big discrepancy between these two methods.
        Figure 10 and Figure 11 challenges the notion of gold as a safe diversification alternative
during times of crisis. It shows that even if gold were to have positive returns, the volatility would be
quite high resulting in a lower Sharpe ratio on average compared to investment grade bonds. An
investor therefore has an alternative in times of crisis, to choose an absolute return that is more
frequently the case with investment grade bonds or choose speculation on higher returns with gold.
Investment grade bonds often results in higher Sharpe ratios, but it all comes down to the investor’s
risk aversion.

                                                                                  31
9. Conclusion
This thesis provides evidence that correlation varies distinctly over time, and that different asset
classes are affected differently over the crisis cycle. Investment grade bonds decreases in correlation
with the stock market during periods of crisis in general, and the case study proves that these
correlation changes can be directly translated into diversification benefits, given the high relative
Sharpe ratios provided by an investment grade bond portfolio during a crisis. Gold is shown to
decrease its correlation with the stock market under severe financial crises such as the Subprime
crisis, and consequently it provided the best diversification benefits in terms of Sharpe ratios during
that crisis. It is however concluded that the volatility in returns from gold is much higher than that
of investment grade bonds during crises. Furthermore it supports previous research regarding the
correlation increases between the stock market and high yield bonds during crises; this is supported
by the case study where the high yield bond- and stock portfolios were the worst performing
portfolios during crises. WTI-Crude Oil was not affected by crises with any statistical significance.
       The diversification benefits that arise from investment grade bonds and gold from the
changes in correlation are partially due to a “flight-to-safety” effect. It is also shown that a well-
diversified portfolio has not benefitted from higher Sharpe ratios than those yielded by the bond
markets, but it has had lower variability according to the same study, which is line with the MPT.

9.1 Suggestions for further research
During the case study it was found that the excessive return on the investment grade bond portfolio
was negative in the times immediately preceding crisis periods, which might imply that negative
investment grade bond returns indicate a future stock market crash, or financial turmoil. This effect
is something that was not considered at the commencement of this thesis, thus no further emphasis
was put on this particular area. Furthermore, it would be beneficial for the investment- and risk
management professions to use economic models without assuming constant correlation, allowing
modelling risk and returns in a way that reflects reality. A reality in which correlations are time-
varying.

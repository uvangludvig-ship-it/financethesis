Do IPOs affect prices of other assets? Evidence from a Swedish Setting

Abstract:
This paper looks at effect of IPOs on both market and industry returns through using a sample
of 149 IPOs between 2005-2022. We find evidence that sizeable IPOs depress market returns
during the pre-offering period where the impact is negatively correlated  with size. Through
looking at the covariance between IPOs and industries we find that IPOs permanently decreases
industry returns on the listing day where the covariance and the return have an inverse
relationship. This depressive effect is amplified when  we look at the impact of large IPOs as
they have an additional depressive effect on industry returns during the pre-offering period,
offering date and listing period. We find support for a portfolio rebalancing hypothesis where
industry prices depress due  to a substitution effect.

Keywords:
Market price, Market return, Initial public offering, IPO, Industry covariance, Asset pricing,
Supply shock

1.     Introduction
The behavior of security prices has traditionally  been explained and researched from the angle
of investor preferences, focusing on factors such as risk aversion (Braun and Larrain, 2009).
Moreover, most of the literature surrounding initial public offerings (IPOs) is centered around
the IPO event itself such as underpricing structures and market conditions rather than the
impact of an IPO on other assets. Supply has traditionally been assumed to be fixed (Lucas,
1978) or perfectly elastic (Cox, Ingersoll, and Ross, 1985), under these assumptions potential
IPO effects are not considered. Given that IPOs represent the introduction of a new asset into
the market, often with substantial value, it is probable that they will also exert outward effects
on the broader market (Braun and Larrain).
Braun and Larrain  (2009)  study the empirical effect of supply on other asset prices through
conducting an event study on 254 IPOs in 22 emerging markets and find that  assets that covary
highly with an IPO will exhibit a permanent decreased return post issuance. Shi, Sun and Zhang
(2018) extend this analysis by looking at the effect of an  IPO on market  returns  in China
through observing 1121 IPOs during the sample period of 1996-2016. They find that a sizeable
IPO has a transitory effect on market prices  as  market returns initially depress but reverse as
liquidity constraints ease.
We  do a geographical extension  by investigating the effects of  149  IPOs on  both  market  and
industry returns  between 2005-2022 in Sweden. The methodology for investigating the market
return is based on of Shi.et.al.’s (2018) paper while the methodology for the industry effect is
based off Braun and Larrain’s (2009) paper.  Braun and Larrain focus on emerging markets
while  Shi et.al concentrate on China,  both markets which differ greatly along multiple
dimensions.  As such, there is a notable gap in literature regarding effect of  asset supply  on
returns  of other assets within a  developed  and  well-established  market Sweden  (Svensk
Värdepappersmarknad, n.d.). Exploring IPO dynamics in Sweden could provide valuable
insights into how supply shocks influence  asset returns  in a  different  market environment.
Sweden has one of Europe’s most robust equity markets from both the viewpoint of investors
and companies. The Swedish equity market ranks amongst the largest in Europe  in regards to
market capitalization relative to GDP  (Svensk Värdepappersmarknad, n.d.). Agari (2024)
reports that as of 2021, Sweden  has  Europe’s deepest capital market and  has second  prior to
that.  Furthermore,  Stockholm experienced a record-breaking year for IPOs with a collective
total surpassing France, Spain and Germany combined in 2021 (Svensk Värdepappersmarknad,
n.d.). Agari further reports that between 2014-2024, 501 companies have been listed in Sweden
which is more than the total number of IPOs in Germany, France, Spain, and Netherlands.
Sweden’s market depth with its broad range of assets and IPOs can be attributed to the investing
culture prevalent in the country. Equity markets have attracted “everyone from the man on the
street to very engaged private banking investors, entrepreneurs, but also the small and mid-cap
investment community” (Agari,  2024). The presence of large institutional investors has also
been a key driver in SMEs getting listed as it gives  comfort and confidence  to  both  businesses
and individual investors. This strong investment culture has been supported and encouraged by
the Swedish government. As an example, in 2012, the state introduced a savings account,  ISK
which allows individuals to invest without  tax reporting frictions.  Further, in the 1990s, the
government started allowing people to invest part of their pension accounts in funds of their
choice. There are also youth groups such as Aktiespararna which go to schools and educate
students aged 16-18 about finance and investing.

The Swedish market also has strong international appeal as over 40% of share ownership stems

since the capital markets are deep in Sweden there are more available companies to aid in
valuation and further required analysis. The  Swedish culture and system have also translated
into the stock market returns with Swedish indexes outperforming their peers  (Agari,  2024).
We observe no direct significant effect of IPOs on market  return  in Sweden during the IPO
window. However, we do find a significant effect that IPOs depress market prices by an average
of 0.013 percentage points on the offering day when  regressing  using size as the independent
variable during our sample period.  This implies that the IPO needs to be large enough for
investors to sell stocks that do not covary with the IPO to collect funds.  We however do not
observe  that market prices rebound which implies that  there is no price pressure theory in play,
rather  the results seem to support the  portfolio rebalancing theory which  yields  a permanent
decrease of prices (Shi.et.al, 2018).
To  further explore the portfolio  rebalancing theory, we extend our analysis by  employ Braun
and Larrain’s  (2009)  methodology  and  sub-setting the market into industries. We explore the
impact of IPOs on industry returns by  studying the  relationship between  IPO industry  and 11
sets of industry portfolios. Regressing  covariance  as  an  explanatory variable we  observe an
inverse relationship between IPO industry covariance and returns.   The analysis shows that
there is an average  depressive effect on industry returns on the listing day of  -0.54 percentage
points. There is no significant effect on any other date in the IPO window due to limits to
arbitrage from uncertainty (De Long et al., 1990; Shleifer and Vishny, 1997). When we interact
covariance and size, we find that large IPOs depress  market-adjusted returns  for  pre-offering,
and pre-listing  by  0.68  and 0.41  percentage points  respectively  and depresses excess return by
0.43 percentage points on the offering day  likely due to  reduction of uncertainty.  These results
indicate that a significant  value of existing holdings  is  sold before  offering to subscribe when
it first gets offered as opposed to before it is listed on the market.  We also find that market
prices are depressed regardless of size and solely on covariance on the listing day. These
findings support a portfolio rebalancing theory as there is a permanent decrease on market
prices as observed by Braun and Larrain rather than the price pressure story observed by
Shi.et.al. (2018). There is also support for the downward-sloping demand of stocks theory as
both covariance and size drive price depression  (Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980).

2.     Literature review

Historically there has been a lack of research on the impact of supply on market prices (Braun
and Larrain, 2009). When it comes to level of supply, Hong et.al. (2008) finds that market-to-
book ratios across the United States have a negative relationship with the measure of relative
asset supply (ratio of a state’s total book equity to total personal income) in a specific state.
Other literature has focused on change in supply and specifically the price effect of equity
issues. Baker and Wurgler (2000) observes that market price levels fall after a period of active
issuance. Furthermore, Ofek and Richardson (2000) document that an increase in supply
caused by the expiration of IPO lockups results in a lower price of recent IPOs. Supply effects
have also been found in the fixed-income market which Newman and Rierson (2004) show by
studying a substantially large bond issuance of Deutsche Telekom and find that this depresses
the prices of bonds of other European telecommunications firms.

Braun and Larrain (2009) are the first to study the empirical effect of changes in supply on
other  assets through studying the impact of IPOs on prices of other assets in the market. They
conduct an event study on over 254 IPOs in 22 emerging markets and show that IPOs
permanently affect the entire cross section of prices across different securities or  assets within
their respective markets  during the month of issuance. Furthermore, Braun and Larrain find a
negative relationship between covariance and price decline i.e. portfolios that have a high
covariances with the IPO experience a greater decline in prices compared to other portfolios in
the month of issuance. Securities which were similar to the IPO assets in terms of size and
book-to-market ratios also experienced a relative price decline. The magnitude of these effects
increases when the supply shock increases. A high covariance between the IPO industry and an
asset indicates that the IPO is a good substitute for previously held similar assets and therefore
are impacted to a larger degree.  The authors  suggest  that a portfolio rebalancing takes place
where demand is constant and downward-sloping which means that investors  obtain  the new
IPO asset at the expense of existing assets which results in depressed industry return.

Shi et. al. (2018) extends these findings by investigating whether sizable IPOs have a negative
impact on the overall market price in China with a sample of 1121 IPOs during their sample
period of 1996-2016. Their initial hypothesis is that a large supply shock in the form of an IPO
may crowd out demand for other stocks in the market which builds upon Braun and Larrain’s
model of downward-sloping demand.  There is anecdotal evidence which suggests that IPOs
have a negative impact on market prices from both previous IPOs and regulations to protect
against depressed market prices enforced by the China Securities Regulatory Commission
(CSRC). Furthermore, in China, investors are required to post the funds when subscribing to
an IPO and these funds are locked-in till the listing date. During most of the sample period,
IPO subscribers froze the required funds for their orders and most IPOs were heavily
oversubscribed. They find evidence that sizable IPOs depress the market return both on offering
day as well as listing day. Furthermore, the IPO has a depressing effect on market return from
the day before the offering until the end of the frozen period which implies that IPOs can impact
market returns even if there is a substantial external capital  flow into the market. This effect is
found to be transitory and provides support for price pressure theory as the market price
bounces back on both post-listing day and the unfrozen day.

Our paper contributes to existing literature through providing a new geographical perspective
of the Swedish market which differs greatly from past geographies both on a macro and micro
level. Compared to both China and emerging markets, Sweden has a substantially higher
financial literacy rate which is likely to impact investor behavior  and stock market reactions

                                   5
(Klapper  et.al.,  2024). Also, both  China and emerging markets  are  not  internationally
integrated. As emphasized by Braun and Lorrain (2009), emerging markets are small and
incompletely integrated with international markets while Shi et.al. (2018) points out that the
Chinese market is largely isolated from international portfolio capital flows, both providing a
distinct sample unaffected by external factors. In contrast to this, Sweden attracts large amount
of foreign funds with 40 percent of share ownership  coming from out of Sweden (Svensk
Värdepappersmarknad, n.d.). The final distinct difference is the investment culture which is
unique to Sweden  (Agari, 2024).  The investment culture has given the Swedish population
more investment knowledge and experience which could impact investor behavior.
China and Sweden also differ greatly when it  comes to participation. Sweden has the highest
stock market participation of 60% (Kaustia  et.al.,  2023) in Europe while in China it is less than
10% (Gao & Fok, 2015). Stock market participation rates can also reflect the level of market
efficiency. Higher participation rates may suggest a more mature and efficient market where
prices quickly adjust to new information, potentially mitigating the impact of IPOs on market
prices. Another key difference is that the Chinese IPO market  has a lot of  zealous speculation
which  is  characterized by high rates of underpricing and oversubscription. This suggests  that
there is a significant impact on market dynamics besides individual stocks  which may impact
the results as Sweden does not have the same  clear speculation  (Shi.et.al., 2018). Lastly, the
regulations and policies surrounding IPOs differs greatly.   Wilhelm and Ljungqvist (2003)
suggest that lockups and their subsequent expirations may result in increased speculative
trading in the market.  Sweden does not have  frozen periods, lock-up periods or CSRC
moratoriums  which alleviates  some of the  speculation and liquidity constraints  compared to
what China faces  during the events of IPOs  and may therefore impact the  results  (Pousette and
Tipner, 2023).

3.2.   Downward-sloping demand of stocks

Braun and Larrain (2009) formalize a model of downward-sloping demand to explain the
portfolio rebalancing effect.  Studying the downward-sloping demand curves of stocks is key
in understanding market dynamics and investor behavior as a response to IPOs. This concept
is a contrast to flat demand curves which are typically assumed in frictionless benchmarks like
the CAPM  model previously discussed. Stocks are downward sloping if there are market
frictions which limit arbitrage opportunities and therefore indicate limited risk bearing capacity

The implications of Equation 6 and Equation 3  are  similar where  the price of a stock that has a
positive covariance with the IPO  falls,  and this effect is amplified by risk aversion and the
weight of the IPO. Both the downward-sloping model and CAPM provide a similar intuition
for the repricing of stocks as they are models of relative pricing i.e the pricing of any stock is
relative to the rest of the stocks in the market. When a new stock is introduced to the market
through an IPO, the portfolio of references changes and the covariance with the IPO measures
the effect on a certain asset.

There are also behavioral considerations where literature suggests that investors categorize
their assets and wealth by styles e.g investors may categorize assets by certain market-to-book
ratios. When an IPO occurs, investors rebalance their portfolios to  maintain their portfolio
composition of different styles. Due to this, stocks with similar styles are sold as the new IPO
is a direct substitute. Following this, downward sloping demand may occur from the interaction
between rational arbitrageurs and biased investors where the IPO crowds out and lowers the
price of the substitutable stocks (Barberis and Shleifer, 2003). Frictions  such as uncertainty
prevent the correction of mispricing that takes place due to style investing (De Long et al.,
1990; Shleifer and Vishny, 1997).

In models with frictionless arbitrage, there is likely no immediate change in asset price on the
date of issuance if agents have correctly predicted the IPO. However, in scenarios where there
are constraints on arbitrage this is not the case. When an IPO is first announced, there is a lack
of certainty, but the certainty increases during the IPO window and peaks on the listing date.
This growth of certainty implies that there is a risk to an arbitrage strategy of short selling
stocks with high IPO covariance and buying those with low IPO covariance until the listing
date. This uncertainty may deter arbitrageurs from pursuing their strategy and affecting prices
until the date of issuance (De Long et al., 1990; Shleifer and Vishny, 1997).

Empirical evidence is in favor of a downward-sloping demand model as it suggests that the
market reaction to equity issuances is mostly negative (Baker and Wurgler, 2000). If empirical
evidence was to support the CAPM model, we would instead see an ambiguous market
reaction.

3.3.    Price pressure

In 3.2, we focused on the portfolio rebalancing as done in Braun and Larrain’s paper (2009).
On the other hand, Shi et.al (2018) rather focus on the impact of size of IPOs on market prices
as they hypothesize that a large supply shock may crowd out demand  for existing stocks in the
market. They argue  the  transitory  price pressure  hypothesis as described by Harris and Gurel
(1986)  that when an IPO is sizable enough and there is great speculation surrounding it,
investors may also sell stocks which do not covary highly with the IPO which will affect the
entire market rather than an industry. If price decline only occurs due to portfolio rebalancing,
then we should expect to see a permanent price decline. However, liquidity or behavioral effects
can result in a transitory price pressure. Price pressure consists of two concepts, first, before
subscribing to an IPO, investors collect funds through selling other stocks and second, market
makers only take these stocks at a discount. This discount may be correlated with IPO
covariance if investors sell similar stocks to finance their new asset. However, investors are
likely to repurchase their previously owned shares once they build up liquidity again and the
share price will rebound. As a result, if the initial price depression is due to a liquidity shortage,
a large price increase in stocks with high covariance with the IPO should be observed in the
period following the IPO as this effect is reversed. This should also be reflected in an abnormal
volume caused by the IPO event.

4.    Background on IPOs

In Sweden, a typical IPO and stock exchange listing spans between four to six months (Pousette
and Tipner, 2023). The process begins with the issuer assembling a team of financial, legal and
public relations advisors. The preparatory phase involves thorough due diligence, prospectus
drafting and engagement with the targeted stock exchange for a listing review, which may also
include a pre-audit by a separate auditor. A prospectus draft is a legal document required by
regulatory authorities in the process of an IPO. It includes detailed information about the
company, its financial performance, operations, risks and the securities themselves which are
being offered for sale. The prospectus is used by potential investors as an information source.
The possibility to place orders for private individuals and institutions  opens  once this
prospectus is published. Order books are typically kept open for 1-2 weeks but at times the
subscription period can also close prior to the decided date. The price per share is set as either
an interval or fixed price before the listing which is  then decided based on  demand once the
company is listed. The listing company decides the composition between institutions and
private individuals and at times, if many people are interested in subscribing, the listing can be
oversubscribed meaning an investor receives fewer shares than they applied for as there are
more people involved. Once you have subscribed you are required to have the required funds
as outlined by the bank on a specific date otherwise you will not be able to partake in the IPO.
However, these funds are not  frozen or locked between the subscription and listing date
(Avanza customer service, personal communication, April 10, 2024). On the day of the listing,
those who signed up and had the required funds will receive their shares (How do I invest in a
company about to list on the stock market?  2022).
The main costs associated with an IPO comprise of the stock exchange listing fee which
consists of a fixed fee at application, variable fees based on market capitalization and in certain
cases, cancellation fees. Additional fees include annual exchange fees, listing auditor fees and

                                   9
the SFSA’s review of prospectus fees. Once the IPO has taken place, financial advisors receive
a fixed fee which is often a percentage of total shares offered (Pousette and Tipner, 2023).
Pousette  and Tipner  (2023)  report  that  IPOs  need  to  meet  the  corporate  governance
requirements outlined by the Swedish Companies Act and the Corporate Governance Code.
These requirements include regulations related to board compositions, nomination committees,
remuneration committees and audit committees.

In this paper,  we investigate  the effect of IPOs on the market  return  and the effect of IPOs on
industry return. For simplicity, we have separated each following section of this paper into the
market and industry.

To look at the IPO effect on other assets we focus on the subscription day (OD), when investors
first subscribe, and the listing day (LD) when investors can trade the shares on the stock

5.2.    IPO Effect on the Market
We study the impact of IPOs on the market through looking at the impact on market return and
market turnover ratio.  To study  this, we collect  open  and  close  prices,  trading value, market
capitalization, bid-ask spread  and number of shares  for each stock listed on the Stockholm
Stock Exchange for our sample period 2005-2022  from Finbas.
We use raw data for the Stockholm Stock Exchange to make our own market index as opposed
to already existing indices (such as OMXSPI or Six Return) as it allows us to isolate the market
price in exclusion of the IPOs in our sample. Our  index is made by value weighting each stock
on the Stockholm Stock Exchange by their market capitalization. To ensure that our market
index is a valid proxy, we run a robustness check with the OMXSPI index and find a correlation

of 0.94 when using open-close  returns and 0.98 when using close-close  returns. As such, we
use close-close returns.  The methodology and regression analysis  are  based on  Shi et.al.’s
(2018)  paper  with adaptations to fit the Swedish market dynamics.

5.2.1.        Regression Analysis of IPO effect on Market return
We use a Garch-in-mean model to test how a sizable IPO would impact market return during

the volatility of asset returns including market returns (Engle, 2001). The Garch-in-mean model

heteroskedasticity i.e. the variance of returns is not constant over time.

5.2.2        Regression Analysis of IPO effect on Market Turnover Ratio

which is reflected in a low turnover. Once the frozen period  ceases, liquidity increases  as
investors start to buy back shares they  previously  sold to fund their IPO subscription. In this
case,  both turnover and market return should  reverse. In Sweden, there is no frozen period;
rather an investor needs to have the required funds in their account by a certain date to be able
to subscribe to the IPO  (Avanza customer service, personal communication, April 10, 2024).
As such,  there is no significant liquidity constraint the promotes this pattern of investor
behavior. We can expect to see an increase turnover before the subscription as investors sell
their existing assets, a decrease when funds have been collected and an increase right after once
investors can reallocate their portfolio and repurchase shares.

To study if covariance between an industry and the IPO industry has an effect on the industry
portfolio returns we use the following regression:

                                     𝑅$,.  =  𝑎  +  𝜎$,789  +  𝜀$,.                              (11)

where the dependent variable  𝑅$,.  is the abnormal return of industry portfolio  i  during the day
t  as defined in Table  1. We use 2 different metrics to measure abnormal return: excess return,
defined as the industry portfolio return less the risk-free rate and market-adjusted return,
defined as industry portfolio return less the market return. Our main explanatory variable  𝜎$,789
is the covariance between the IPO  industry  and industry portfolio  i.
We calculate 11 return observations for each IPO referring to 11 industry portfolios. We control
for time and industry through interacting year and industry dummy variables as fixed effects
represented as a in our equation. We use year as opposed to month due to sample size restraints.
This is contrasting with Braun and Larrain (2009) who use a vector containing a set of IPO
fixed effects. We use industry year fixed effects  represented by  𝑎  as opposed to IPO fixed
effects  since  industry-year fixed effects  allow us to capture the specific dynamics and trends
within each industry over time rather than controlling for the IPO itself, in this way we isolate
the effect of the introduction of the IPO from any industry-specific dynamics that may affect
industry return. We run a separate regression for each defined event date  t.
The main obstacle with running Equation  11  is that the covariance between an IPO and each
industry is not observable before it is listed therefore, a proxy  is needed.  Braun and Larrain
(2009)  use  the covariance between the industry  of the  IPO and  a  set of industry portfolios  as a
proxy.  They however argue for using a US based  proxy  as they  highlight that an in-sample
covariance faces noisy samples and endogeneity problems. The out-of-sample proxies provided
advantages tailored to emerging markets. For example,  firms are often organized as diversified
conglomerates due to poor development of financial intermediaries which can be reflected in
high covariance between firms and industries. They also argue that by using US covariances
they can have a forward-looking  benchmark as the US is a diversified and internationally
integrated market. Sweden has both of those characteristics making it a good benchmark
without the need for using the US  (Agari, 2024).  While in-sample covariances can present
endogeneity issues,  IPOs are endogenous in  and of  themselves as they are planned based on
market conditions and coordinated with financial institutions  which makes it difficult to
control.  Therefore, it is more interesting to focus on other confounding variables such as
industry and year fixed effects.
Another argument given by Braun and Larrain (2009) for using out of sample covariances is
that an IPO dramatically changes the market structure which would have implications of the
inter industry covariances. Based on this, they claim that in-sample covariances are not a
good forward-looking covariance benchmark. We ran a robustness check on the market
structure and inter-industry covariances by comparing the average ranking the industries have
amongst each other in terms of correlation in our sample period  (2005-2022) and in a 5-  year
historical period (2000-2005). When comparing the means for both correlation rankings, the
general market structure of the industries stays constant over time, so we find it reasonable to
use in-sample covariances  (see Table A1 and Table A2 in the Appendix).

5.5   IPO Size Effect on  Industry

impact of covariance on the return of a stock. Larger stocks will result in a great price
depression as there will be a large change to the portfolio reference. The downward-sloping
demand of stocks also show that this effect is amplified by size (Grossman and Stiglitz, 1980).

the combined effect of covariance and size.

Braun and Larrain  (2009) find that one standard deviation increase in IPO covariance results
in a 0.40% fall in market adjusted returns in their sample of 254 IPOs in 22 different emerging
markets during 1989-2002. We find a bigger effect size at 0.54 percentage points due to various
reasons. Firstly, our study is done using data from a different period, 2005-2022  and stock
market participation in Sweden has increased over those years which means more investors can
implement an arbitrage strategy which in turn has a bigger  impact on returns. Furthermore, as
Sweden has previously experienced great IPO activity and is one of the most developed
markets in Europe, investors are likely more sophisticated than emerging markets and therefore
can extract more value from their arbitrage trades resulting in greater market effect. From a
more practical standpoint, there are also differences in methodology, specifically industry
definitions. We use Eikon definitions for defining the industry whereas Braun and Larrain use
Fama French definitions and this can impact industry composition and response to IPOs. Braun

7.    Discussion

We do not find a  causal relationship between IPOs  and the market return during the defined
event window  which  contradicts previous literature as  Shi.et.al’s (2018) finds  as a  negative
causal relationship between IPOs and market return which is further amplified by size on both
the offering day and the listing day. A key reason as to why we do not find support for the
direct effect is that the  presence  of  zealous speculation  in China. This speculation is
characterized by oversubscribing to the IPO and is also a driver behind selling stocks which do
not covary with the IPO  to collect necessary funds.  This  speculation  is  also  the primary
justification that Shi et al. (2018) give  behind  the effect they find.  Furthermore,  Wilhelm and
Ljungqvist (2003) find that the lock-up period in China is a key driver of market speculation,
a concept which is not present in Sweden and therefore points to less speculation than China.
Furthermore, the strong investment culture of Sweden (Agari, 2024) coupled with significantly
higher financial literacy,  supports the notion of Sweden having less speculation  (Klapper et.al.,
2014). Furthermore, as Sweden is one of the deepest capital markets in Europe (Agari, 2024),
consisting of a wide range of assets, the impact of an IPO is reduced. Merton (1980) explains
that the introduction of a new stock into the market impacts the market composition and in turn
influences risk premiums. As Sweden has a deep capital market, the influence of IPOs on risk
premiums reduces resulting in a smaller impact on market prices.  Despite not finding a direct
causal relationship, we do find a significant effect of  -0.013 percentage points on the market
return on the pre-offering period when  observing  the impact of  IPO size on market prices. This
supports Shi.et.al's hypothesis that a large IPO does result in a depression of market prices
through investors getting ready to subscribe to an IPO by selling existing holdings that do not
only covary with the IPO on the pre-offering period. This emphasizes the importance  of IPO
size. It is also important to note that there was no effect on listing day. This could be related to
the IPO issuing  ratio  is  significantly greater than the  IPO  trading value  ratio. As such,  most
investors  likely  sell  their assets  to  subscribe to  an  IPO on the offering date.

Naturally, we did not find a reversal effect  of market prices  like Shi.et.al.  (2018).  Harris and
Gurel (1986) point out that the transitory effect on market prices is attributable to liquidity
and/or behavioral effects. We have already established that China may face more behavioral
constraints which are evident through the presence of over speculation in the IPO market. China
has a unique institutional set up such that funds committed to an IPO are  frozen up until near
the listing of the IPO. This creates a serious liquidity constraint as investors first need to sell
their assets to collect funds and then lock them up. As Avanza clarified, Sweden does not have
the same level of market controlling regulations and does not require funds to be committed.
Investors only need to have the required funds by a certain date in their bank account to be able
to  partake  in  the  IPO.  As  such,  we  do  not  find  any  support  for  the  price  pressure
hypothesis.  There is a  possibility that we find a more prevalent effect of IPOs on the market if
we use a larger sample size. In this case, our sample size was limited due to data availability
and quality issues. As we see an impact of size, it is possible that a sample which consists of a
greater  number  of large IPOs will have a more pronounced effect.

We find robust evidence that the covariance of IPOs has a causal effect on industry returns on
the listing date. During our sample period of 2005-2022, we find that an IPO reduces market
adjusted return by 0.54 percentage points on average at the 1% significance level. We observe
additional support for these findings when  studying  the relationship between size and IPO.
These findings suggest that industries that covary highly with the IPO experience depressed
returns due to the increased selling pressure that comes with investors trying to rebalance their
portfolios. Barberis and Shleifer (2003) provide a behavioral explanation behind this as they

                                  28
suggest that investors rebalance their portfolios when new assets enter the market to maintain
their exposure to certain styles of assets such as industry in this case. When there is a new IPO,
investors sell their existing holdings in that industry to be able to buy the new asset. This results
in a portfolio rebalancing effect and the returns of the assets which are sold are depressed. The
CAPM model also provides additional insights; as an IPO is introduced into the market, the
way assets correlate with each other changes which has an influence on both covariance and
expected returns. If the asset covaries highly with the IPO, it will have a higher expected return
because of a higher risk premium. As higher covariance results in a higher risk premium, it
also results in a decrease in price for that asset. This explains why we see that on the listing
date there is an average decrease in industry returns (Merton, 1980).

We do not find a significant effect for any other events in the defined IPO window when
studying  the direct relationship between covariance and returns  likely due to  the downward-
sloping demand of stocks. Portfolio rebalancing results in a depression of market prices and
cannot be corrected due to uncertainty which is a key constraint to arbitrage. This uncertainty
is highest at the announcement date and reaches a low  on the listing date as investors can be
sure of their arbitrage strategy involving short selling stocks with high IPO covariance and
buying those with low IPO covariance. As such, investors are likely to delay their strategy up
until the listing date  and likely  why significant effect  of covariance  on industry return  is only
present on the listing date.  We therefore  for constraints on arbitrage. (De Long et al., 1990;
Shleifer and Vishny, 1997).

Furthermore, when  observing  the effect of size interacted with covariance on industry returns,
we find that for large IPOs depresses market returns during the pre-offering, offering, pre-
listing, and listing period.  The most notable effect is that a when a large IPO covaries with an
industry on the pre-listing day it depresses industry returns by average of 0.68 percentage points
which is much larger than any other effect on industry return. This implies that more investors
subscribe to the IPO during the offering period rather than  when it is listed. This is  also  in line
with our results on the market effect which show significant  during  the pre-offering  period.
The results of size of IPOs expand on what we observe in Table 12 where there is only an effect
on the listing day. We believe has to do with the increased information and transparency that
follows an IPO of a larger company. Larger IPOs may have more extensive information
dissemination and market coverage leading up to the listing day. This could reduce the
uncertainty  surrounding the IPO which deters investors from implementing their arbitrage
strategy and influencing market prices until the listing day (De Long et al., 1990; Shleifer and
Vishny, 1997). This increased information could influence trading activity of selling stock that
have a high covariance with the IPO on both the day before offering and listing. Furthermore,
a larger IPO is likely to be backed by large institutional investors due to their high prevalence
in Sweden which further increases comfort of investors (Agari, 2024). Overall, our findings
that IPOs result in a permanent depression of industry returns are in line with those of Braun
and Larrain (2009).

We do not find an impact of IPOs on turnover which is not in line with either of the papers. An
IPO might depress market prices due to increased selling pressure as investors reallocate their
portfolios to participate in the IPO. This selling pressure can lead to a temporary imbalance
between supply and demand, causing prices to decrease. However,  it may not significantly
affect turnover because turnover represents the volume of shares traded within a given period,
which can remain unaffected by short-term  price movements caused by IPOs. Investors may
still engage in trading activities for other reasons unrelated to the IPO, maintaining turnover
levels despite price fluctuations.  Furthermore,  the frozen period in China gives a very clear

                                  29
picture of turnover. The turnover is high before subscribing to the IPO  due to high rates of
oversubscription  but then becomes negative  during the frozen period when the funds are
locked. Once this period ends, the liquidity constraint  alleviates,  and investors can buy back
their old holdings which again drives up the turnover. Sweden does not have a frozen period
and has less speculation  which explains the  results found  on turnover  ratio  Shi et.al.  (2018).

This paper provides support for the downward-sloping demand of stocks and the portfolio
rebalancing  hypothesis. Our findings suggest that investors rebalance their portfolios when a
new asset is introduced into the industry causing a permanent depression of prices which cannot
be corrected due to market frictions such as uncertainty.  This permanent price effect is seen
both on a industry level but also a market level which has not been found before.  We do not
find evidence supporting the price pressure hypothesis as the industry return does not rebound
likely due to a highly financially literate population with high stock market involvement and a
lack of a frozen period imposed by the government.

8.    Conclusion

This paper studies asset supply shocks in the form of 149 IPOs on the Stockholm Stock
Exchange for the years 2005-2022. Previous papers done on this topic by Shi.et.al (2018) and
Braun and Larrain (2009) have looked at China and emerging markets respectively. Sweden is
a dramatically different geography when it comes to stock market participation (Kaustia et.al.,
2023), financial literacy (Klapper et.al., 2014), financial infrastructure and investment culture
(Agari, 2024). Through doing this geographical expansion we can study if IPOs can have
effects  in  a  relatively  developed  and  internationally  well-established  market  (Svensk
Värdepappersmarknad, n.d.). We find that IPOs depress market prices during the pre-offering
period and do not experience a rebound  once the asset  is  listed. This is inconsistent with
previous literature.  Shi.et.al find a price pressure transitory effect where market prices are first
depressed during the offering and listing but then rebound once liquidity is restored. A key
reason why  our results differ is  due to  the regulatory differences between Sweden and China.
Since  Sweden does not have a  lockup  period,  there is  likely  less liquidity constraint  in the
market. Furthermore, Sweden does not experience the same level of speculation surrounding
IPOs as China which impacts subscription. As such, we do not find support for a price pressure
story in our findings. We do however note that a larger sample size could yield different results,
and this serves as a possible extension for future work within this topic.

As we find a permanent price depression amongst market prices, we subset the market into
industries as a next step  to explore the portfolio rebalancing hypothesis. To understand the
impact of IPOs on industry returns, we focus on IPO industry covariances. We find that an
asset supply shock has an inverse relationship with covariance on returns i.e. a positive
covariance between an IPO and industry will result in a  negative return and the greater the
covariance the greater the impact. The industry return is depressed by an average of 0.54
percentage points on the listing day due to increased selling pressure as investors are trying to
rebalance their portfolios. We also look at the effect of the size of the IPO on industry returns
and find that large IPOs impact depress returns of multiple periods in the IPO window due to
the decreased uncertainty. Decreased uncertainty  is the alleviation of a friction which allows
investors to perform their arbitrage trades earlier and therefore impact a wider range of industry
returns. As we find a permanent depression of returns for both the market and the industry and
observe frictions to arbitrage which also impact returns,  we find support for the downward-
sloping demand curve of stocks and the portfolio rebalancing hypothesis.

                                  30
These findings show that there is a portfolio rebalancing effect in well-established markets
(Agari, 2024)  with low  government interference  (Pousette and Tipner, 2023)  like Sweden.
Markets that exhibit similar characters are likely to find a  permanent depressive effective
amongst both market prices and industry prices.  This is due to  the demand of investors being
constant and following a downward-sloping demand curve which results in subscribing to an
IPO at the expense of existing holdings (Braun and Larrain, 2009).

A possible extension in the empirical analysis of supply shocks on other asset prices would be
to isolate IPO waves to observe the effects on return during a single large supply shock. This
analysis would offer a deeper understanding of the dynamics driving asset price movements
during periods of high IPO activity.

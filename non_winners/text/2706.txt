       Trends in Abnormal Returns to Hedge Fund Activism:
                      An Empirical Analysis

                                                   *
                           Johan Karlsson & Oliver Kull**

                                    Abstract
   Using a manually collected dataset of 2111 hedge fund activist campaigns, spanning the
   years 1999-2014, we find that the abnormal returns in the activist market exhibited a
   declining trend in the early years of the sample period. Since 2002 abnormal returns have
   been stable around 6-9% and show no signs of approaching zero. The short-term market
    reactions to activist campaigns remain to be significantly positive, and long-term reversals
   have not picked up. Despite the prevalence of abnormal returns to activist campaigns, the
    number of interventions have been stable during the years 2009-2014. In contrast to results
    from the European activist market, we also find that hedge funds not specialized in activism
   have been successful in generating abnormal returns in their sporadic ventures into
   shareholder activism in US target companies.

Key words: Hedge Fund Activism, Shareholder Activism, Abnormal Returns

1 Introduction
Shareholder activism has been on the rise since the 1980s, when institutional investors such as
pension and mutual funds started to invest in companies with the explicit objective of
influencing their management. While some early activists appeared to be more successful than
others, they in general failed to realize abnormal returns from the activist strategy (Gillan &
Starks, 2007).
   Hedge fund activism is a more recent phenomenon which gained significance during the 90s
(Brav, et al., 2013). Hedge funds are under lighter regulation than other institutional investors.
They are also equipped with stronger financial incentives to make profits, and have thus proven
to be more successful in creating value in target firms through shareholder activism. (Brav, et
al., 2010)
   Previous authors, most prominently Clifford (2008), Boyson and Mooradian (2007), Klein
and Zur (2009), Brav et al (2008) and Becht et al (2015) coherently find that hedge fund activist
campaigns has exhibited significant abnormal returns during sample periods extending to 2010.
Later research on hedge fund activism has focused on other issues, such as its impact on
innovation (Brav, et al., 2014) and productivity (Brav, et al., 2015) in target firms. Another
finding in earlier research is that the abnormal returns to hedge fund activism has declined
sharply (see Becht et al (2015, p. 26) and Brav et al. (2008)). Since their samples extend to 2010
or earlier, the question whether the trend of declining returns has persisted still remains
unanswered.
   Our main hypothesis is that the abnormal returns have continued to decline and approached
zero. Behind this hypothesis are two rationales. First, the pattern in earlier studies show a
downward trend in abnormal returns for the years prior to 2010. Second, since hedge fund
activism was a novel phenomenon, the competition in the activist market had likely not caught
up during its early years. As long as it exhibits abnormal returns, more investors should pour
their money into hedge fund activism until the abnormal returns becomes adjusted and the
market reaches its equilibrium. Brav, et al., (2008) discuss that hedge fund activism might just
be another form of arbitrage, which will disappear as the market corrects itself.
   Another question that remains unanswered by the previous literature, is whether hedge funds
with explicit activist strategies are alone in being able to generate abnormal returns by
conducting activist interventions in the U.S. There is some evidence from the European activist
market that hedge funds with other strategies than activism on average fail to realize any
abnormal returns from activist campaigns (Becht, et al., 2010). The topic has not been studied

                                                                           3
for U.S. activist interventions. Differences in laws, rules and institutions between jurisdictions
affect the characteristics of hedge fund activism in different countries and regions (Becht, et al.,
2015). Therefore, we formulate a second hypothesis, that hedge funds focused on the activist
strategy have on average generated larger abnormal returns in their activist campaigns, than
have the non-activist hedge funds.
   As there is no central database for hedge funds, we manually construct a dataset of activist
campaigns. Whenever an investor acquires more than 5% ownership in a publicly traded firm
with the intention to influence that firm, they are by federal securities law under section 13(d)
in the 1934 Exchange Act, obliged to file a beneficiary ownership report (Schedule 13D form)

                                               1
within 10 days of reaching the 5% threshold. Using the Securities and Exchange Commission’s
EDGAR Archive we search for Schedule 13D filings between 1999 and 2014.
   We also classify each hedge fund into two categories, based on their strategy. Through fund
websites, Factiva searches, and by reading Item 2 “Identity and Background” in the Schedule
13D filings we are able to categorize most of the hedge funds that have engaged in shareholder
activism. With this process we collect data on 2111 Hedge fund-target firm pairs, including 279
hedge funds and 1857 individual targets, from 1999 to 2014. The data is complemented with
firm characteristics and returns to the stocks of each target firm, from Compustat Annual and
CRSP.
   To test our hypotheses we compute cumulative abnormal returns during a 41 days event
window (-20,20) centered around the Schedule 13D filing date. We regress the average
cumulative abnormal returns over time and for individual years, to analyse the trends for
different periods in our sample. Regressions are also made for the different strategies
individually to address whether funds with an activist strategy generate larger abnormal returns
than funds with a non-activist strategy. We perform similar regression for the long-term buy
and hold returns to research whether hedge fund activism suffers from any long-term reversals.
To to accurately measure the abnormal returns in the long-term perspective, we benchmark our
target firms to a matched set of control firms. Pairing the control firms to each target firm we
calculate the one year buy-and-hold abnormal return for the targeted firms, starting from the
end of the short-term event window. If such long-term abnormal returns are close to zero, the
short-term abnormal returns can be deemed appropriate proxies of the total returns to an activist
campaign.

   Consistent with previous research we find positive abnormal returns to hedge fund activism,
and a negative trend in abnormal returns during the period 1999-2010. However, there is no
support for the hypothesis that average cumulative abnormal returns have continued to decrease
towards zero. We find that short-term cumulative abnormal returns have stabilized around 6-
9% during the years 2002-2014.
   For the whole sample period, we conclude that hedge fund activism do not suffer from any
significant long-term reversals. We study whether long-term reversals might have picked up
during the last years, but find no evidence that could support any such trend.
   Both hedge funds with activist strategies and non-activist strategies on average generate
positive abnormal returns in their activist campaigns. Regarding our second hypothesis, we do
not find any conclusive evidence that hedge funds with activist strategies outperform hedge
funds with non-activist strategies, in their respective activist interventions.
   On a more general note, we discover that the activist market is increasingly stable and
predictable. Abnormal returns and volatility in abnormal returns and were considerably lower
during the last years, compared to the first few years in our sample period. Additionally, the
number of activist campaigns was more stable.

2 Previous literature

2.1 Shareholder activism
In early literature on shareholder activism, all types of institutional activists were typically
studied. Black (1998) reasons that shareholder activism may improve corporate performance
by an increase in monitoring of management, thus reducing the agency costs of the firm. Early
studies on shareholder activism indicate that activists sometimes are able to improve corporate
governance structure (Strickland, et al., 1996) and other times not (Bebchuk, 2005). In
conclusion, researchers did not find enough evidence to conclude that shareholder activism as
a general phenomenon increases long-term performance of targeted firms.
   In order to explain the inability of shareholder activists to induce long-term performance
improvements, previous researches discussed their lack of managerial skill and short-term focus
(Lipton & Rosenblum, 1991). However, none of the early research on shareholder activism
specifically studied hedge fund activism, partly due to hedge fund activism being a more novel
phenomenon.

                                                                           5
   Hedge funds are subject to lighter regulation and have stronger financial incentives to
generate returns. They are a new breed of institutional activists that have proved to be more
successful in creating value in target firms (Brav, et al., 2010).

2.2 Hedge fund activism
The comparability of earlier work is limited by the various definitions of hedge fund activism
used by different authors. Some researchers use a narrow definition of hedge fund activism and
only include campaigns by hedge funds with an explicit activist strategy, while others use wider
definitions.
   Schedule 13D filings are used in most previous studies to identify activist campaigns in the
US. Any hedge fund that obtains a 5% stake in a target company’s equity, and has the intention
to exert influence over that company, is required to file a Schedule 13D with the Securities and
Exchange Commission (SEC), within ten days.
   Becht et al (2010) study 362 activist interventions in Europe, and divide their sample based
on the strategy of the hedge funds. 183 of the events in their sample are carried out by specialist
activist funds, which use shareholder activism as a substantial part of their investment strategy.
The rest of the activist interventions are done by “non-specialist funds”, which engage in
shareholder activism sporadically or only in special situations. They find that the funds focused
on activism on average generate cumulative abnormal returns of 6.9% over a 40 day event
window, whereas the non-specialists on average generate cumulative abnormal returns of 0.6%,
insignificantly different from zero. The authors note that the better performance of specialist
funds “may reflect the fact that they engage more frequently, and with intent to profit from their
engagement strategy”.
   While abovementioned results may seem predictable, they carry important implications for
both fund managers and scholars trying to understand hedge fund activism. If the abnormal
returns are largely dependent on the strategic focus of the hedge funds, non-specialist hedge
funds should be wary of pursuing shareholder activism, and scholars should be careful when
comparing results from studies using different definitions of what constitutes an activist event.
For example, Greenwood and Schor (2009) use a wide definition of activism and include both
interventions pursued by hedge funds with focused activist strategies and campaigns by less
specialized hedge funds. They find no improvements in target firms’ performance after activist
interventions, despite other studies having done so (Brav, et al., 2010). One explanation for
such discrepancies could be that the inclusion of a large amount of interventions carried out by

                                                                           6
non-specialist hedge funds will skew the results negatively compared to studies using more
narrow definitions of activism.
   The results of Becht et al (2010) regarding specialist activists versus non-specialists have
not been tested on U.S. data. Therefore, we divide the hedge funds in our sample in the same
way, and denote them “activists” and “non-activists”, to study the differences in abnormal
returns of their respective interventions.
   Most previous research have nonetheless supported the hypothesis that hedge fund activist
campaigns are associated with positive abnormal returns. The period from 1994-2010 has been
extensively studied by other researchers. Clifford (2008), Boyson and Mooradian (2007), Klein
and Zur (2009), Brav et al (2008) and Becht et al (2015) reach similar results when they research
hedge fund activist events during the periods 1998-2005, 1994-2005, 2001-2006, 2003-2005
and 2000-2010, respectively. Each of those studies conclude that hedge fund activists have been
able to generate abnormal returns in target firms. Abnormal returns are recorded to be
significantly positive during short-term event windows and there are no signs of long-term
reversals of those abnormal returns. Later research on hedge fund activism mostly focus on
other areas, such as its impact on innovation (Brav, et al., 2014) and productivity (Brav, et al.,
2015) in target companies.
   Brav et al. (2008) find that the average cumulative abnormal return from a hedge fund
activist campaign was about 7 % for the period 2001 to 2006. They note that the cumulative
abnormal returns in their sample dropped from 15.9 % in 2001 to 3.4 % in 2006, and discuss
the potential of activism being just another form of arbitrage that will eventually be depleted.
When more investors pour their money into activist funds, the competition for attractive targets
will increase and drive abnormal returns to zero. Assuming markets will incorporate all
available information into security prices, the potential of being targeted by an activist should
also increase the price of stocks in likely target firms, they argue.
   Becht et al (2015) use a sample of 1740 activist events from 23 countries to study the
performance of hedge fund activism in different markets. For the North American events in
their sample, they find that the short-term returns from hedge fund activism interventions have
declined from 10.5% over the period 2000-2005 to 5.8% over the period 2006-2010 (Becht, et
al., 2015, p. 26).
   One of the questions triggered by the results in previous research is thus whether hedge fund
activism will continue to exhibit diminishing returns, and whether increased competition in the
activist market will eventually reduce abnormal returns to zero. By extending our sample period

                                                                           7
from 1999 through 2014, we are able to study the trend in abnormal returns to activism during
more recent years.

2.4 Hypotheses
The trend reported in previous research shows that abnormal returns to hedge fund activism
have been declining steadily up until 2010. We predict that the abnormal returns will have
continued to decline during the subsequent years and approached zero. While the continuation
of the trend is not obvious in itself, we expect that as long as there are abnormal returns to
capture in the activist market, the competition for target firms will increase and drive abnormal
returns to zero. Our hypothesis is that the average abnormal returns to hedge fund activist
campaigns will have continued to decline during our sample period, until they reached zero.
   We also predict that campaigns conducted by activist funds have been more successful in
generating abnormal returns than those carried out by non-activist funds. Hedge funds focused
on the activist strategy have more experience of shareholder activism and should be able to
adjust lock-up provisions to better suit the activist strategy, which typically requires large
investments over long periods of time. On the other hand, it may also be that non-activist hedge
funds only act on particularly good opportunities to pursue shareholder activism, which could
compensate for their relative lack of experience. The second hypothesis is however that hedge
funds focused on the activist strategy have on average generated larger abnormal returns in
their activist campaigns, than have the non-activist funds.

3 Data

3.1 Hedge fund activist campaigns
As a first step in our data collection, we create a directory of hedge funds. Since there is no
exhaustive database of hedge funds, we use several sources to create this list. We first include
all 401 constituents in the CSFB/Tremont hedge fund index. That sample should be expected
to introduce survivorship and backfilling biases as the index only includes active and
voluntarily reporting hedge funds. To mitigate those biases we complement our list of hedge
funds by including funds found in the news articles from Factiva searches on activist
interventions. We search for articles from 1999-2014 with the text strings “Hedge fund
activism” and “Hedge fund 13D filing”, to collect identities of additional hedge funds that have
been involved in activist interventions. We confirm that all the entities that show up in the news
searches can rightly be described as hedge funds by browsing the fund’s websites and by
conducting supplementary searches on the identities of those funds.
                                                                           8
   To construct a variable that indicates whether a hedge fund has an activist strategy or a non-
activist strategy, we gather information about the strategies of each hedge fund from the news
searches, fund websites, and by looking under Item 2 “Identity and Background” in the
Schedule 13D filing. In this way we are able to classify most hedge funds as either activist or
non-activist. However, for 24 hedge funds in our sample, we do not find any classification and
instead classify them based on their frequency of activist interventions. Those remaining 24
funds, we classify as activist if they have pursued ten or more activist campaigns during our
sample period, and non-activist if they pursued fewer than ten campaigns.
   In a second step we search the Security and Exchange Commission’s EDGAR Archives for
Schedule 13D filings that have been filed by the hedge funds we listed. Any investor that
acquires a 5% stake in a target company’s equity, and has the intention of influencing the
management of the company, is required to file a Schedule 13D within ten days of crossing the
5% threshold. The question whether hedge funds truthfully disclose their activist blockholdings
has been discussed, but as Clifford (2008) notes, hedge funds have strong incentives to do so,
mainly because the management of target companies can effectively use misstatements of
intentions as a basis of lawsuits against the activists. Thus, we use the Schedule 13D filing dates
to identify initiations of activist campaigns. From the Schedule 13D forms we also obtain the
identity of the target firms.
   Finally, we match the data on hedge fund activist campaigns with the CRSP database to add
stock returns of the targeted firms. We use daily stock returns around the filing date to compute
short-term abnormal returns, and monthly holding returns until one year after the event window
to compute long-term abnormal returns subsequent to the initial market reaction. We also use
the Compustat Annual database to retrieve data on firm characteristics that we use to match
targeted firms with a control group of non-targeted firm, for the long-term event study.
   In SEC’s EDGAR database, firms are identified by a CIK-code. We drop all firms that miss
matching identifiers in the CRSP and Compustat Annual databases.

3.2 Descriptive statistics
By following the process outlined above, we collect data on 2111 activist campaigns from 1999
through 2014. The activist campaigns were conducted by 279 hedge funds, against 1857
individual target firms. Of the total activist campaigns, 621 were carried out by hedge funds
with non-activist strategies and 1490 by hedge funds with an explicit activist strategy.
   Our sample of hedge funds and target firms show similar characteristics to those collected
by previous researchers (Brav, et al., 2010). The average target firm is a small to mid-cap

                                                                           9
company with high growth rate but modest profitability. Target firms are spread widely across
industries, with the most common industry being business services. The average number of
events for each hedge fund in the sample is 7.6 (see Table 1).
   The distribution of hedge fund activist events during our sample period shows that the
activist market became increasingly busy in the years leading up to the financial crisis (Figure
1). During the subsequent years, the number of activist events have been more stable, averaging
at 140 events per year in our sample.

4. Methodology
To answer whether the abnormal returns to hedge fund activism have declined and approached
zero, we study both the trend in abnormal returns and the abnormal returns for each year during
the sample period. We do this separately for short-term and long-term abnormal returns. To
answer the question posed by our second hypothesis, whether campaigns by non-activists have
lower abnormal returns than campaigns by activists, we test for differences between the two.
   The short-term abnormal returns are computed as the difference between actual return and
predicted return based on the correlation with the S&P500 index during a one-year estimation
window preceding the event day. We construct a variable for the cumulative abnormal returns,
which sums the abnormal returns for each day in a 41 day event window (-20,20), centered
around filing date.
   For the calculation of long-term abnormal returns, appropriate risk-adjustment becomes
more critical and we therefore devise a more sophisticated estimation model. Instead of using
expected returns based on the correlation with an index, we use the returns of a matched
reference firm. Additionally, we construct a variable for the buy-and-hold abnormal returns,
which geometrically sums the returns, since compounding effects will be more substantial in
the long-term.

4.1 Short-term abnormal returns
We use the filing date of the Schedule 13D as the event date. For most campaigns, the filing
date is the date when the event is first publicly announced. To refine the event date selection,
Boyson and Mooradian (2007) used news searches to find campaigns that were publicly leaked
before the filing date and used the date of the leakage as the event date for those cases. However,
their results were largely similar when they used the filing date as event date for their entire
sample. Therefore the filing date should be considered appropriate to use as the event date.

                                                                          10
   The event window we use is set to 41 days (-20,20). Defining the event window as such, we
capture potential abnormal returns related to early leakage of information about the campaigns
as described above. Secondly, we also capture the abnormal returns that can be caused by
trading frictions around the filing date. As shown by Brav et al (2008), the abnormal trading
volumes are most significant during the ten days immediately preceding the filing date, but
continue to positive during the subsequent 20 days.
   The estimation window is set from 252 trading days before the event to 20 days before the
event. Thus, the estimation window starts one year before the filing date which makes for an
appropriate estimation of the stocks predicted performance. Interventions in target firms that
miss daily stock return data for any day during the event window, or for more than 25 days in
the estimation window are dropped from the sample.
   As a benchmark to calculate the expected returns, we use the S&P 500 index. We compared
with another benchmark by using Nasdaq Composite Index instead, which yielded similar
results. The S&P500 is nevertheless the index most commonly used by professional investors,
and should better reflect the wide range of industries in our sample, as the Nasdaq index is
disproportionately weighted towards technology firms.
   We regress the returns for each individual firm on the market return during the estimation
window to estimate a beta for the firm. We then calculate the abnormal returns as the difference
between actual returns and predicted returns, based on the estimated betas, for each day in the
event window. Abnormal returns for each day is accumulated to construct the variable CAR,

                                                                                            2
which captures the cumulative abnormal returns for the entire event window.
   We compute the average CARs for various time periods, individual years, and for the two
hedge fund strategy categories. Furthermore, we use two different types of regressions, to
determine if the average cumulative abnormal returns are declining and approaching zero. We
regress the mean of the CARs for each year in our sample period (see Table 4). We also regress
the mean of the CAR on year dummy variables, to identify trends. To compare our results with
previous literature, we do one trend regression for the years 1999-2010. To examine how the
trend has developed after the period covered by previous research, we also run a trend
regression for the years 2010-2014 (see Table 2).
   The differences in cumulative abnormal returns to interventions executed by non-activists
and activists, is studied by using t-tests (see Table 6). This is to distinguish if there is any
statistical evidence that the coefficients from the two strategies are different from one another.

4.2 Long-term reversals
To measure if short-term abnormal returns associated with an activist event are reversed over
the long-term, we calculate buy-and-hold abnormal returns for the 12 months immediately
following the short-term event window. The average holding period for hedge fund activism is
approximately 2 years (Boyson & Mooradian, 2007), which is the reason we do not use a longer
window than one year, as we want to avoid effects from a fund’s exit to largest possible extent.
   We compare the characteristics of our target firms with a matched sample of firms that have
not been targeted by activism. We use these comparable firms as a benchmark to our target
firms in order to find the effect of being targeted. Propensity score matching is used to find a

                                                                                               3
set of control firms. In order to obtain a credible estimate of the counterfactual , we match on
year, SIC-3 code and market value. In this way participation is independent on outcomes
conditional on our matching variables.
   We match on a 1-to-1 basis and use the control firms as a benchmark to calculate the buy
and hold abnormal return for the targeted firms. By calculating long-term abnormal returns
using matched firms, we alleviate the new listing, rebalancing and skewness biases associated
with calculation of abnormal returns using asset pricing models or reference portfolios. (Barber
& Lyon, 1997)
   Barber & Lyon (1997) also argue that when estimating long-run abnormal returns, the buy-
and-hold abnormal returns is favoured due to a smaller measurement bias. The buy-and-hold
abnormal returns allow for compounding effects and better mimic investor experience over

                                                            4
longer horizons than cumulative abnormal returns.
   In the same way as for the short-term abnormal returns, we regress average buy-and-hold
abnormal return for each year individually (see Table 5), and on year dummy variables to study
trends (see Table 3). We also do a t-test to analyse differences between long-term BHARs for
activist and non-activist funds (Table 7).

more prevalent during the latter years in our sample period. The coefficient for the trend during
the period 2010-2014 is positive, but insignificantly different from zero, implying that short-
term abnormal returns can still be considered adequate proxies of total returns to activist
interventions.
   Table 4 reports the trends in short-term CARs to activist campaigns. The trend during 1999-
2010 is significantly negative at -0.5% per year, which corresponds to the trend observed in
previous literature. For the subsequent years, 2010-2014, the trend is however insignificant.
Figure 4, which plots the mean of CARs for each year, furthermore shows that the negative
trend is mostly attributable to the particularly high returns during 1999-2001, when the mean
of CARs were higher than 10% for all years. Since 2002, the mean of CARs for each year have
been stable around 6-9%, with the only outlying year being 2012. Not only have the CARs for
each year levelled out at a lower rate than before, the volatility of CARs within each year have
also been reduced. For the last four years the standard deviation of CARs have been between
0.20-24, which can be compared to the standard deviation of 0.54 during year 2000.
   The results conclusively reject our main hypothesis that abnormal returns to hedge fund
activism have declined and approached zero. A negative trend persisted for the early years in
our sample period, but for the last 12 years the abnormal returns have levelled out at a lower
rate than before.

5.2 Activist and non-activist strategy hedge fund
Long-term BHARs for hedge funds with non-activist strategies are insignificant just as for the
activist hedge funds (see Table 5). In other words, we find no conclusive evidence that
campaigns by the non-activist funds are subject to long-term reversals in any greater extent than
the campaigns by the activist funds. However, the coefficient for the non-activist BHARs is
negative, whereas it is positive for the activist BHARs. This could indicate that the activist
hedge funds are on average better at sustaining the initial value creation in target firms, although
it cannot be statistically confirmed.
   From Table 4 we note that the short-term CARs from the interventions by non-activists are
on average 6.9%, whereas the short-term CARs from interventions by activists are on average
8.6%, both significant at the 1% level. Table 7 reports the differences in short-term CARs
between campaigns by activist hedge funds and non-activists, and the results show that there is
no significant difference between the two.
   Our second hypothesis, that hedge funds with non-activists strategies on average fail to
realize the same level of abnormal returns as the activist hedge funds, has little support in our
data. To the contrary, non-activist hedge funds are on average able to generate similar abnormal
returns as the hedge funds with a focused activist strategy. However, these results are somewhat
less conclusive than the results on our first hypothesis. It could be that some of the initial value
creation is reversed in the average campaign executed by hedge funds with non-activist
strategies.

6 Conclusions and implications
Both hedge funds with an explicit activist strategy and hedge funds with other strategies are on
average able to realize abnormal returns from pursuing activist campaigns. This stands in
contrast to previous research on the European activist market, where non-activist hedge funds
on average fail to realize any abnormal returns when they sporadically pursue activist
campaigns. The reasons for this is most likely the differences in laws, rules and institutions
between jurisdictions in the different countries and regions discussed in Becht, et al. (2015).
However, for U.S. hedge fund managers of non-activist funds, our results signal that ventures
into shareholder activism might be worth considering.
   The distribution of events during our sample period undermines one of the underlying
assumptions behind our main hypothesis, that returns in the activist market will decline and
approach zero. Both in previous research, which focuses mainly on the period leading up to the

                                                                          14
                                                        5
financial crisis, and more recent media reports , there are frequent suggestions that a growing
herd of hedge fund activists are increasing their efforts to go after target firms. In contrast, our
results indicate that the activist market has reached an equilibrium. The number of campaigns,
the short-term CARs and the volatility in short-term CARs have all levelled out at a lower rate
than before. In contrast to individual activist interventions, which are often characterised by
high levels of uncertainty, the aggregate activist market is better described as increasingly
mature and predictable.
   Despite the fact that prevalence of significant abnormal returns, fund managers are not
rushing to compete in the activist market. One likely explanation for this are the substantial
costs that can be associated with shareholder activism, as described by Gantchev (2013). The
costs of for example launching a proxy battle, may be so high that the average net abnormal
returns to the investors in the funds are in fact close to zero. The robustness of abnormal returns
combined with the relative absence of competition certainly support the notion that net
abnormal returns to hedge fund activism are low. Thus, in the typical activist campaign, the
initial shareholders are the only ones that will be unambiguously better off, since they benefit
from the value creation without having to bear the costs. Regulators should take this in account
when considering stricter control of hedge funds.
   There are no strong reasons to believe that abnormal returns to hedge fund activism will take
any different turn in the future. To better understand the activist market, future research might
focus on the supply side of the activist market. Although target firm’s operative and financial
characteristics have been thoroughly researched, more work could be done to map out the
defence tactics in target firms and their relative likeliness to succeed in repelling hedge fund
activist interventions.

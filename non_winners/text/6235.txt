
Unveiling the Puzzle of ESG Investing: An Analysis of ESG-Efficient Frontiers
Across U.S. Macroeconomic Periods

Abstract:

   We examine the interaction between Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG)
   factors and stock performance in the U.S. from 2002 to 2022, with a focus on
   analysing the differences between periods of economic expansions and recessions.
   Using a dataset of S&P 500 constituents, we construct ESG-Sharpe ratio (SR)
   frontiers based on the framework proposed by Pedersen et al. (2021). Our analysis of
   expansions reveals that the benefit of incorporating ESG information in portfolio
   selection diminishes over time, while the cost of non-pecuniary ESG preferences
   increases. However, we are unable to clearly assess similar effects during recessions,
   mainly due to the randomness of outcomes observed in those periods.

Keywords:

   ESG, ESG Investing, ESG-Efficient Frontier, Recessions, Expansions, ESG-SR
  Frontier, ESG-CAPM

1. Introduction
In recent decades, practitioners and academics alike have demonstrated a soaring interest in
the two-way relationship between financial markets and the wide range of topics
encompassed by Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) concerns. The concept was
initially introduced in a report in 2004 titled “Who Cares Wins”, compiled as a joint initiative
between The U.N. Global Compact, the Swiss Government and twenty financial institutions
from various countries.
       Despite extensive research, findings in this domain remain ambiguous and appear
sensitive to methodological choices, including time periods and proxies for ESG concerns.
This study contributes to the literature by examining whether the costs and benefits of ESG
investing vary across macroeconomic conditions, focusing on the U.S. stock market.
Specifically, we analyse how ESG considerations impact returns in expansions and
recessions, as defined by the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER).
       We build on the theoretical framework of Pedersen, Fitzgibbons, and Pomorski
(2021), which quantifies the costs and benefits of ESG investing using an ESG-efficient
frontier. Pedersen et al. (2021) also propose an ESG-CAPM model that explains how returns
depend on whether investors price in ESG preferences or future profit predictions. Extending
this framework, we address the following research question:

  How do the costs and benefits of incorporating ESG factors into investment decisions vary
 across different macroeconomic periods and over time, and what are the key drivers of these
                                        variations?

By using Standard & Poor (S&P) 500 constituents from 2002 to 2022, we calculate
ESG-efficient frontiers and examine Sharpe ratios under two scenarios: one allowing
short-selling and the other prohibiting it. We estimate expected excess returns using a
simplified adaptation of Pedersen et al. (2021), with 24-month rolling averages of historic
monthly correlation coefficients. Risk expectations are derived from daily stock return
covariance matrices with diagonal shrinkage. Optimal portfolio weights are computed to
maximize Sharpe ratios for different ESG scores. Additionally, we perform pooled
regressions with time-fixed effects and clustered standard errors at the firm-level to assess the
relationship between ESG scores and firm fundamentals under varying market conditions.
During expansions, we observe a negative relationship between ESG proxies and both
expected and realised Sharpe ratios. Over time, the cost of tilting portfolios towards higher
ESG scores increases, reflecting improved market efficiency as more ESG information is
priced in. Furthermore, the realised Sharpe ratio advantage of ESG-aware investors
diminishes over time.
       We find that reliably assessing the costs of incorporating non-pecuniary ESG
preferences during recessions is challenging due to the high randomness and unpredictability
of outcomes. Similarly, the benefits of incorporating ESG information into portfolio selection
during recessions are difficult to assess. Nonetheless, we do find some support that using
ESG information is beneficial during such periods as ESG-aware investors overall seem to
achieve higher realised Sharpe ratios than ESG-unaware investors.
       While our findings do not resolve the ambiguities in the ESG literature, they provide
valuable and unique insights into the varying effects of ESG investing across business cycles.
The results emphasise the need for more sophisticated models to estimate expected returns
and risks.
       The remainder of this thesis is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews existing
literature pertinent to this study. Section 3 outlines the theoretical framework that forms the
foundation of our analysis and hypotheses. Section 4 describes the data and scope of our

empirical analysis, including any adjustments made to the dataset. Section 5 details the

2. Literature Review

2.1. Literature Review
This paper aligns most closely with the strand of academic literature on sustainable finance
that examines the performance of responsible investing approaches, specifically relating to
macroeconomic conditions and investor behaviour and beliefs. While concepts such as ESG,
Socially Responsible Investing (SRI), and impact investing are often used interchangeably,
they differ in focus and underlying motives (Starks, 2023). This paper focuses specifically on
ESG investing, considered driven by both pecuniary and non-pecuniary preferences of
individual investors.

2.1.1. ESG and Stock Performance
We contribute to the literature by incorporating a macroeconomic time dimension to the
existing, often conflicting, body of research on ESG and performance. Among the papers
suggesting that ESG lowers expected returns, most follow the early findings of Merton
(1987) on market segmentation, attributing these effects to divestment and exclusionary
screenings, which are linked to Becker's (1971) early economic theories of discriminatory
tastes. Among these studies, Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) empirically demonstrate that “sin”
stocks generate positive abnormal returns. In contrast, Edmans (2011) finds that certain
intangible factors, such as employee satisfaction, have contributed to outperformance over
the long term.

2.1.2. ESG and Stock Performance Across Time
Literature incorporating the time dimension includes a recent paper by Bansal, Wu, and
Yaron (2022), which investigates the time variability of alpha generated from Socially
Responsible Investing. Their main finding is that highly rated SRI stocks outperform during
strong economic periods but underperform during downturns. Other papers include Engle,
Giglio, Kelly, Lee, and Stroebel (2020), who provide a methodology for constructing hedging
portfolios for climate risk based on how publicly traded assets respond to climate change
news, using third-party ESG scores as proxies for a firm’s exposure to climate risk.
Furthermore, Lins, Servaes, and Tamayo (2017) demonstrate that firms with high levels of
Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) outperformed during the 2008-2009 financial crisis.

2.1.3. ESG and Investor Dynamics
In addition, there exists a body of literature focused on theories and studies related to investor
dynamics. For example, Pástor, Stambaugh, and Taylor (2021) propose a theory on the
interplay between investors’ ESG preferences and market equilibrium, demonstrating how
agents' prevailing preferences influence the returns of green holdings. Further on this topic,
Brøgger and Kronies (2020) document a significant difference in the returns to sustainable
investing across investor types, arising from some investors’ ability to exploit the strict
non-pecuniary preferences of others. In addition to examining the relationship between ESG
factors and investor demand, we incorporate a macroeconomic time dimension.
       Our paper also connects to the literature on unconditional versus conditional CAPM,
including research by Jagannathan and Wang (1996). Following the framework of different

                                                        2
investor types in Pedersen et al. (2021), investors vary in how they believe ESG information
influences a firm’s risk exposure over time when forming their future return expectations.

2.1.4. Conceptual Framework
In this paper, we apply the ESG-efficient frontier framework proposed by Pedersen et al.
(2021) to conceptualise and quantify the costs and benefits of ESG investing. In addition to
using different proxies, we extend their framework by adjusting for variability across
macroeconomic conditions. Thus, we contribute an empirical analysis of how ESG investing
integrates with different market environments.

2.2. Contributions to Literature
Our contributions to existing literature are fourfold. Firstly, we extend the sample period of
Pedersen et al. (2021) to capture additional market dynamics influenced by recent global
events such as the COVID-19 pandemic. Secondly, we calculate the ex-ante and realised
ESG-efficient frontiers for different macroeconomic periods, allowing for a comparison of
the financial costs and benefits of ESG preferences in terms of the Sharpe ratio. Thirdly, we
perform scenario analysis by adjusting our optimisation model to different levels of permitted
short-selling, aiming to produce more realistic and practical results for both institutional and
retail investors. Lastly, we investigate the performance of ESG investing in terms of stock
returns over time, analysing how firms' profitability, demand and valuation fluctuate with
changing macroeconomic conditions and how these insights help explain the cost of an ESG
preference.

3. Theoretical Framework

3.1. Three Types of Investors
Following Pedersen et al. (2021), we assume that the investment universe comprises three
types of investors, each with different ESG preferences, which in turn influence their
individual portfolio optimisation problems.

3.1.1. The ESG-unaware Investor (Type-U)
An ESG-unaware investor is motivated by pecuniary considerations and are unaware of ESG
scores. These investors do not incorporate ESG considerations when constructing their
portfolios, adhering instead to the standard mean-variance trade-off framework introduced by
Markowitz (1952). Depending on the informational value of ESG data, this omission may
place them at an informational disadvantage relative to investors who integrate ESG factors
into their decision-making (Pedersen et al., 2021).

3.1.2. The ESG-aware Investor (Type-A)
Similarly, ESG-aware investors are also motivated by pecuniary motives but incorporate ESG
scores to enhance their information during portfolio construction. As a result, they are never
worse off than Type-U investors in terms of mean-variance optimisation and may have an
advantage depending on the informational value of ESG data (Pedersen et al., 2021).

3.1.3. The ESG-motivated Investor (Type-M)
Akin to Type-A investors, Type-M investors also utilise ESG scores to enhance their
information. However, unlike Type-A investors, they also have non-pecuniary motives
towards ESG and incorporate these preferences when constructing their portfolios (Pedersen
et al., 2021).

                                                        3
3.2. The ESG Efficient Frontier
Pedersen et al. (2021) describe the risk-return trade-off using the Sharpe ratio and combine it
with ESG considerations, creating what they denote as the ESG-SR frontier, which is
independent of any investor preferences.
       The tangency portfolio using ESG information, represents the portfolio with the
highest attainable Sharpe ratio and is equivalent to the tangency portfolio (all assets) on the
standard mean-variance frontier (Markowitz, 1952), thus representing the peak of the frontier.
Furthermore, the ESG-SR frontier is hump-shaped, as selecting a portfolio with an ESG score
different from the optimal one in terms of Sharpe ratio effectively imposes an asset
constraint, thereby lowering the maximum attainable Sharpe ratio.
       An ESG-aware investor will choose the tangency portfolio, utilising all available
security information, where ESG scores may provide informational value. ESG-motivated
investors will select a portfolio to the right of the tangency portfolio, located on the
ESG-efficient frontier. These investors, therefore, sacrifice some Sharpe ratio to achieve a
greener portfolio, with the magnitude depending on the strength of the individual investor's
ESG preferences. Lastly, ESG-unaware investors may choose a suboptimal portfolio, as they
condition their portfolio optimisation on less information, assuming that ESG scores indeed
provide informational value. If ESG scores do not offer such value, both ESG-unaware and
ESG-aware investors will choose the tangency portfolio.

3.3. Investor Dynamics & ESG-CAPM
Pedersen et al. (2021) demonstrate that an ESG-adjusted Capital Asset Pricing Model can
explain expected returns. In an economy with a significant proportion of ESG-unaware
investors, and where high ESG scores predict high future profitability, high-ESG stocks
deliver higher expected returns because most investors optimise their portfolios without
incorporating this information. Conversely, in a market dominated by ESG-aware investors,
these investors bid up the prices of high-ESG stocks until an equilibrium is reached, where
the prices fully reflect their expected profitability. Furthermore, in an economy with many
ESG-motivated investors, high-ESG stocks provide lower expected returns, as these investors
bid up prices beyond equilibrium, accepting reduced returns in exchange for a greener
portfolio.

3.4. Hypothesis Development
The insights from the preceding sections form the basis for our empirical analysis, leading to
the following hypotheses:

H1: The cost of having an ESG preference when investing will be more pronounced during
recessions.
       This should be reflected by a steeper slope in the ESG-SR frontiers during such
periods. Specifically, for each standard deviation increase in ESG scores, the reduction in
expected excess returns will be greater during recessions. We believe this is because the
utility ESG-motivated investors derive from ESG considerations diminishes during periods of
heightened financial uncertainty, causing their behaviour to more closely align with that of
ESG-aware investors.

H2: Over time, the benefits associated with incorporating ESG information during periods of
economic expansion are expected to diminish, suggesting increasing market efficiency.
       As the emphasis on ESG intensifies with the transition to a greener economy, we
hypothesise that the equilibrium during expansions will be one in which ESG-aware investors
dominate. Consequently, the benefits of incorporating ESG information will diminish as ESG

                                                        4
factors become integrated into asset valuations, even if a subset of investors does not directly
incorporate ESG data (Pedersen et al., 2021). This equilibrium of market efficiency will
effectively reduce any informational advantages.

4.1. ESG Proxy Data
For brevity, we base our empirical research on two proxies for Environmental, Social, and
Governance (ESG) concerns: an overall ESG score and a governance measure. The data is
retrieved from the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) Eikon, one of the industry’s more
                                                 1
prominent third-party ESG score providers. Despite Pedersen et al. (2021) using MSCI ESG
ratings, we opted for LSEG Eikon ESG scores due to their longer time series, with data
available from 2002, whereas MSCI's more comprehensive ESG measure dates back only to
the beginning of 2007 (MSCI Inc., 2020).
       In a study by Berg, Koelbel, and Rigobon (2022), they document a significant
discrepancy between the ESG ratings issued by six leading ESG rating agencies. The
pairwise correlation between these ratings ranges from 38% to 71%, with divergence arising
from differences in measurement, scope, and weighting. As such, reliance on LSEG as the
sole provider introduces the risk that using a different provider could lead to different results
and conclusions. Additionally, there are inherent risks in using ESG proxies to capture
real-world dynamics. However, unlike Pedersen et al. (2021), who base their governance
proxy on accounting accruals, the LSEG Governance Score incorporates multiple
measurements, potentially capturing a broader range of factors that investors might consider
in governance assessments.

estimate the expected return models and approximate the return premia in a simplified
manner, while remaining as close as possible to the methodology outlined by Pedersen et al.

       We assume that investors form their expectations regarding the general equity risk and
return premia based on observations from the previous 24 months. We argue that this is a
reasonable assumption due to the tendency of investors to form their future expectations
based on past data, as proposed by He & Shen (2010). Moreover, we believe companies may
potentially change too much on a fundamental level to reasonably form future expectations
based on data from more than 24 months ago.

respective stock’s one-month-lagged book-to-market z-scores for each month. For the
ESG-aware investor, we add the one-month-lagged ESG z-score of stocks as an independent

variable to the regression.   We apply lagged values to avoid the possibility of reverse
causality as the company’s book-to-market score is directly affected by its stock price and,
therefore, its return.
       The ordinary least squares (OLS) regression model for the ESG-unaware investor,

To compute expected risk, Pedersen et al. (2021) use the Barra US Equity Risk Model

month based on observations from the previous 24 months. Thus, for each month, a
covariance matrix is computed based on daily stock returns for our S&P 500 constituents

all months to ensure comparability. Through a trial-and-error approach, we determined that

For every month, based on the expected risk and return per investor type, we calculate the
                                                                                                                           13
optimal portfolio weights that maximise the Sharpe ratio for each portfolio ESG score.
When not imposing any short-selling constraints, we set the maximum negative and positive

equivalent to not restricting short-selling, while also representing a highly unrealistic
scenario. When short-selling is restricted, the maximum weight per stock is set to the full size
of the portfolio.
       Using the weights of the respective optimal portfolios, we calculate the realised
Sharpe ratios for each portfolio ESG score. Akin to Pedersen et al. (2021), we use the
monthly excess returns and daily stock returns for the respective month. The ESG-SR
frontiers for a given period are calculated by creating two separate plots: one showing the
average ex-ante maximum Sharpe ratios and the other displaying the average realised Sharpe
ratios. Both metrics are determined for each portfolio's ESG score and investor type,
averaged across all months within the specified period.

6. Empirical Findings & Discussion

6.1. Overview of Findings
Based on our study parameters and empirical findings, we draw the following inferences
regarding our research question and hypotheses:
       Addressing our first hypothesis, we show that forming reliable ex-ante estimates of
the cost associated with incorporating an ESG preference during recessions is challenging
due to the high randomness of outcomes. Similarly, the benefits of incorporating ESG
information into portfolio selection during recessions are unpredictable. The financial cost of
integrating non-pecuniary ESG motives can only be reliably calculated in expansionary
periods, and is consistently higher than expected across both proxies, further amplified when
short-selling is restricted. Notably, this cost increases over time, supporting the market
efficiency hypothesis: as more information becomes available and market participants adjust
their strategies accordingly, deviations from it become progressively more costly.
       To address our second hypothesis, we analyse the ESG-SR frontiers during
expansionary periods as well as the results from our regression models. Our findings show
that, although the overall shape and form of the frontiers are similar to those reported by
Pedersen et al. (2021), our ESG proxies have a negative impact on both expected and realised
risk-adjusted returns. Our quantitative analysis reveals a diminishing advantage of
incorporating ESG over time, assuming each investor selects their respective ex-ante optimal
portfolio. In the initial expansion period, Type-A investors achieve a higher Sharpe ratio than
Type-U investors. However, this premium gradually declines, with the most recent expansion
showing nearly identical performance between the two types. The decline in the
informational advantage of ESG supports the hypothesis that market efficiency improves
over time, diminishing the potential for Type-A investors to outperform.
       Thus, as an overarching response to our research question, our empirical findings
support the notion that the costs and benefits of incorporating ESG factors into investment
decisions vary across different business cycles and over time. While our study provides
valuable insights into the drivers behind these variations, it leaves several unresolved issues,
particularly regarding recessions, where our results remain ambiguous and warrant further
research.

7. Limitations
General limitations apply to our results, as they are constrained by our sample period, sample
stocks and selected ESG proxies. The difference between our ESG-SR frontiers and those
computed by Pedersen et al. (2021) highlights the significance of the chosen ESG proxy.

7.1. Impact of Shrinkage on Covariance Matrix Estimation
The main limitation of our analysis is the degree of shrinkage applied to the covariance
matrix. A shrinkage factor of 0.9 increases the variance of individual assets towards one
while reducing the covariance between assets towards zero. This directly affects the selection
of optimal portfolio weights and therefore likely our findings.

7.2. Limitations of Expected Return Estimations
While Section 6.5.1. demonstrates that our ESG proxies effectively capture historical effects
on excess returns and that we made reasonable assumptions regarding investors’ future return
expectations, there are limitations highlighted by other studies that could affect the validity
and interpretation of our results. Beyond fundamental critique, such as Miller’s (1977)
argument that assuming all investors (of a certain type) have identical return expectations in
an uncertain future is implausible, several papers offer more nuanced implications.
       For instance, Ozoguz (2009) argues that increased uncertainty about the economy
correlates with lower asset valuations and higher expected returns, as investors demand
higher risk premiums. Our model does not account for this, particularly during recessions,
which are characterised by heightened uncertainty.
       However, we would expect this effect to manifest in our ex-post ESG-SR frontiers,
with realised Sharpe ratios being consistently lower than ex-ante expectations. This holds for
both ESG proxies when short-selling is not allowed. Yet, when short-selling is permitted, we
observe that the maximum realised Sharpe ratios during recession periods exceed ex-ante
expectations. One possible explanation is that this effect is more pronounced when investors’

                                                       26
beliefs are more sensitive to new information (Ozoguz, 2009). Gomez-Cram (2021) suggests
that investors tend to react with delay to recessions, which supports our decision to estimate
expected returns using a historical rolling average.
       While we argue that our model is adequate for addressing our research question, we
acknowledge limitations, primarily the assumption that investors do not incorporate
forward-looking or macroeconomic information into their beliefs.

8. Concluding Remarks and Suggestions for Future Research
This study examines the relationship between ESG and stock performance, focusing on its
evolution over time and the influence of the macroeconomic environment to assess the
implications of ESG integration in investment decisions. Using S&P 500 constituents and
data from 2002 to 2022, we quantify the costs and benefits of ESG, exploring its impact
during recessions and expansions. We also test potential explanations for the observed results
and assess whether markets have efficiently priced in ESG information over time.
       We find a consistent relationship between ESG and stock performance during
expansionary periods, but this becomes noisy during recessions, with some correlation among
investors. While the cost-benefit analysis offers practical insights for investors in
expansionary periods, no robust conclusions can be drawn for recessions. Moreover, our
findings indicate that the value of incorporating ESG information has decreased over time,
pointing to a possible market saturation or adaptation to ESG metrics, which aligns with the
efficient market hypothesis.
       Our research contributes to understanding ESG’s interplay with stock performance
across business cycles and over time. However, the difficulty in reconciling our results with
the disparate literature suggests that ESG and stock performance remain poorly understood
and evolving, with no consensus reached. As stated in the introduction, the findings remain
ambiguous and seem sensitive to study parameters. Therefore, the puzzle is far from resolved,
and further research is needed. The noisy and chaotic relationship between ESG and the
Sharpe ratio during recessions also warrants deeper investigation. Additionally, the negative
excess returns for both high and low ESG scores suggest that trading based on ESG proxies
alone is insufficient to hedge against market downturns, opening avenues for future research.
Lastly, while our study provides insights into the underlying causes, it is geographically
limited to the U.S. stock market. Future research on European markets, where ESG
integration is more advanced, could offer valuable comparisons and reveal different dynamics
in the relationship.

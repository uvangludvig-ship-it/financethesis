Beyond Credit Ratings

The Role of (E)SG in Sovereign Debt Investments

Oscar Wollmann
Filip Frisk
Bachelor Thesis
Stockholm School of Economics
2023
Beyond Credit Ratings: The Role of (E)SG in Sovereign Debt
Investments
Abstract:
The study investigates the correlation between ESG performance and sovereign
bond yield spreads using regression analysis. The results reveal a significant neg-
ative correlation between the Governance and Social indices and bond spreads,
emphasising the importance of good governance practices and social stability in
reducing the risk of sovereign debt default. However, the Environmental index
does not exhibit a significant correlation, possibly due to a time-horizon mismatch
and limited selection of environmental variables. Integrating ESG performance,
particularly Governance and Social factors, alongside traditional credit ratings can
provide investors with a comprehensive view of risk in sovereign debt investments.
Monitoring ESG signals and excluding the worst ESG performers from portfolios
may help reduce volatility while maintaining portfolio characteristics. The study
acknowledges limitations, such as time horizon mismatch, omitted variable bias,
and data quality, suggesting avenues for future research to address these limita-
tions and enhance the robustness of the analysis.

Keywords:
ESG rating, Sovereign debt investments, Sovereign bond yields, Sustainability

1 Introduction

1.1 Background
ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) investing is becoming increasingly
popular among investors. ESG investments consider the environmental impact,
social responsibility, and governance aspects of the investment. According to a
report by PwC (2022), institutional investment in ESG-focused products is ex-
pected to rise by 84% to SEK 346.5 trillion between 2021 and 2026 with a CAGR
of approximately 13%. Investments in ESG products are then expected to account
for approximately 22% of total global assets under management.
To enable investors to account for ESG factors, regulators have enforced audited
reporting demands on corporations. In Sweden, corporate sustainability reporting
has been mandatory, from previously being voluntary, by the Annual Accounts
Act since 2016 for larger companies Swedish Agency for Growth Policy Analysis
(2018). The regulation is adopted from the EU Non-Financial Reporting Direc-
tive (NFRD) in 2014 European Parliament and Council (2014). The regulatory
demand for non-financial reporting has increased, especially in Europe. For ex-
ample, one recently adopted framework was the EU Taxonomy in 2020 European
Commission (2021). The increasing awareness regarding non-financial reporting
is also noticeable through articles such as ”Accounting for Climate Change” by
Kaplan and Ramanna (2021) in Harvard Business Review. The article extends cri-
tique against the catch-all non-financial corporate reporting and suggests further
improving and extending the current framework with so-called E-liabilities.
Today it is not only regulators that have an extensive focus on ESG. All the major
rating agencies today provide ESG ratings on firms, regions, and sectors Deloitte
(2021). However, when comparing methodologies employed by different agencies,
it becomes increasingly evident that the space is immature and that multiple
different methodologies and data sources are used.
In addition to the increased focus on ESG by regulators and rating agencies for
corporate information, investors are also paying more attention to the ESG creden-
tials of fixed-income instruments. There exist many different types of fixed-income
instruments, but one important type is government bonds through sovereign is-
suers which represent 50 % of the fixed-income market according to a report made
by World Bank (2020).
This investor attention has led to the development of new themed financial prod-
ucts such as green bonds which according to International Finance Corporation
(2016) was first issued in 2007 by the European Investment Bank dedicated to
renewable energy and energy efficiency projects. Another example is social bonds

                                   2
issued by Social Finance (2021) with the objective of reducing the reoffending rate
among short-sentenced offenders leaving Peterborough prison.
While issuers of corporate financial instruments deal with natural regulatory pres-
sure related to ESG reporting, the same is not true for the vastly larger market for
instruments issued by sovereigns. The lack of regulation among sovereigns leads
to a lack of accountability and transparency in reporting ESG data on their issued
financial instruments. This in turn makes it difficult for sovereign debt investors
to incorporate ESG factors into their investment thesis.
There have been multiple efforts to solve this problem by creating an ESG rating
for government bonds by utilising UN-associated World Bank public database to
create country-level ESG indices as a proxy for the issuers’ ESG performance.
Pineau, Le, and Estran (2022) investigated the importance of ESG factors in
sovereign credit ratings, Crifo, Diaye, and Oueghlissi (2017) did similar investi-
gations for sovereign borrowing costs and Gibson and Krueger (2019) for sovereign
bond yield spreads. However, there are only a few rating agencies which provide
these ratings to the market, one example being the Institutional Shareholder Ser-
vices Group of Companies (ISS). Asset managers reports such as Morgan Stanley
Investment Management (2021), Franklin Templeton (2022) and AllianceBernstein
(2021) also tried to establish their own ratings using undisclosed methodologies.
The hardship of incorporating ESG considerations in investment analysis for fixed-
income investments is concerning when considering the United Nations Principles
for Responsible Investment (UN PRI) which have been signed by 3,000 institutions,
representing approximately SEK 1,000 trillion assets under management according
to World Bank (2020). The signatory asset managers, who have a large portion
of their portfolio in government bonds according to an institutional investment
report by Allianz Global Investors (2017), need to improve their integration ESG
data into their investment decision in order to rightfully claim that the UN PRI
is honoured.

1.2 Research questions
In light of the above, it is interesting to further investigate how macro-level ESG
data can be used in sovereign fixed-income debt instruments. To answer this, the
following research question has been studied:
Is there a historic correlation between country-level ESG factors and government
bond yield spreads for OECD countries?

                                   3
1.3 Methodology
To answer the research questions a set of regressions were performed. The depen-
dent variable was always the 10-year government bond yield spreads of the OECD
countries (excluding Estonia, Mexico, and Turkey due to missing bond yield data)
in 2015. The US bond yield was used as a reference point, to calculate the spread.
As independent variables, we used Environmental, Social, and Government indices
created from associated variables from the world bank public database. These vari-
ables are summarised in Table 1. A method called Principal Component Analysis
(PCA) was used to create the indices from the raw ESG data. A set of control
variables were also used in the regressions to check the robustness of the results.
More details about the selection of variables and the creation of indices can be
found in the methodology section.

1.4 Results
From the regression, we found that both the Social and Governance indices have
significant negative correlations with the yield spreads. This means that for coun-
tries with strong Social and Governance performance, the yields tend to be lower
and vice versa. However, the Environmental variable was not found to be signifi-
cantly correlated with the yield spreads.

1.5 Interpretation
These results imply that ESG performance, particularly in the Social and Gover-
nance dimensions, can provide an alternative and multi-dimensional view of risk,
complementing traditional credit ratings when considering sovereign debt invest-
ments. The potential long time horizon until the materialisation of ESG risk factors
emphasises the importance of actively monitoring and evaluating early warning sig-
nals to anticipate potential impacts on the credit risk profile of sovereign issuers.
Additionally, investors should be aware of the non-linear relationship between ESG
performance and yield spreads. This non-linear relationship could make investors
consider the exclusion of the worst ESG performers to reduce portfolio volatility
without significantly altering its other characteristics.

2 Main Empirical Analysis

This section will serve as a detailed description and motivation of the method-
ology applied to answer the research questions. The methodology contains four
main steps, 2.1 Delimitation, 2.2 Data gathering, 2.3 Creation of indices, and 2.4
Regression analysis.

                                   4
2.1 Delimitations
2.1.1 10-year government bond
The chosen fixed-income instrument was 10-year government bonds. The 10-year
government bond was chosen as they are widely used as a benchmark for pricing
other financial assets and also has easily available data for many countries.

2.1.2 OECD countries
The analysis was delimited to OECD countries. This decision was mainly driven
by the fact that OECD countries have frequent and available reporting of both
ESG-variables and government bond-yield spreads. Moreover, in order for the
correlation mentioned in the research question to appear, the studied countries
need to have a functioning bond market. It’s reasonably safe to assume that
this is the case for the majority of OECD countries. Moreover, this delimitation
is also motivated from an asset management perspective since investments into
sovereign debt of OECD countries are more frequent in asset managers’ portfolios
than investments into non-OECD countries according to public trading platform
TradingView (2023).

2.1.3 Single year case study
The analysis is a single year case study, rather than a time-series analysis due to
the low quality of data on an aggregate level. 2015 was selected since it had the
fewest data points missing for both bond yield data and ESG data.

2.2 Data gathering
2.2.1 ESG variables
The ESG performance data were retrieved from three different databases aggre-
gated and hosted by World Bank. The set of variables was chosen based on the
previous research by Crifo et al. (2017). Variables were collected from three differ-
ent databases World Development Indicators (WDI), World Governance Indicators
(WGI) and Environment Social and Governance (ESG). The full list of variables
and references are found in Table 1.
The database consisted of thousands of variables, from these we collected 25 vari-
ables. Firstly, we collected six Environmental variables, such as air quality and
forest area, where high performance in these variables may positively correlate
to long-term commitment and willingness to pay off debt Eaton and Gersovitz
(1981). Secondly, we collected seven Social variables, such as school enrolment

                                   5
and life expectancy, which according to Lins, Servaes, and Tamayo (2017) is re-
garded as a long-term commitment and therefore may correlate with willingness
to pay off debt. Lastly, we collected six governance variables, such as Rule of Law
and Regulatory Quality which are shown to be positively associated with economic
performance according to Kaufmann, Kraay, and Mastruzzi (2010) and Butler and
Fauver Butler and Fauver.

2.2.2 Control variables
We also include several control variables in our analysis. The selection of vari-
ables was again inspired by Crifo et al. (2017) which chose variables considered
as determinants of sovereign risk according to Afonso, Arghyrou, and Kontonikas
(2009), Barbosa and Costa (2010) and EU institute European Stability Mechanism
(2018).
Firstly, the GDP growth rate was chosen, which is an indicator of the evolution
of a country’s wealth; A high GDP growth indicates that the country will grow in
economic strength and the debt burden will consequently become easier to bear in
the future.
Secondly, the inflation rate was chosen as a control variable acting as a proxy for
a country’s monetary policy according to Attinasi, Checherita, and Nickel (2009),
there are two opposing consequences of inflation on sovereign risk. One perspective
is that higher inflation rates can broaden the tax base of a nation and reduce the
real value of outstanding domestic currency-denominated debt. This can ease
financial constraints and lower bond margins on borrowing in foreign currencies.
Conversely, higher inflation rates are associated with increased macroeconomic
instability and could negatively impact a country’s creditworthiness if they exceed
certain levels.
Thirdly, variables are selected to evaluate the fiscal condition of a country: total
public debt and primary balance in relation to GDP. A higher level of debt and
larger fiscal deficits suggest a higher risk of default according to Attinasi et al.
(2009), Sgherri and Zoli (2010) and Gruber and Kamin (2005).
Fourthly, the current account of the balance of payments is used as a control
variable. This variable is an indicator of the ability to raise funds for debt servicing
and a high balance should imply a negative impact on spreads. The expected
impact of the current account balance is negative. Lastly, the two last control
variables were total reserves in months of import and trade as a percentage of
GDP. Reserves should have a positive impact on debt repayment ability and higher
trade activity should increase a country’s ability to refinance its present stock of
debt.

2.3 Index creation
By combining the data for each collected variable into indices, it is possible to
answer the question of whether Environmental, Social, or Governance viewed as a
concept is correlated to bond yield spreads, not just whether a specific variable is
correlated to the yield spreads.
There exist a few ESG rating indices published by actors, such as Institutional
Shareholder Services Group of Companies (ISS) (2021) and Crifo et al. (2017).
Despite their use, these ratings’ validity and dependability have been questioned
Chatterji, Levine, and Toffel (2009). According to Gonenc and Scholtens (2017),
the majority of ESG ratings reflect policy rather than real reductions of environ-
mental or social consequences and associated risks. Additionally, even though the
ratings cover a wide range of potentially important concerns, it is frequently un-
clear what is actually measured. Moreover, according to Halbritter and Dorfleitner
(2015) the ESG ratings are not verified, validated, or replicable. With this in mind,
we instead created our own indices using a technique called Principal Component
Analysis (PCA) and publicly available data.
PCA is a statistical technique for reducing the dimension of a dataset. The pop-
ularity of PCA stems from its property of preserving the maximum amount of

                                   7
information in the dataset. Formally, PCA is defined as an orthogonal linear
transformation that transforms the data to a new coordinate system such that the
greatest variance by some scalar projection of the data comes to lie on the first co-
ordinate (called the first principal component), the second greatest variance on the
second coordinate (called the second principal component), and so on. More about
PCA can be reviewed in a machine learning textbook such as Bishop (2006). A
mathematically rigorous explanation of PCA can be found in Appendix A.6.
By applying PCA to our dataset we can transform the data into E, S, and G
indices. We can also create other indices, e.g. ESG indices by applying the same
PCA transformation to the entire datasets, and not just groups of data.

ernment bond yield spreads a series of regression analyses were performed. The
dependent variable was the yield spreads in every regression, while the independent
variables were an index, e.g. E index, together with the control variables.

3.2 Mechanisms
The results of our study indicate that the Governance, Social and SG indices
have significant negative correlations with the bond yield spreads, while the En-
vironmental index and overall ESG index exhibit no significant correlation. The
Governance and Social Index are also found to be highly correlated with each
other. Moreover, when looking at the regression plots in Appendix D, we can see
that the relationship between ESG scores seems to be non-linear for outliers.
In this section, possible underlying reasons for these results are discussed.

3.2.1 Governance index
We begin by discussing the Governance index. According to Delmas and Lim
(2017), there are several possible reasons why governance factors, i.e. quality of
legal institutions, have a negative correlation with bond yield spreads. One expla-
nation is that good governance practices can increase political stability and lead
to more efficient use of resources. Both these factors can, in turn, lead to higher
economic growth rates according to a report by OECD (2015) which creates higher
tax revenues for the government. This is important since total tax revenues ac-
count for more than 80% of total government revenue in about half of the countries
in the world – and more than 50% in almost every country according to Our World
in Data (2022). Tax revenues are thus an essential determiner of their ability to
pay off debt.
According to Pech (2022), the mechanism at play may be that countries with
good governance practices often have well-established rule of law and a robust
legal framework that protects the rights of bondholders. This ensures that bond-
holders’ interests are safeguarded and provides them with legal recourse in case
of any disputes. The presence of a strong legal framework thus reduces perceived
investment risks, thereby lowering bond yields.
Additionally, countries with good governance practices often enjoy positive in-
vestor sentiment due to their reputation for responsible governance, transparent
disclosure and respect for the rule of law. Positive sentiment attracts a broader
investor base, including international investors seeking safe and stable investments
to a report by UNCTAD (2022). The increased demand for government bonds
lowers their yields.

3.2.2 Social index
We continue by discussing the Social index. There are several possible reasons why
Social variables have a negative correlation with bond yield spreads. One possible

                                   9
explanation is that countries with strong performance in social variables, such as
low-income inequality, access to education, healthcare, and social safety nets, tend
to have greater social stability. Social stability reduces the risk of social unrest,
protests, or political instability, which are factors that can negatively impact a
country’s economy and financial markets. Lower risk of social instability leads to
lower bond yield spreads as investors perceive lower risk in holding government
bonds.
Moreover, countries that prioritise social variables often invest in human capital
development such as education and skills training. A well-educated and skilled
workforce contributes to economic productivity and competitiveness, leading to
higher economic growth according to Berger and Fisher (2013). Using the same
argument as previously, this should lead to a higher ability to repay debt and thus
lower yields.
Although Social variables serve as indicators for sovereign debt risk, many argue
that it is the public governance that drives sovereign issuer’s progress toward social
development Krebbers (2020). This is also reflected in Appendix B.6 where it is
shown that the S and G index has a strong correlation. It could thus be argued
that many of the underlying mechanisms explaining the correlations between the
S and G-indices with the yield spreads are the same.

3.2.3 Environmental index
The only variable which did not exhibit a significant correlation with the yield
spreads was the environmental index. This is in line with the results of similar
studies made by asset managers Morgan Stanley Investment Management (2021)
and Allianz Global Investors (2017). One potential reason for this might be a time-
horizon mismatch. The consequences of environmental issues often take a long
time to appear and the time until impact may extend beyond the 10-year period
of the bonds studied in this paper. Bond yields are typically more influenced by
short-to-medium-term economic factors and government policies rather than long-
term considerations according to Diebold and Li (2003). This mismatch in time
horizons would then diminish the correlation between environmental variables and
bond yields. Similar findings were made in Berg, Margaretic, and Pouget (2016)
which suggest that environmental (and social factors), in contrast to governance,
affect sovereign bond spreads with a lag as the impact of measures to improve
these factors tends to materialise over time.
Another reason for the lack of correlation might be that a large share of the set of
variables we chose to include in our E-index does not reflect a risk for investors.
For example, it is not clear how the percentage of forest area of a country influences

                                   10
the country’s ability to repay its sovereign debt. It might be the case that if we had
included other environmental variables in our E-index, we would have observed a
significant correlation. Nonetheless, this still does not rebut the observation that
environmental variables might be less correlated to bond yield spreads than social
and governance variables.
The lack of significance for the Environmental index, likely also explains why
the composite ESG index is not significantly correlated with yield spreads. The
addition of the environmental data to the composite index removes the correlation
that would have been present if only the governance and social index would have
been included.

3.2.4 Non-linearity of results
Although a linear regression has been performed in the study, the relationship
between ESG performance and yield spreads seems to be of non-linear nature for
outliers. When looking at the visualised regressions in appendix D, we see that the
market seems to penalise countries with the lowest ESG scores more than would
be expected when looking at the linear trend line.
One reason for this non-linear behaviour could be that market sentiment plays
a crucial role in determining bond yields. Countries with low ESG scores may
face negative market sentiment due to concerns about their environmental impact,
social stability, or governance practices. Research shows that corporate bonds
appear underpriced (with high yields) during pessimistic periods and overpriced
(with low yields) during optimism periods Nayak (2010), the same may be true
for sovereign bonds in the context of ESG performance. If that is the case, nega-
tive sentiment can lead to a disproportionate increase in bond yields as investors
perceive higher risk and are more hesitant to invest in those countries, creating a
non-linear relationship.

3.3 Interpretations
There are different ways to interpret the results of this study in the context of fixed-
income investments. Some industry reports, such as Morgan Stanley Investment
Management (2021) and Allianz Global Investors (2017) argue that traditional
credit ratings do not adequately reflect a country’s ESG performance. However,
our results indicate that there is a significant correlation between ESG performance
and bond yield. This illustrates that ESG performance, especially Social and
Governance performance, can be used as an alternative and multi-dimensional view
of risk that investors can use to complement the information currently contained
in traditional credit ratings. Important to also note is that using an overall ESG

                                   11
index as a risk measure might not be useful due to the lack of correlation with
yields caused by the inclusion of environmental variables.
Moreover, as discussed earlier, studies such as Berger and Fisher (2013) indicate
that ESG risk factors gain significance over time (especially Environmental and
Social factors), suggesting their long-term nature. This has implications for portfo-
lio management, particularly concerning sovereign ESG risk factors. These factors
may appear insignificant during a holding period. Still, they can suddenly become
activated due to a combination of adverse circumstances, leading to a substantial
alteration in the credit risk profile of a sovereign issuer. Therefore, it is crucial to
anticipate ESG risks by actively monitoring and evaluating early warning signals
to mitigate their potential impact.
Finally, observing the non-linear relationship between ESG performance and yield
spreads is important for investors’ asset allocations. Investors should be aware that
countries which are part of the group of worst ESG performers may be subject to
extreme pricing adjustments in terms of bond yields. To reduce the volatility of
the portfolio, investors may therefore consider excluding the worst ESG performers
completely, especially since according to Allianz Global Investors (2017) excluding
the worst ESG-performing countries does not alter the main characteristics of a
portfolio.

3.4 Limitations of the study and future research
One limitation arises from the time horizon mismatch between the ESG variables
and the bond yield data used in the analysis. As previously discussed, ESG factors
typically have longer-term impacts that may take time to materialise and affect
bond yields. However, the thesis utilises 10-year bond data, which might not
fully capture the long-term effects of ESG performance. Considering a longer
maturity, such as 30-year bond yields, would have been beneficial to capture a
more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between ESG and bond
yields.
Another limitation stems from the thesis’s reliance on data solely from 2015. Re-
stricting the analysis to a single year may not provide a complete picture of the
relationship between ESG performance and sovereign bond yields. ESG factors
and their importance in financial markets have evolved over time, and analysing a
broader time range would have allowed for a more comprehensive examination of
the correlation.
One potential limitation of the regression analysis is omitted variable bias, which
can be further studied in Stock and Watson (2007). Omitted variables bias refers
to relevant factors that are not included in the regression model but can impact

                                   12
both the independent variable (ESG performance) and the dependent variable
(bond yield spreads). For example, factors such as macroeconomic indicators,
fiscal policies, political stability, or specific country characteristics may influence
both ESG performance and bond yields. Although we have incorporated some
control variables, we may have not included all the important variables and omitted
variable bias may therefore still be present. The omission of these variables can
lead to biased estimates and an incomplete understanding of the true relationship
between ESG and bond yields.
Lastly, the quality of ESG data is another limitation of the study. ESG data
vary in coverage, consistency, and comparability across countries and time peri-
ods. Variations in measurement methodologies, disclosure practices, or reporting
standards could introduce inconsistencies and affect the accuracy of the findings.
These data limitations can impact the robustness of the results and conclusions
drawn in the thesis.

4 Conclusion

In conclusion, this thesis provides insights into the correlation between ESG per-
formance and sovereign bond yield spreads. The findings indicate a significant
negative correlation between the Governance and Social indices and bond spreads,
highlighting the importance of good governance practices and social stability in
reducing the risk of sovereign debt default which in turn leads to lower sovereign
bond yields. However, the Environmental index did not show a significant correla-
tion with bond yields, potentially due to a time-horizon mismatch and the limited
selection of environmental variables.
Regarding the practical implications of the thesis, integrating ESG performance,
particularly Governance and Social factors, alongside traditional credit ratings
can provide investors with a more comprehensive view of risk in sovereign debt
investments. The findings underscore the importance of actively monitoring and
evaluating ESG signals related to ESG risks which in some cases can materialise
slowly over time. Furthermore, the study concludes that investors should consider
excluding the worst ESG performers from their portfolios to lower volatility while
keeping other characteristics of their portfolios.
The study has a number of limitations. Future research could for example explore
longer-term bond data, such as 30-year yields, to capture the full impact of ESG
factors. Additionally, addressing the potential issue of omitted variable bias and
enhancing the robustness of the analysis would strengthen the conclusions.

Let y ∈ R describe an observed point. We then have that the expected variance
                                                 T                           d×k

                                                  T

                T            T    T                              T       T

The key property of PCA is that the PCA transformation maximizes this variance.

   Momentum in Stock Returns Following Dispersion and Consensus in
                         Analysts’  Forecasts

                            Amanda Hedlund (23496)*

Abstract
Our study shows that it is possible for an investor to employ profitable zero-cost portfolio
strategies  on the OMX Stockholm Benchmark  Index  that exploit momentum following
analysts’  forecasts.  The significant alpha of the monthly rebalanced long-short portfolios
suggests  that the analysts’ forecasts  momentum should be exploited within a month.  As
predicted by earlier literature, we find a negative relationship between dispersion in analysts'
forecast and future stocks returns, while a portfolio of low-dispersion stocks not necessarily
outperforms.  Our  results  on the liquid and investable OMX Stockholm Benchmark  Index
support that any market friction, not necessarily short-selling constraints, will produce the
negative relation between dispersion in forecasts and future returns.

Key  words:  momentum,  forecast  dispersion,  analysts’  recommendations,  short-selling
restraints, behavioural finance

1. Introduction

We study the existence of momentum following analysts’ forecasts on stocks in the OMX
Stockholm Benchmark Index (OMXSB) and the practical ability for an investor to exploit these
anomalies. The four portfolio strategies are based on dispersion in target price forecasts,
dispersion of buy/hold/sell-recommendations, the consensus in buy-recommendations, and a
combined factor of the  two  best performing  analysts’  forecast factors; the dispersion in target
price forecasts and consensus in buy-recommendations.
       According to the efficient market theory, attempts to use analysts’ forecast to produce
above-normal  returns  are  doomed.  The  mass  distribution  makes  them  a  public  good
immediately upon release which leads to a direct adjustment of any arbitrary over-  or
underpricing. However, empirical studies  have shown  that  it  exists an underreaction to public
information such as analysts forecast and  one can  achieve  abnormal  returns by exploiting the
following drift in stock prices  (Barber et al. 2001) (Givoly and Lakonishok, 1980).
       The profitability of momentum strategies are generally supported, yet the momentum
anomalies  remain  unexplained in the context of  the  efficient market theory (Fama and French,
1996). Miller (1977) was the first to propose the intuitively appealing theory that when
opinions diverge, and short-selling restraints bind, pessimistic investors are hindered from
expressing their opinions which  lead stock prices to reflect an overly optimistic view. In using
dispersion in  analysts’  forecasts as a proxy for diverging opinions, Diether et al. (2012) find a
significant negative relation between high dispersion in analysts’ forecasts and future returns.
Hong et al. (2000) suggest another widely acknowledged theory that the momentum arises
from gradual information flow of  analysts’  firm-specific information. In using market size and
analyst coverage as a proxy for information diffusion, Hong et al. (2000) find that momentum
exists for stocks with low market capitalisation and low analyst coverage.
      These and many  other momentum studies are based on a complete market universe
where the practical issues of trading small and illiquid stocks are overlooked (Hong and Stein,
1999) (Diether et al., 2002). Apart from the liquidity issues in the practical implementation of
previous studies, another relates to the neglect of realistic rebalancing frequencies. Daily
rebalancing is not a common action for investors other than professional day-traders, yet daily
rebalancing frequencies is a recurrent theme in the literature body.
       The practical implication of momentum strategies are far less covered in academia, and
few covers the  Swedish market  in specific. Therefore, we believe it is interesting to study
whether the general results from earlier studies also hold for the Swedish equity market and in

                                     3
practice by using less frequent rebalancing periods on a more investable sample. The illiquidity
is cared for by limiting the  universe  to stocks traded on the OMXSB. The OMXSB consists of
the largest and most traded stocks, representing all ten sectors, and is by nature a liquid and an
investable index  (Nasdaq, 2018). The sample characteristics also enable us to ensure a  high
minimum number of analysts covering each stock. In using the liquid OMXSB we test  the
practical ability for an investor to exploit the momentum in  analysts’forecasts  on the OMXSB,
and  the two central theories of short-selling restraints and  information diffusion.
       To study the existence of momentum following analysts’ forecasts, we create four
different   factors   based   on   analysts’   forecasts   on   target   prices   and   buy/hold/sell-
recommendations;   Target   Price   Dispersion,   Recommendation   Dispersion,   Buy-signal
Revision,  and the Combination factor formed by  the  Target Price Dispersion and Buy-signal
Revision. Stocks in the security universe are ranked  based  on the factor measure and grouped
accordingly into quantiles.  We take a long position  in  the stocks in quantile 1 and a short
position in the stocks in quantile 5 for each factor to test whether it is possible for an investor
to employ profitable zero-cost portfolio strategies using  these factors. We  value-weight our

                                                     st                          nd
portfolios and  test the strategies between 31  of March 2005 to 2  of January 2017, and
benchmark to the value-weighted OMXSB and the value-weighted index of all Swedish stocks.
      We  find  that it is possible for an investor to exploit negative momentum anomalies by
employing a monthly rebalanced  zero-cost portfolio using any of the above factors to select
stocks, except for the Recommendation Dispersion factor. A long-short portfolio under
monthly rebalancing created using the Target Price Dispersion,  Buy-signal Revision or the
Combination factor, has generated significant estimated annual excess returns under the
Carhart four-factor model of 21.17, 21.67 and 19.66 percent  respectively. Meanwhile, a buy-
and-hold strategy of the OMXSB and the index of all Swedish stock has generated mean annual
returns of 8.65 and 12.72 percent  respectively. As the results  remain  under the  Carhart four-
factor model,  our  results  are not simply a result of common  systematic risk exposures.
       Our  results  align with the theory of Diether et  al. (2002) that any market friction, not
necessarily  short-selling constraints,  will produce the negative  momentum to  analysts’
forecasts.  Our sample characteristics contradict the possibility that our market friction is
derived from  information diffusion  since the proxies for information diffusion used by Hong
et al. (2000) are small stocks and low analyst coverage.  In line with previous literature, we find
across all factors that the high-dispersion portfolio  underperform  while the low-dispersion
stocks  not necessarily outperform (Miller, 1977). The same holds true for the Buy-signal
Revision factor.

2. Literature review

In documenting significant  alpha on portfolios  based  on  analysts’ forecasts  on a security
universe composed of the liquid and investable  OMXSB, we extend two centered papers in the
theoretical debate of momentum; Diether et al. (2002), and in turn Miller (1977), theory of
market frictions and short-sale constraints, and Hong and Stein (1999) documented relation
between information diffusion and a stronger earnings momentum effect. Our methodology
can easily be extended to the study of other anomalies, such as corporate events or the post-
earnings announcement drift.
       Our results are also currently relevant in the light of the EU reform MiFID II effective
from January 2018. The equity research sector will be profoundly affected by the regulation as
asset managers will need to fund external research. Under MiFID II, competition is likely to
increase  within  equity  research  as  asset  managers  become  more  selective.  Therefore,
understanding whether it is possible for an investor to exploit analysts forecast momentum
anomalies is of central importance both for asset managers and for analysts.
      Givoly and  Lakonishok (1980) were among the first to report a momentum effect
following analysts’ revisions of their forecasts of corporate earnings. A related effect is price
momentum where  the notion  is that  past winners continue to perform well, and past losers
continue to perform poorly. An impressive magnitude of one percent abnormal return per
month is reported by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) for a self-financing price momentum
strategy. The momentum anomalies have been left unexplained in the context of efficient
market theory, where predictable patterns in asset returns should be able to be explained by
differences in loadings on economically meaningful risk factors. Fama and French (1996)
acknowledge that their three-factor model is unable to explain the momentum effect reported
by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993). Nevertheless, we employ both the Fama-French three-factor
model and the Carhart four-factor model, which includes a momentum factor built upon the
findings by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993), to reject the suggestion that our documented returns
are a result of priced risk-factors.
      The theoretical debate on the drivers behind the momentum effect centres on the
intuitively appealing theory set forth by Miller (1977). Miller (1977) suggests that when
opinions diverge, and short-selling constraints exist, prices will reflect a more optimistic
valuation since pessimistic investors will not sell the stock. In interpreting dispersion in
analysts' forecasts as a proxy for diverging opinions about a stock, Dither et al. (2002)
document that stocks with high dispersion yield abnormal negative returns and thus generate a

                                     5
profitable portfolio strategy of shorting high-dispersion stocks. Diether et al. ( 2002) extend
the short selling-restraints by Miller (1977) by arguing that any market friction, such as the
incentive structure of analysts when issuing forecasts,  that prevents the revelation of negative
opinions will produce this negative relation between dispersion in forecasts and future returns.
       Another widely recognised theory is the one by Hong and Stein (1999) that a lower rate
of information diffusion among investors leads to a stronger earnings momentum effect. Hong
et al. (2000) use market size and analyst coverage as a proxy for information diffusion and find
that momentum is more pronounced for stocks with low market capitalisation and low analyst
coverage. Their rationale is that if momentum arises from gradual information flow, then the
stocks where information diffuse slower should have more momentum.
       Along the lines of diverging opinions (Miller, 1977) (Diether et al., 2012), we employ
two dispersion measures; the Recommendation Dispersion and the Target Price Dispersion.  In
employing these two measures on our liquid and investable securities universe, we  test the
practical  implementation of using dispersion in analysts’forecasts in portfolio management and
challenge the theory of short-selling restraints as the facilitator for momentum following
analysts’  forecasts. By basing our sample on the OMXSB we also test the validity  of the
information diffusion theory, that momentum following analysts’ publications of firm-specific
information should be trivial for large stocks with high analyst coverage.
       The robustness of the momentum effect to different risk-adjustments has motivated
explanations from the premise of behavioural finance as an alternative to the likes of Diether
et al. (2002) and Hong et al. (2000) that relax the market efficiency assumption of homogenous
expectations. Turning to behavioural finance, the momentum anomaly is consistent with
several  irrationalities that characterise individual decision  making. Daniel et al. (Daniel et al.
1998) document a model where overconfidence leads to negative long-lag autocorrelations and
where   biased   self-attribution   adds   positive   short-lag   autocorrelations.   Investors’
overconfidence in their ability to price stocks leads them to underestimate their forecast error
variance and their biased self-attribution lead them to underreact to public information that
contradicts their private information. Consistently in these behavioural models, momentum is
driven from the underreaction of price adjustments following public news, and overreaction
following private signals. These empirically documented theories that prices drift in the
direction of  analysts’  forecasts  consensus  underpin our motivation to test a portfolio strategy
of buying stocks with the  most  buy-revisions and selling stocks with the least buy-revision in
relation to  all other sample  stocks, in search  for abnormal returns trailing investor behavioural
biases.

3. Data

We obtain  daily return  data from the Bloomberg terminal  using the Backtesting  tool  to create
the  portfolios based on  the securities universe  on  the  OMX Stockholm Benchmark Index
(OMXSB)  and  the  four  different  analysts’  forecasts  factors;  Target  Price  Dispersion,
Recommendation Dispersion, Buy-signal Revision and Combination  factor. The returns are
value-weighted, and we rebalance each portfolio over four different holding periods; one
month, three months, six months and twelve months; respectively. Thus, for the one month
holding period the rebalancing frequency is monthly.  We also gather the value-weighted daily
returns from Investing.com on the full OMXSB to benchmark  against our portfolio  returns.
Since the OMXSB  is value-weighted  by praxis,  we  can  better  capture the economic
significance of the performance of our portfolios by value-weighting the stocks in the portfolios
and then  compare  the returns to  the benchmark. Also, the more heavily representation of small
firms in equal-weighted portfolios could  overstate the portfolio returns.

       Our securities universe is based on the OMXSB  which consists of a selection of the
largest and most traded Swedish stocks. Our motivation to use the OMXSB is that it is designed
to ensure an investable and liquid index. The constituents of the OMXSB are screened to ensure
liquidity and selected on the basis of the free float-adjusted market value, which ensures that
only the part of the share capital that is considered available for trading is included  (Nasdaq,
2018). Moreover, twice a year the OMXSB is revised to ensure that it offers high investability
and low transaction costs. Note that the investable universe for the portfolios at each
rebalancing date consists of the effective OMXSB constituents at that particular point in time.
       The large stocks and low transaction costs that characterise the OMXSB may bias our
results against finding evidence of abnormal returns since markets are likely to be most
efficient for the largest securities  (Hong et al. 2002). Hong et al. (2000) prove that the
momentum effect is larger for smaller stock and argue that this is because information diffusion
is likely to be largest for these stocks. Along with this  reasoning, Diether et al. (2002) claim in
line with Miller (1977) that smaller stocks are likely to create larger momentum since they are

                                     7
the hardest to short and least likely to have traded options. However, as our objective is to
empirically document a practicable momentum investment strategy, our standpoint is that the
bias of our sample is more of an addition than a limitation of our paper. The large stocks  and
investable characteristics  ensure less shorting restraints compared to highly illiquid small
stocks.
       The stocks that constitute our security universe are included based  on  the fulfilment of
three criteria; trading status is active at portfolio rebalancing date, it is the primary security of
a company, and the stock is covered by more than six analysts at portfolio rebalancing date.
Any stock in the universe that fails to fulfil on one or more of the criteria during the period is
dropped from the sample and included once regained status. If a stock is included in a portfolio
and fails to fulfil a criterion over the holding period, it will maintain in the portfolio up until
the end of the holding period. Our requirement of more than six analysts caps our momentum
effect as a function of information diffusion from analyst coverage, thereby isolating the effect
from the specific revision and dispersion  factor. As shown in Table 1,  typically,  14  analysts
covered a single stock over our sample period. Hong et al. (2000) use analyst coverage as a
proxy for information diffusion and find that a majority of  the low coverage stocks carry
negative momentum. Their sample consists of as few as zero analysts for a majority of their
sample period and for their strategy the median analyst coverage of the low-coverage portfolio
is 0.1 and only 7.6 for their high-coverage. In using a higher analyst coverage  we  implicitly
test  the validity of the information diffusion theory.

4. Methodology

We assign stocks into quantiles based on the ranking of the stocks according to each factor at

                                               th
the rebalance date. Stocks above the 80  percentile are grouped into quantile 1, and the stocks

          th
in the 20  percentile are grouped into quantile 5. The strategy is to take a long position on the
stocks in quantile 1 and a short position in the stocks in quantile 5 for each factor. For each
portfolio, we report estimated excess returns  after adjusting for four common risk factors  for
monthly, quarterly, semi-annual and annual rebalancing.
       The four different factors created to test the practical possibility to exploit momentum
following  analysts’  forecasts are based on either analysts’ estimated target prices or analysts’
buy/hold/sell-recommendations. These measures are natural choices as they arguably are the
easiest accessible estimates online by investors; it is delivered by several financial information
providers such as Bloomberg and incorporates the opinion and information  of several analysts.
We use two different factors on dispersion in analysts’ estimates  to test the practical
implementation of using dispersion in  analysts’ forecast in portfolio management on a Swedish
sample. The first is the dispersion in analysts’ estimated target prices and the second is the
dispersion in analysts’ buy/hold/sell-recommendations. As we base our sample on the
investable OMXSB, we also test the validity of the theories of short-selling restraints and
information diffusion on the Swedish market. The Buy-signal Revision factor is created to test
the how the change in the level of consensus and future stock returns are connected. Initial
testing showed that the Buy-signal Revision factor and the Target Price Dispersion factor
performed the best, which motivated  us to combine  the two factors into  the  Combined factor.
       We create all  revision and dispersion factors using the Bloomberg database, the
Bloomberg Equity Screening tool (EQS) and the Bloomberg Backtesting tool applied on our
security universe. Any estimate older than three months at the rebalance date is dropped from
the sample.
       We create the Target Price Dispersion factor by taking the difference between the
highest and the lowest of the analysts’ target prices in SEK at each rebalance date, divided by
the lowest price target,  for each stock. Thereafter, the stocks in our security universe are ranked

                                                                                         th
where the stock with the smallest spread (lowest dispersion) is in the 100  percentile. Thus,
the stocks with the lowest Target Price Dispersion measure are in quantile 1, and the stocks
with the highest Target Price Dispersion measure are in quantile 5.
        The Recommendation Dispersion factor is built upon the total number of sell-, hold-
and buy-recommendations respectively for each stock in the universe. We create the factor by

                                     9
computing the standard deviation in analysts’ sell-, hold-  and buy-recommendations for each
stock at each rebalance date. Thereafter, the stocks in our security universe are ranked where

                                                                                          th
the stock with the lowest standard deviation (lowest dispersion) is in the 100  percentile. Thus,
the stocks with the lowest Recommendation Dispersion measure are in quantile 1, and the
stocks with the highest Recommendation Dispersion measure are in quantile 5.
       We build the Buy-signal Revision factor by taking the ratio between the number of buy-
recommendations to the number of total  buy/hold/sell-recommendations for  each  stock at  the
rebalancing date, divided by the same ratio one month back. Thereafter, the stocks in our

                                                                                                 th
security universe are ranked where the stock with the highest revision is in the 100  percentile.
Thus, the stocks with the highest Buy-signal Revision measure are in quantile 1, and the stocks
with the lowest Buy-signal Revision measure are in quantile 5.
       Lastly, we create a combined factor of the Buy-signal Revision factor and the Target
Price Dispersion factor called  the  Combination  factor. We first rank the stocks from our
universe on each factor separately, and thereafter rank the stocks into quantiles using their
combined ranking of the two factors. Thus, the stocks with the highest Buy-signal Revision
measure and the lowest Target Price Dispersion measure are grouped into quantile 1, and the
stocks with the lowest Buy-signal Revision measure and the highest Target Price Dispersion
measure are grouped into quantile 5. The high-revision-low-dispersion portfolio (quantile 1)
will be called HRLD, and the low-revision-high-dispersion portfolio (quantile 5) will be called
LRHD.
       To  determine whether  profitable investment  strategies exists involving  analysts’
forecasts  revision and the two dispersion measures,  we initially couch quantile 1, quantile 5
and the long-short portfolio in terms of their mean value-weighted raw returns, standard
deviation and determine their Sharpe ratios. Throughout, annualised returns are calculated by
multiplying  the   daily returns by 252, as this is the average number of trading days on the
Swedish stock market. The performance of the factor portfolios are compared to two
benchmarks;  the OMX Stockholm Benchmark Index  and the index consisting of all Swedish
stocks, provided by the Swedish House of Finance. We use both as benchmarks because our
security universe is based on the OMXSB whereas the four risk factors used in our time-series
regressions on the portfolio returns are based on all Swedish stocks.
       Thereafter, we employ three different time-series regressions for risk adjustments  for
both the quantile portfolios and the long-short strategy and employ t-tests for individual
significance across each holding period for all explanatory variables and intercept. Firstly, we

employ the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) and estimate the following time-series
regression across each holding period to yield parameter estimates of alpha and beta:

       In line with Fama and French’s findings (1996), we employ the three-factor model by
Fama and French (1993) to explore the possibility that the CAPM average-return anomalies
are reflecting pricing of systematic risk exposures. We estimate the following time-series
regression for both the two quantile portfolios  and the long-short portfolio for each of the four
factors for all holding periods respectively:

           𝑅$%  −  𝑅'%  = ∝$+  𝛽$,𝑅-%  −  𝑅'% . + 𝛿$  ∗ SMB + ℎ$ ∗ HML +  𝜀$%

       The coefficients  𝛽$,  𝛿$, and  ℎ$, are factor loadings, also called  sensitivities,  on the risk
premium factors.  The HML variable (High-Minus-Low) is the value-weighted return premium
equal to the average return on Swedish high book-to-market stocks (“value stocks”) minus the
average return on Swedish low book-to-market stocks (“growth stocks”). The SMB variable
(Small-Minus-Big) is the value-weighted return premium equal to the average return on
Swedish small-cap stocks minus the average return on Swedish  large-cap stocks.  Our principal
testable implication for this Fama-French three-factor model is that ∝$  (the  portfolio alpha) is
equal to zero for all portfolio strategies.
       Along the Fama-French  risk factor rationale (1996), we add a momentum factor to
capture the medium-term continuation of returns from past winners and losers stocks which  is
found  to be an additional source of priced systematic risk (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993)
(Carhart, 1997).

     𝑅$%  −  𝑅'%  = ∝$+  𝛽$,𝑅-%  −  𝑅'% . + 𝛿$  ∗ SMB + ℎ$ ∗ HML +  𝑘$  ∗ MOM +  𝜀$%

       In this Carhart four-factor model, the MOM variable represents the value-weighted
return premium equal to the return on Swedish past  winner stocks  minus the return on Swedish
past loser stocks,  and  𝑘$  is the loading factor.
       To explore the robustness of our  results, we  employ  three different robustness checks.
Firstly, we test for  a  lag in portfolio formation by using three, six and twelve months revision
periods in the Buy-signal Revision factor and  the  Combination factor.  The results are risk-
adjusted under the CAPM, Fama-French three-factor model  and the Carhart four-factor model.
The rationale behind this robustness check is that our  returns could be a result of different
information available in analysts’ forecasts at different points in time.
       Secondly, we employ a sub-period analysis where we calculate risk-adjusted returns
using the  CAPM and  Carhart four-factor model  across all factors over the time periods; March
2005  to February 2009, March 2009 to January 2013,  and February 2013 to January 2017. The
sub-periods consist of 984 days, 989 days and 974 days respectively.  It is interesting to analyse
the performance of the factor portfolios over different sub-periods considering that the sample
period covers the Financial Crisis.
       Lastly, we employ  all  four factors with  equal-weighted portfolio returns.  Most studies
document substantial momentum anomalies from this return weighting and,  consistent with the
practicability goal of our study, we  recognise  the possibility for an investor to use equal-
weighted portfolios. The SMB, HLM and MOM factors are  equal-weighted when controlling
for other risk factors  in  the CAPM,  Fama-French’s three-factor model  and Carhart  four-factor
model.

5.  Results

Target Price Dispersion
       As evident from Table 2, the yearly mean raw returns are very high for the portfolio of
low-dispersion stocks (quantile 1) across the four different holding periods compared to the
benchmarks. However, the portfolios are much riskier compared to the OMXSB benchmark
and the benchmark consisting of all Swedish stocks, confirmed by high  standard deviations,
negative skewness, high kurtosis and  large  negative returns in the 1st percentile.  Although the
portfolios of low-dispersion stocks are riskier than the benchmarks, the Sharpe ratios indicate
that holders of the  low dispersion  portfolio are better compensated for the risk compared to
holders of any of the two benchmarks.
       The average daily returns across different holding periods of the high-dispersion
portfolio (quantile 5) are substantially lower than the corresponding holding periods of the low-
dispersion  portfolio. However, the high-dispersion portfolio still outperforms the OMXSB
benchmark for the monthly, quarterly and semi-annual rebalancing periods. Similar to the low-
dispersion portfolios, the portfolios of high-dispersion stocks is much riskier than the two
benchmarks, which in combination with the low Sharpe ratios suggest that investors should
avoid buying the stocks in the high-dispersion portfolio. The low mean returns for the high-
dispersion portfolio and the lower mean returns for longer holding periods are in line with
previous literature which documents that high-dispersion stocks have a negative association
with future stock returns (Diether  et al., 2002)  (Johnson, 2004)
       The average return of the long-short portfolios is lower than  the average returns of the
long  leg when compared across the corresponding holding periods. Nevertheless, except for
quarterly rebalancing of the long-short portfolio, the Sharpe ratio of the long-short portfolios
are better than the Sharpe ratio of  the two benchmarks and the long  leg. The results indicate
that the long-short strategy is providing better risk  compensation compared to the OMXSB and
the benchmark consisting of all Swedish stocks and the long and short  leg individually.

in both the HRLD and LRHD portfolio are generally riskier than the market. The insignificant
intercepts indicate that the high exposure to market risk is enough to explain the high average
returns of the HRLD portfolio presented in Table 8. The HRLD portfolios are significantly and
positively tilted towards MOM across all rebalancing periods, and also to the HML variable
for the two longest rebalancing periods. The loadings indicate that the stocks behave like value-
stocks that have performed well recently. In comparison, the LRHD stocks for the two longest
rebalancing   periods   behave   like   growth-stocks   that   have   performed   poorly   recently.
Interestingly, the significant monthly rebalanced LRHD portfolio does not load significantly
on any of the risk factors other than the market risk premium, and the large negative estimated
risk-adjusted annual return of  -15.62 percent  is thus left unexplained by the four risk factors.
       In line with the risk-adjusted returns of the LRHD and HRLD portfolio, the estimated
intercept under CAPM of the monthly rebalanced long-short portfolio is economically and
statistically significant. In particular, the estimated annual excess returns is 18.90 percent under
the CAPM. The intercept remains significant at a 5 percentage  level under Fama-French’s
three-factor model and the Carhart four-factor model. Miller (1977) and Stambaugh et al.
(Stambaugh, Yu and  Yuan, 2012) suggest that the zero-cost portfolio often is driven by a
relatively larger overpricing of stocks in one leg. Considering the low significance of the
HRLD, the significant monthly rebalanced portfolio of the long-short portfolio is likely a result
of the significant monthly rebalanced LRHD portfolio.
        The loadings of the long-short portfolio across all rebalancing periods indicate that the
constituents are tilted towards value stocks that have performed well recently. Also, for the
monthly rebalanced long-short portfolio the loading on the SMB factor becomes positive which
indicates that the constituents behave like small stocks.

General Results of the Long-Short Portfolio Strategy
       The high standard deviation and kurtosis, the negative skewness,  and strong negative
returns in the 1st percentile compared to the two benchmarks indicate high risk across all factor
portfolios. Underlying the riskiness of the factor portfolios is the low diversification benefits

                                     23
of the portfolios with sample sizes with  as few as seven stocks in one portfolio (quantile) at
certain points in time. The double amount of stocks in the long-short portfolios compared to
the individual quantiles provide an important explanation to why the risk measures are lower
for the long-short portfolios. The Sharpe ratios of the long-short portfolios are in general fairly
better than that of benchmarks, but the Sharpe ratios never surpass one and are rarely above
0.7. As expected from the raw return statistics, the following risk-adjusted portfolios express
high and significant loadings on the market risk premium across all factors. The beta even
surpasses a loading of 2 for at least one holding period of either the short- or the long  leg across
all factors except for the Recommendation Dispersion factor. The consistent loadings  of above
1  on the market risk premium  along with insignificant intercepts  for the risk-adjusted  short-
and long  legs across all factors suggest that the high mean raw returns are  just  compensations
for great exposure  to market risk. As expected from a long-short strategy, the risk-adjusted
zero-cost portfolios across all factors never display volatility above that of the market when
looking at the loadings on the market risk premium.
       Except for the Buy-signal Revision  factor, which lacks loadings on other risk factors
than the market risk premium, all risk-adjusted high-dispersion portfolios prove a clear pattern
concerning loadings on the size-, value-  and momentum-premium. Completely  opposite to the
findings of Diether et al. (2002), we find that the book-to-market ratios are negatively related
to dispersion in analysts' forecasts. The loadings indicate that high-dispersion stocks behave
like large growth stocks that have performed poorly over the past year (“loser stocks”). As
expected, the significant loadings of the long-short strategies are the reverse of the significant
loadings of the short  legs. Thus the constituents of the long-short portfolios, again except the
Buy-signal Revision factor, consistently behave as small value stocks that have performed well
on average (“winner stocks”).
       The negative momentum loadings across the high-dispersion portfolios are in line with
previous literature that negative abnormal returns are related to past losers (Jagdeesh and
Titman, 1993) (Hong et al., 2000). Hong et al. (2000) also find that negative momentum is
particularly pronounced for low coverage stocks. Along the theories of information diffusion,
Hong et al. (2000) argue that low coverage stocks are proxies for information diffusion and
that the information of bad news (bad past performance) travels slower than good news (good
past performance), which results in the abnormal negative returns for past losers. Since our
sample is based on a minimum analyst coverage of seven  analysts, our results contradict the
notion that the analyst coverage, and thus information diffusion, is especially crucial for the
momentum return to  analysts’  forecasts that we find.

                                     24
       The  significance  of  the  risk-adjusted  rebalancing  frequencies  concludes  that  a
rebalancing (holding) period of one to a maximum of six months are optimal for high-
dispersion and low-revision portfolios. All of the risk-adjusted factors document insignificant
intercepts for the long  leg of the zero-cost portfolio across all  rebalancing frequencies. Again,
this result  is consistent  with  Miller’s (1977) findings that  high-dispersion stocks will
underperform while low-dispersion stocks will not necessary outperform. Except for the
Recommendation Dispersion factor, all factors document significant intercepts for the long-
short strategy under a monthly rebalancing frequency even after risk adjusting the returns
according to the Carhart four-factor model. In particular, a risk-adjusted zero-cost portfolio
under monthly rebalancing based on either the Target Price Dispersion factor, the Buy-signal
Revision factor or the Combination factor has generated significant estimated annual excess
returns under the Carhart four-factor model of 21.17, 21.67 and 19.66 percent  respectively.
Meanwhile, a buy-and-hold strategy of the OMXSB and the benchmark of all Swedish stock
respectively has generated mean annual returns of 8.65 and 12.72 percent  respectively. Since
the results of the long-short portfolios remain after adjusting for the Carhart four-factor model,
our strategies are not likely to be a result of common systematic risk exposures.
       The monthly rebalancing frequency indicates a more practicable strategy than the
academically prevalent daily rebalancing. Barber et al. (2012) test for different holding periods
of long-short strategies based  analysts’  recommendation on US stocks, and find that there are
no significant abnormal returns to rebalancing periods longer than daily. Our results therefore
both indicate a more practical opportunity and that market characteristics likely play a role in
the profitability of a long-short strategy on  analysts’  recommendations.  Nevertheless, even
though the lower frequency is useful in addressing the practical ability, we recognise that we
do not have a perfect measure of the specific transaction cost of each stock and therefore cannot
confidently rule out variations in  net  returns of investing according to our long-short strategies
in practice.
       In the light of the MiFID II, our results prove the benefits of using or considering
analysts’  estimates in portfolio management. For institutional or individual investors that are
long-only, the high risk and negative performance of the high-dispersion stocks indicate that
these stocks should be avoided or excluded  from the portfolios. An investor with the ability to
short can benefit from the negative relation between dispersion in forecasts and future returns
by employing the zero-cost portfolio strategy with monthly rebalancing.
       Our results question  the theoretical centrepiece behind momentum, the idea presented
by Miller (1977) that when opinions diverge and when short-selling constraints prevail,

                                     25
pessimistic investors will be restricted from selling stocks and stock prices will reflect the view
of the optimistic  investors. According to the rationale by Miller (1977), our sample consisting
of the liquid and investable OMXSB should not exhibit any noteworthy levels of abnormal
returns following momentum in diverging opinions. Nevertheless, our results show significant
estimated excess returns both for the long-short strategy as well as for the short  leg of the
portfolio. Our results therefore  better  align with Diether et al. (2002) that extend the short-
selling restriction to that any market friction that prevents the revelation of negative opinions
will produce the negative relation between dispersion in forecasts and future returns.
       Since our significant abnormal returns are yielded from our investable and liquid
sample, we deviate from the idea of market frictions being the existence of gradual information
diffusion (Hong et al. 2000). The large stock and high analyst coverage characteristics of our
sample are the complete reverses of the small stocks and low analyst coverage features that are
used as proxies for information diffusion in Hong et al. (2000). Since information, according
to the theory of information diffusion by Hong et al. (2000), should flow faster when the stock
is large and covered by more analysts, our strategies on our sample should generate
insignificant returns. Nevertheless, we document significant abnormal returns both for the
long-short strategy as well as for the short  leg of the portfolio.
       Instead, the apparent market friction of our sample aligns with the literature body of
behavioural finance. The market friction and our following momentum effect could be a result
of behavioural irrationalities of investors, rather than prevailing short-selling constraints or
information diffusion. The negative momentum returns are supportive of the overconfidence
and biased self-attribution theories put forward by Daniel et al. (1998). According to Daniel et
al. (1998), investors have overconfidence in their ability to value stocks, which leads them to
underestimate their forecast error variance, and their  biased self-attribution lead them to only
partly correct their positions when faced with public information that opposes their private
signal. The underreaction related to the low-revision stocks is indicative of overconfident
investors that remain confident and in a biased manner only partially correct their positions.
The investor can remain even more confident about his position when judging stocks with high-
dispersion in  analysts’  estimates, by biasedly acknowledging  the recommendation that aligns
the  most with his position.

5.1 Robustness

To explore the robustness of our study, we  employ  three different robustness checks. Firstly,
we test for a lag in portfolio formation by using three, six,  and twelve months revision periods
in the Buy-signal Revision factor and the Combination factor.  The returns are risk adjusted
under the CAPM, Fama-French three-factor model and Carhart four-factor model.  Secondly,
we employ a sub-period analysis where we calculate risk-adjusted returns using the CAPM and
the Carhart four-factor model  for  all factors and holding periods but over three time periods;
2005-2009, 2009-2013 and 2013-2017. Lastly, we test the returns to Target Price Dispersion,
Recommendation Dispersion, Buy-signal Revision and the Combination factor, but with equal-
weighted portfolios instead of value-weighted portfolios  for  all holding periods.

5.1.1 Lag in portfolio formation

We test for a lag in portfolio formation by using three, six and twelve months revision periods
in the Buy-signal Revision factor. The portfolios are created by taking the ratio between the
number  of  total buy recommendations to the number of total recommendations for each stock
at the rebalancing date, divided by the same ratio three months, six months and twelve months
back in time respectively. As the Buy-signal Revision measure is a part of the Combination
factor, the Combination factor will also be analysed over the three different revision periods.

General results of Lag in portfolio formation for Buy-signal Revision
       While the Buy-signal Revision measure with one-month-revision presented significant
negative estimated excess returns when rebalancing the low-revision stocks monthly, the
robustness check of the Buy-signal Revision measure with different revision periods presents
no such significance. In general, the outcomes of the Buy-signal Revision over longer revision

                                     28
periods are insignificant and not consistent with the loadings and intercepts signs of the one
month revision period. The rationale behind the difference in performance of the low-revision
portfolio across different revision periods is probably because of the different information
available in analysts’ forecasts at different points in time. As a case in point, Diether et al.
(2002) document that as lags in portfolio formation increase, returns drop as a result of the
information being stale. Diether et al. (2002) argue that disappointed investors have already
sold their stocks, resulting in that prices on the stocks in the portfolio have already fallen.
Therefore, the Buy-signal Revision factor performs best when the revision between the number
of buy-recommendations to the  total number of recommendations is as short as one month.
       The  apparent  long-term  reversal  of  high-revision  stocks,  indicated  by  the  new
significant negative intercepts for high-revision stocks over the two longest rebalancing
periods, could be a result of Daniel et al. (1998) so-called biased self-attribution effect.
Overconfident investors find supportive public information as a validation of their beliefs but
only partly revise their own positions  when the information contradicts their private signal.
This means that the stock prices are corrected in the long-term as the market gradually
recognises its past errors.

General results of Sub-period analysis
       Our results prove robust to the latter time periods, but the lack of significance in the
2005-2009 period across all strategies raises questions. None of the factors has generated any
significant estimated excess return in the 2005-2009 period. It is difficult to disregard that this
is not simply a reflection of noise in our sample,  or a reflection of the financial  crisis,  but
considering the relation between sample size and level of significance, we have reason to deem
differently. As an increased number of stocks decrease the standard deviation in a portfolio,
the number of stocks in a portfolio is positively correlated to the t-value and level of
significance. One important explanation to the insignificance of the 2005-2009 period is
therefore the low number of stocks in the universe compared to the other two sub-periods.  The
number of stocks in the universe during the 2009-2013 period and the 2013-2017 period are
considerably more than  in the first sub-period (Table 1).
       However, as the number of stocks in the universe has been about the same over the last
two sub-periods, the number of stocks is not providing any  explanatory value to why both of
the two dispersion factors performed much better in the 2013-2017 period compared to the
2009-2013 period. The average number of analysts covering each stock in the universe is the
about the same in the two sub-periods and  provides no additional contribution in explaining
the mixed performance over time (Table 1). Instead, the results can be explained by the
increased exposure to market risk and the related growth of the benchmarks. As shown in

                                     35
Figures 1a-4e and Table 28, the benchmarks grew substantially during the later sub-periods,
and during the same time both quantiles generated greater returns.
       The  superior performance during the last period  is also possible to explain from the
perspective of behavioural finance. In the shadow of the financial crisis, the average sentiment
on the Swedish stock market during the 2009-2013 period was generally lower compared to
the more optimistic sentiment in the 2013-2017 period. As an investor can become anchored
on the economic condition of a country or stock (Pompian, 2006), investors in the 2013-2017
period were probably more likely to underreact following the negative public news, thus
creating longer momentum compared to the 2009-2013 period.

5.1.3  Equal-weighted portfolios

In this section, we  use the four factors but weigh  the stocks equally within the portfolios. The
main idea of testing equal-weighted strategies is to study how the returns are affected when
smaller stocks have a greater weight in the portfolios compared to the  value-weighted
portfolios.

Target Price Dispersion
       Table 24 in appendix presents the estimated daily excess returns for all holding periods
for quantile 1, quantile 5 and the long-short portfolio under the CAPM, the Fama-French’s
three-factor model and the Carhart four-factor model. Table 24 documents improvement of the
long  leg (quantile 1) when equal-weighted, concluded by the nearly twice as large intercepts
compared to the value-weighted portfolio and the significant annual rebalanced low-dispersion
portfolio under the Carhart four-factor model. The semi-annual rebalanced high-dispersion
portfolio (quantile 5) becomes significant, instead of the annual rebalanced high-dispersion
portfolio under value-weighted portfolios. The risk is about the same as the value-weighted
portfolios, indicated by the comparable loadings on the market risk premium.
       The market adjusted excess daily returns of the equal-weighted long-short strategy are
significant for the same period as the value-weighted long-short strategy  but considerably
higher. Table 24 reports the continued significance under the Carhart four-factor model, and
also the increased magnitude of the intercepts. Specifically, the four-factor adjusted equal-
weighted long-short portfolio with an annual holding  period has generated up to 29.48 percent

                                     36
in estimated annual excess return, which in turn is 7.05 percentage points better than the value-
weighted long-short portfolio with annual rebalancing.

Recommendation Dispersion
       Table 25 in appendix presents the  estimated daily excess returns for all holding periods
for quantile 1, quantile 5 and the long-short portfolio under the CAPM, the Fama-French’s
three-factor model and the Carhart four-factor model. None of the high-  or low-dispersion
portfolios generates  significant alphas over any holding period. On the back of the mainly
insignificant loadings on other factors than the market risk premium, the returns of the equal-
weighted portfolios seem to be largely explained by exposure to market risk.
       Whereas the  value-weighted long-short strategy was unsuccessful, the equal-weighted
long-short strategy performs even more poorly. Both intercepts and significance close to zero
for monthly and quarterly rebalancing of the long-short strategy.

Buy-signal Revision
       Table 26 in appendix presents the estimated daily excess returns for all holding periods
for quantile 1, quantile 5 and the long-short portfolio under the CAPM, the Fama-French’s
three-factor model and the Carhart four-factor model. Just as for the value-weighted portfolios,
the monthly rebalanced low-revision portfolio (quantile 5) is significant, but at a lower
significance (5 percentage  significance level) than for the value-weighted equivalent (1
percentage  significance level). None of the holding periods of the long  leg (quantile 1)
generates significant alphas, but the intercepts improve both in magnitude and in t-value
compared to the value-weighted high-revision portfolios. The improvement of the long  leg is
not enough to offset the loss in the significance of the monthly rebalanced low-revision
portfolio, and the monthly rebalanced arbitrage portfolio therefore no longer generates a
significant alpha as it did for the value-weighted portfolio strategy of the Buy-signal Revision
factor.

Combination
      Table 27 presents the estimated daily excess returns for all holding periods for quantile
1, quantile 5 and the long-short portfolio under the CAPM, the Fama-French’s three-factor
model and the Carhart four-factor model. Similar to the Buy-signal Revision factor, the
monthly rebalanced LRHD portfolio (quantile 5) generates significant alpha also under equal-
weighted returns, but at  a lower significance (1 percentage  significance level) than the value-

                                     37
weighted equivalent (0.1 percentage  significance level). Just as  for the long  leg of the Buy-
signal Revision, the value and significance of HRLD portfolios’ intercepts (quantile 1) improve
for all holding periods under equal-weighted portfolio structure. The exposure to market risk
increases marginally for most portfolios under equal-weighted returns, and a majority of the
loadings on the value-premiums becomes insignificant.
       The equal-weighted long-short portfolio with a one-month holding period has generated
29.99 percent  in estimated annual excess return under  the Carhart four-factor model, which is
10.33 percentage points more compared to the monthly rebalancing of the value-weighted
long-short portfolio. The increased profits of the long-short strategy likely arise from the
improvement of the long  leg.

General results of  equal-weighted portfolios
       In general, the small stocks have contributed to a noteworthy upswing in the returns for
the long leg whereas the short  leg perform worse than under value-weighted returns. Across all
factors, the significance of the short  legs’ intercepts of the equal-weighted counterparts either
remain or decrease, and the magnitude of the intercepts consistently decrease.
        The increased estimated excess returns and significance of the long-short portfolios that
are evident for the  Target Price Dispersion factor and the Combination factor are derived from
improvement in the long  leg. This also explains why the long-short strategy for the Buy-signal
Revision factor no longer shows significance for the monthly rebalanced long-short portfolio,
since the results were largely driven by the short  leg which under equal-weighting proved to
be less pronounced.
       According to Hong et al. (2000), a lower rate of information diffusion among investors
leads to a stronger earnings momentum effect. As market size is used as a proxy for information
diffusion, weighing up small stocks in the high-dispersion portfolio would theoretically
generate more pronounced momentum. Similarly, Diether et al. (2002) document that smaller
stocks generate larger momentum as the short-selling restraints is greater than for larger stocks.
However, these theories stand in contrast to our findings. Either our small stocks are too big to
be  considered as stocks with a  low  rate of information diffusion, or the findings of Diether et
al. (2002) about short-selling restraints and the connection to stock size does not hold true for
our sample. Both suggestions are easily explained by our sample characteristics. Since we base
our sample on the OMXSB, we limit our universe to larger stocks with high analyst coverage.
Thereby, we deliberately avoid illiquid and small stocks with low analyst coverage that are
related to a slower rate of information diffusion and short-selling restraints.

                                     38
6. Conclusion

Our study shows that it is possible for an investor to employ profitable zero-cost portfolio
strategies on the OMX Stockholm Benchmark Index  (OMXSB)  that exploit momentum
following  analysts’  forecasts. A long-short portfolio under monthly rebalancing created using
the Target Price Dispersion factor, the Buy-signal Revision factor or the Combination factor,
has generated significant estimated annual excess returns under the Carhart four-factor model
of 21.17, 21.67 and 19.66 percent  respectively. Meanwhile, a buy-and-hold strategy of the
OMXSB and the benchmark of all Swedish stocks  respectively has generated mean annual
returns of 8.65 and 12.72 percent  respectively. As the results remain after adjusting for the
Carhart four-factor model, this indicates that our  returns are  not simply a result of systematic
risk exposures.
       Our results extend the power of earlier literature in finding significant and consistent
estimated excess returns for monthly, instead of daily, rebalancing by exploiting momentum
on analysts’ forecasts. Similar to the optimal rebalancing (holding) period of the long-short
strategy, we conclude that a rebalancing (holding) period over one to a maximum of six months
is  significant for the short  leg of the zero-cost portfolios. When testing for robustness, we find
that the revision period of one month is central to our findings of the Buy-signal Revision
factor, but that the Combination factor is robust to revision periods up to six months. The
robustness check also show that the results over the sample period are largely driven by the
two latest sub-periods; 2009-2013 and 2013-2017. Furthermore, we document that when the
performance  of  the  long-short  portfolio  improves  under  equal-weighted  portfolios  the
improvement is derived from development  of the long  leg.
       The   significant   momentum   returns   thogether   with   the   liquid   and   investable
characteristics of our sample contradict the theoretical core of short-selling constraint as the
main driver behind momentum from divergence in opinions among investors (Miller, 1977).
Our significant risk adjusted returns align more with the theory of Diether et al. (2002) that any
market friction, not necessarily short-selling constraints, will produce the negative relation
between dispersion in forecasts and future returns. Our sample characteristics also contradicts
the possibility that our market friction is derived from the theory by Hong et al. (2000) of
information diffusion since the proxies for information diffusion used by Hong et al. (2000)
are small stocks and low analyst coverage. The fact that we are able to generate abnormal
momentum returns by deliberately choosing liquid and large stocks adds to the practical
benefits of the strategies presented in our paper and earlier literature.

                                     39
       As predicted by the literature, we find across all  factors that the high-dispersion
portfolio   significantly   underperforms   while   the   low-dispersion   stocks   not   necessarily
outperform (Stambaugh et al., 2012) (Johnson, 2004) (Miller, 1977). The same pattern emerges
for the Buy-signal Revision factor where the low revision portfolio has significant and negative
intercepts while the intercepts for the high-revision portfolio shows mixed and insignificant
signs. The high mean return of the long legs are explained by the significant exposure to market
risk, proven  by the loadings on the market risk premium and insignificant alphas. Thus, our
results does  not only  suggest abnormal returns from a zero-cost portfolio based on  analysts’
forecasts, but also an opportunity for meaningful performance improvement for a long-only
investor from avoiding the stocks with high-dispersion in Target Price or Recommendation
estimates, or low-revision in Buy-signal  estimates.
       Important to note for the practical implementation of our study is the high risk
characteristics shared across all portfolios. All factors share significant loadings on the market
risk premium, high standard deviation and kurtosis, as well as negative skewness. Furthermore,
the small sample sizes with as few as seven stocks in one portfolio at certain points in  time
translates to low diversification benefits which further adds to the risk of the portfolios. The
somewhat higher Sharpe ratios compared to the benchmarks shows that investors are partially
compensated for the additional risk they take on.
       The significant alphas of the latter time periods (2009-2013 and 2013-2017) and the
documented possibility to generate abnormal returns from a long-short portfolio based on
analysts’  forecasts  estimates also provide insights to the current topic of MiFID II. Seemingly,
analysts’  forecasts has a value both for investors without short-selling restraints and for long-
only investors. For investors interested in investing in the OMXSB, it is likely helpful to
continue to use  analysts’  forecasts  also under the MiFID II. However, since our results are not
robust  over  all sub-periods, we cannot with confidence conclude that the results will remain
consistently going forward.
       Since our long-short strategy proved significant alpha for only one of our two dispersion
measures, we  suggest for further research an analysis across  several  dispersion measures to
conclude  the  characteristics of  the most successful dispersion measures. It would also be
interesting to  see whether the abnormal returns from  analysts’  estimates are industry  related.
Above all, we  suggest  a study on a larger sample of stocks over a longer period of time. Even
though a larger sample might call  for more relaxed restrictions, such as  our  minimum  analyst
coverage of seven analysts, we believe  that it  would give a  more accurate image of the
corresponding risks and risk related returns of the strategy.

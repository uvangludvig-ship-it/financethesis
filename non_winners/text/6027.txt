Inventory Effects: Market Makers’ Impact on Short-term Returns

Abstract:

   According to the inventory paradigm, market makers are not risk-neutral and demand risk
   premiums for holding positions. An order book imbalance can lead to inventory build-up
   of market makers. Price pressures caused by market makers' unloading of positions
   following build-up of inventory can potentially affect prices and, in extension, returns.
   This paper investigates whether stock trading strategies that trade in the direction of the
   previous day's order imbalances are profitable and the relationship between the order
   imbalance one day and the returns of the next day, using linear regressions. Even if it is
   not profitable to trade directly on lagged order imbalances, it should be of interest to
   anyone trading the markets if there exists a relationship between lagged order imbalances
   and returns. We cannot prove a relationship between one day’s order imbalance and the
   following day’s return, and the tested trading strategies do not produce statistically
   significant returns.

Keywords:

   Inventory effect, Market maker, Order imbalance, Short-term return, Stock

                                     I. Introduction
     Several studies have found a relationship between order imbalances and returns for
stocks. This paper examines imbalance-based trading strategies that have been found to yield
statistically significant returns and the relationship between lagged order imbalances and
returns for stocks included in the OMX Stockholm 30 (OMXS30) index. The trading strategies
are based on taking positions in the direction of the previous day's order imbalance. The Lee-
Ready algorithm, which classifies transactions as either buyer- or seller-initiated, is used for
calculations of the order imbalance during a trading day.
      The inventory paradigm suggests that market makers are not risk-neutral and demand
risk premiums for holding positions. If this is true, a market maker should seek to get out of a
position following a day or period where it has ended up with excess inventory as a result of
other market participants buying and selling activity. A market maker should even be willing
to offer favorable prices to get out of the position if the risk premium it demands is sufficiently
large. Market makers typically set both bid and ask quotes and let other market participants
either buy or sell from them. If there is an equal volume bought and sold of a security, a market
maker should be able to make a profit made up by the difference between the prices it buys and
sells at. What might happen in practice, however, is that there is more volume bought from or
sold to the market maker. When this happens, the market maker will end up with a change in
its inventory.
      The market makers’ selling or buying activity following a build-up of inventory can
impact prices in the short term, which, in extension, also affects returns. Consider, for example,
a case where a market maker has ended up with a big position in a particular stock as a result
of other market participants’ selling activity. If the market maker wants to get out of this
position, it is likely forced to offer other market participants favorable terms to do so. By
trading against the market makers in instances where they have had a build-up of inventory,
and in a sense providing them liquidity, one can potentially achieve excess returns. The returns
would stem from getting favorable prices when entering positions and unloading them later
when prices have returned to normal levels. One possible implementation would be to enter
positions in the direction of the previous day’s order imbalance, in the beginning of the trading
day and unload the position at the end of the trading day. Even if it is not profitable to trade
directly on lagged order imbalances, it should be of interest to anyone trading the markets if
there exists a relationship between lagged order imbalances and returns. We do not find
statistically significant relationships between lagged order imbalances and returns for stocks,
and we do not get statistically significant returns from following the trading strategies.

Related Literature
      Chordia, Roll, and Subrahmanyam (2002) study market-wide returns for the Standard &
Poor’s 500 index and find that returns are affected by order imbalances, even after controlling
for aggregate volume and liquidity. Their results are largely supportive of the inventory
paradigm’s theoretical implications, which suggests that market makers set quotes in a way
that encourages trading on the side that lets them get out of their position(s) when they have
accumulated excess inventory. Further, they argue that a significant portion of the daily
movement in stock prices can be explained by the general public’s buy and sell activity. We
study the relationship between lagged order imbalances and contemporaneous returns for
individual stocks, with the inventory paradigm serving as our main theoretical framework.
     Chordia and Subrahmanyam (2004) examine the relationship between order imbalances
and daily stock returns for stocks on the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) in the period 1988
to 1998. They argue that much of modern financial theory is based on an intermediation
paradigm, where market makers provide liquidity to buyers and sellers from the general public.

                                    2
This indicates that order imbalances should be strongly related to changes in price. They find
trading strategies trading in the direction of the previous day’s order imbalance, which yields
statistically significant returns. Their results align with the inventory paradigm, which suggests
that market makers set quotes to evoke trading on one side of a market after an event has caused
a significant inventory imbalance on the other market side. Chordia and Subrahmanyam (2004)
argue that it can be profitable for market makers to offer favorable prices for a period of time
if it lets them unload unwanted inventory. They further argue that there is some room for traders
with low transaction costs to take advantage of these favorable prices.
     While Chordia and Subrahmanyam (2004) find a relationship between lagged order
imbalances and returns and trading strategies that yielded significant returns, the financial
markets have changed since their sample period (1988 to 1998). This paper investigates
whether there is a relationship between lagged order imbalances and returns for individual
stocks and if the strategies tested by Chordia and Subrahmanyam (2004) are profitable today
and in a different market. Several arguments could be made as to why the profitability of the
trading strategies might have changed. It is possible that the risk tolerance of market makers
has changed over time. The risk tolerance of market makers will directly affect how favorable
prices they should be willing to offer to get out of positions and, in turn, how profitable it is to
take advantage of the favorable prices. It is also plausible that the competition for these
favorable prices has increased over time as market participants have found out about them,
which will affect how profitable it is to take advantage of them. Market makers may also have
become more proficient in encouraging trading on the side that lets them get out of a position,
in which case, they are not as dependent on setting favorable prices to get out of positions.
      Lee and Ready (1991) evaluate different methods for classifications of trades as either
market buys or market sells. They discuss two common ways to classify the direction of a trade:
the “tick test” and a method where the trade price is compared to the prevailing quotes. The
“tick test” compares the trade price to the preceding trade(s) price. If the trade price is higher
(lower) than that of the preceding trade(s), the trade is classified as an uptick (downtick) and is
considered buyer- (seller-) initiated. If the trade price is equal to the price of the adjacent
trade(s), the direction of the trade is set equal to that of the adjacent trade(s). The other method,
using prevailing quotes, compares the trade price to the order books at the time of the trade. A
trade that takes place at the current ask (bid) is considered buyer- (seller-) initiated. A trade can
also take place somewhere in between the current best ask and bid quotes. This can sometimes
be due to data issues relating to timing, but when controlling for this, Lee and Ready (1991)
find that many trades still happen inside the spread. Two possible ways to deal with these trades
are to simply ignore them or to classify them as buyer- (seller-) initiated if they happen above
(below) the midpoint of the current best bid and ask prices.
      Lee and Ready (1991) define a classification method, commonly referred to as the Lee-
Ready algorithm, that combines the two classification methods. They highlight problems with
using current quote midpoints for classification and argue that looking at the midpoint five
seconds prior to the trade improves the classification accuracy. The Lee-Ready algorithm
defines the direction of a trade based on the best bid offer midpoint five seconds prior to the
trade and at what price the trade takes place relative to that midpoint. If the price is higher than
the prior midpoint, the transaction is classified as buyer-initiated, and if the price is lower than
the midpoint, it is classified as seller-initiated. If the price is equal to the midpoint five seconds
prior, the “tick test” is used, where a transaction with an uptick is classified as buyer-initiated,
and a transaction with a downtick is classified as seller-initiated. If the trade price happens to
be the same as the previous transaction’s price, the direction of the trade is set equal to that of
the prior transaction.
      The Lee-Ready algorithm is used to classify transactions as either buyer-initiated or
seller-initiated in this paper. This transaction classification is then used to calculate the order

imbalances for individual stocks. Measuring market makers’ inventory build-up with perfect
accuracy is impossible without direct access to their books. Order imbalances can, however,
provide valuable insights and be used for approximations of market makers’ changes in
inventory. As market makers typically do not initiate transactions, but rather make it possible
for other market participants to initiate transactions by continuously providing a market, they
are likely to be sellers in buyer-initiated transactions and buyers in seller-initiated transactions.
The Lee-Ready algorithm, therefore, allows us to approximate changes in the inventory of
market makers.

                                     II. Methodology

A. Classifying transactions
      We collect historical level 1 order book data, aggregated by milliseconds and level 1
order book data when the transactions happen from the NASDAQ HFT database, along with the
number of shares traded and the transaction prices for each transaction. This allows us to
classify all transactions as either buyer- or seller-initiated through the use of the Lee-Ready
algorithm (Lee and Ready, 1991). For all transactions, we classify them by matching them with
the order book data that is closest to five seconds prior. We allow for a maximum of six seconds
and a minimum of four seconds prior to the transaction. If order book data cannot be found
within that span, we assume the data is faulty and do not classify the transaction.

B. Defining order imbalance measures
      We use two measures to quantify the order imbalance of a stock during a trading day.
We define OISVOL as the order imbalance in SEK divided by total SEK volume and OIBNUM
as the order imbalance in terms of the number of transactions divided by the total number of
transactions. Further, we define buyer-initiated transactions as 1 and seller-initiated
transactions as -1.

D. The trading strategies
     We test the profitability of the trading strategies implemented by Chordia and
Subrahmanyam (2004). We use OISVOL and OIBNUM to determine when there has been a
positive or negative order imbalance during a day. We classify a day with a positive OISVOL
(OIBNUM) as a day with a positive order imbalance and a day with a negative OISVOL
(OIBNUM) as a day with a negative order imbalance. OISVOL and OIBNUM values are
calculated for each trading day and each stock. After having examined if the order imbalance
is positive or negative during a day, we test if the strategy of buying (selling) one share at the
ask (bid) corresponding to the first transaction of the next day when the order imbalance has
been positive (negative). The position is unloaded at the best bid or ask corresponding to the
last transaction of the day. All transactions where we buy are calculated using the best ask
quotes and all the transactions where we sell are calculated using the best bid quotes. Chordia
and Subrahmanyam (2004) found the same trading strategies profitable in their sample for
stocks on the NYSE (1988 to 1998).
      The logic for why these strategies could be profitable is the following: A positive
(negative) order imbalance during a day implies that market makers are likely to have gathered
a position. They will, according to the inventory paradigm, want to exit this position. Market
makers will, therefore, offer favorable terms in order to elicit trading on the side that lets them
get out of their position. They will want to exit their positions early to limit their risks. By the
end of the day, market makers are likely to have been able to get out of their positions and the
bid and ask prices at the end of the day will be fairer. By getting favorable terms in the entries

                                    5
and fair prices in the exits, one could potentially get excess returns. The potential problems and
limitations with this strategy are, however, that you are paying a spread in every trade, and a
positive (negative) OISVOL or OIBNUM does not necessarily mean that there has been a
build-up of inventory of market makers big enough for them to offer favorable terms. We also
test the results of the same strategy of buying or selling, but only when the order book
imbalance is big, compared to a historical average for each stock. To do so, we only trade when
OISVOL or OIBNUM is one, respective, two standard deviations away from zero for each
stock. This should exclude the cases where the change in inventory is negligent to the market
maker.
      To limit the spreads paid, we also test the same strategies but where we exit the positions
in the closing auction. The trading strategies are based on getting favorable prices when
entering positions following a previous day's order imbalance, but theory does not suggest that
you will get favorable prices when exiting the positions. By exiting the positions in the closing
auction, we should get prices that are equally fair, but without having to pay a spread in half of
the transactions. This strategy should therefore improve the returns of the trading strategies.
We also report the results of the different trading strategies with the spread loss added back. It
is theoretically possible to trade without having to pay a spread through the use of dark pools,
where the prices are matched based on the mid-point offers on the central order book. It is,
however, not possible to know or test if one would be able to get an order filled at a specific
point in time.

OMXS30 return times the stock’s beta from the stock’s trading return.

      As the regressions involve correlated variables, the value of the regression results will be
somewhat limited. The returns of one stock will affect the return of the OMXS30. Further, the
return of one stock could affect the returns of other stocks included in the index. Order
imbalances might also be correlated across stocks. If order imbalances are correlated across
stocks, and if there is a relationship between lagged order imbalances and returns for the
individual stocks, an order imbalance of one stock would have predictive power of the other
stocks’ returns the next day. We test the correlation between order imbalances across stocks to
see if such prediction power could exist.

                                    III. Data Description
      The datasets for the order book data are available through Swedish House of Finance.
We collect the orderbook data for the 30 companies included in the Swedish Stock index
OMXS30 and classify all transactions as either buyer- or seller-initiated during the period
2021-10-12 to 2023-10-11, which is the last two years for which we can access the orderbook
data. The index OMXS30 is revised twice a year, but revisions can also be made when a stock
is no longer eligible for continued inclusion. As we do not have access to all revisions made
during the sample period, we use the stocks included in the index per the date per the date
2024-05-02. The datasets are based on NASDAQ OMX Historical ITCH files and should
therefore be reliable. It is, however, possible that the data sets contain some data errors. Beta
values for the different stocks are retrieved from Yahoo Finance and are average monthly betas
for the last five years. Intraday returns for OMXS30 are calculated using the index open and
closing prices each trading day. The Yahoo finance data for NASDAQ OMX Stockholm is
provided by ICE Data Services.
     OMXS30 consists of the 30 most traded stocks on the Swedish stock exchange NASDAQ
Stockholm. By analyzing stocks with higher liquidity, we make sure that a successful trading
strategy would be viable and executable with size. If it is not possible to execute the trading
strategy with size, it is likely that transaction costs will eat up a big portion of the profits.
Further, the spread is typically lower in bigger stocks and the spread paid has a big impact on
the returns when a strategy revolves around buying and selling over the spread. We chose to
analyze companies on Nasdaq Stockholm, because it is the only stock exchange for which we
are able to access detailed historical order books.

      Logic suggests that OISVOL should be a more direct measure of market makers' build-
up of inventory compared to OIBNUM, as OISVOL accounts for the volume of each
transaction. OIBNUM simply measures the number of transactions that are buyer- versus
seller-initiated and can therefore be sensitive to very small transactions. If a single share is sold
to a market maker repeatedly, for example, it will not affect the inventory in a significant way,
but it will have a big impact on the OIBNUM measure. Market makers, as long as they demand
premiums for holding inventory, should be willing to offer the most favorable terms when the
build-up of inventory is the greatest. It, therefore, makes sense that there should be a linear
relationship between OISVOL and the next day’s return and less of a linear relationship
between OIBNUM and the next day’s return. We do indeed see indications of a linear
relationship between OISVOL and the next day’s return, and less of a relationship when using
OIBNUM as the order imbalance measure. We do however observe low R-squared statistics in
both regressions.

                   Correlation between order imbalance measures
The correlation between daily OISVOL and OIBNUM is 0.762.

     OISVOL and OIBNUM are highly correlated, which could make the returns of the
trading strategies quite similar no matter which of the two measures of order imbalances is
used. OISVOL should nonetheless, if anything, be the superior determinant of market makers’
build-up of inventory.

      Even with the spread loss added back, we cannot see statistically significant returns from
following any of the trading strategies. The t-statistics do however indicate that some
relationship exists between the order imbalances one day and the returns the next day. We can
also observe that the returns are the biggest when we have high order imbalances measured by
OISVOL. This is what is predicted by theory, as market makers should be willing to offer the
most favorable terms when they have had a big build-up of inventory.
      In the implementation of any trading strategy, the transaction costs have to be considered.
The proposed strategies involve frequent buying and selling, and the transaction costs will,
therefore, determine the profits or losses to a large extent. Retail investors typically have to pay
high brokerage commissions and are not able to access detailed order book data on a daily
basis. The proposed trading strategies should, therefore, only be considered by professionals
with access to low transaction cost. While the tested trading strategies at best can be said to be
marginally profitable, if one were to get filled in the dark pools, they do show some potential
for the design of trading strategies revolving around lagged order imbalances that could yield
good profits. The tested trading strategies are quite simplistic, and it is likely that the trading
strategies could be tweaked and improved in ways that would improve the returns. One such
example would be to define a measure that captures the build-up of market makers’ inventory
better than the measures we used. Adding more lagged imbalances could also improve the
results. Further, it is likely that adding order imbalances as a factor to some other trading
strategy could improve its results. While we show some potential for the design of a profitable
trading strategy trading on lagged order imbalances, we have yet to adjust them for market
returns during the holding periods.

                                  V. Conclusions
     We study the relationship between lagged order imbalances and returns and trading
strategies trading in the direction of the previous day’s order imbalance for stocks included in
the OMXS30 index. The linear relationship between lagged order imbalances and the returns
is not statistically significant. We also find that the lagged order imbalances do not explain
much of the variation in returns, and we cannot reject the null hypothesis that there is no
relationship between the order imbalance of one day and the returns of the next day for
individual stocks.
     The trading strategies implemented by Chordia and Subrahmanyam (2004) do not yield
statistically significant returns in our sample. We find positive returns for some strategies
trading on lagged order imbalances when we add back the spread loss, specifically when we
have big order imbalances measured by OISVOL. Our theories predict that these should be the
best and most profitable strategies. The returns are, however, small and could be fully explained
by randomness. Even if the returns of the trading strategies are positive in expectation, few
market actors would have low enough trading costs to take advantage of the returns.
      When we adjust for market returns, we find no linear relationship between lagged order
imbalances and returns. This does however not necessary mean that no relationship exists for
individual stocks.

                                       Abstract

Index funds have grown to become a significant part of the family of investment vehicles, both
internationally and in Sweden. However, the amount of research conducted on their performance
and attributes has not grown at the same rate. While replicating an index might seem
straightforward, differences persist in how well index funds perform. This study first evaluates
the performance of Swedish index funds using five different measures, and then investigates what
factors affect the performance. The findings show that Swedish index funds on average do
poorly in tracking their benchmark index, and that their index-tracking performance is worse
than that previously exhibited by their U.S. counterparts. Significant differences in performance
are documented across funds and over time, while empirical differences in performance are
documented depending on which benchmark index the funds replicate. Fund characteristics,
namely fund expenses and trading activity, are found to explain differences in performance across
funds, while external factors, namely index volatility and dividends, are found to explain
differences in performance over time.

Keywords: Index Funds, Performance Evaluation, Tracking Error, Fund Characteristics

   1. Introduction

Since Jensen (1968), an extensive amount of research has shown that actively managed mutual
funds on average underperform their benchmarks. This brings about the relevance of an
alternative investment vehicle, one that seeks to replicate rather than to outperform its
benchmark. This is what an index-replicating fund, henceforth called index fund, does. The fact
that actively managed funds on average underperform their benchmarks has led to increased
attention towards index funds overall, and not least in Sweden. This is underlined by data from
the Swedish Investment Fund Association, showing that by the end of 2012, a fraction of 10.17
percent of the Swedish wealth invested in equity funds was allocated to index funds, compared to
7.85 percent by the end of 2010.
      The purpose of an index fund, being only to replicate the return of its benchmark index,
and not to outperform it, seemingly is simple. However, there are several difficulties in
attempting to replicate the return of an index, the main being that the index is measured as a
“paper portfolio” (Frino and Gallagher, 2001), and therefore is free of all the frictions that a
physical portfolio is exposed to.
      An investor holding an index fund does so in order to attain a risk-return relationship as
close to that of the benchmark index as possible. Because of this, how well an index fund tracks
its benchmark index, or in other words, its index-tracking performance, is what is most
important. Since physical portfolios are exposed to frictions, the potential effects of these on the
index-tracking performance are of vital importance, especially if the frictions are of a systematic
nature and to some extent could be avoided by the investor.
       In this thesis, we hypothesize that there are no systematic factors affecting the index-tracking performance of
Swedish index funds. In the process of testing this hypothesis, we provide an overview of the
performance of Swedish index funds, and how it varies across funds and over time, before we
investigate what external factors and fund characteristics affect both the index-tracking
performance and the risk-adjusted returns. The study is conducted on 14 Swedish index funds,
tracking six different Swedish equity indices, during the period 2008 through 2012. The
performance of the index funds is evaluated using five different measures. Three of them serve as
measures of the index-tracking performance, the fourth as a measure of the risk-adjusted return,
as well as an indicator of how passive the index fund truly is, and the fifth serves mainly as a
reference point for the fourth.
      Extensive research has been conducted on the performance of mutual funds overall, and
actively managed mutual funds in particular. However, not nearly as much time has been invested
in research on how well index funds track their benchmark index. Only a few studies have been

                                                                                1
conducted on the performance of index funds internationally and, to the best of our knowledge,
none have been conducted on the performance of Swedish index funds. Also, none of the studies
of index fund performance in other markets have extensively investigated the effects of fund
characteristics on performance. While the studies conducted on other markets find some
differences in the performance across index funds, our performance evaluation exposes
differences in the performance across Swedish index funds that are much larger. We also find
that Swedish index funds on average do worse in tracking their benchmark index than their U.S
counterparts. This makes the investigation of factors affecting the performance of Swedish index
funds all the more relevant, and the findings of this study could provide valuable insight to the
mechanics of passively managed Swedish funds. At the same time, the findings could also be of
use to investors in their choice among index funds.

   2. Previous literature and theory
2.1 Previous literature directly related to this study
The four most direct predecessors to this thesis are Dahlquist et al. (2000), Elton et al. (2004),
and Frino and Gallagher (2001 and 2002), and we implement some of the methodology used in
these studies in our study of Swedish index funds.
      Dahlquist et al. (2000) studied the relation between fund performance and fund attributes
in the Swedish market. The authors measured fund performance as the alpha in a linear
regression of fund returns on several benchmark indices, and then analyzed the cross-sectional
relation between performance and fund attributes. While it is a study of the performance of
Swedish mutual funds, Dahlquist et al. (2000) studied four different classes of mutual funds
during the period 1993 through 1997, measured performance as the risk-adjusted returns, and
placed no focus on index funds. While some of the 126 equity funds studied by Dahlquist et al.
(2000) may have been index funds, Garber and Logova (1999) found that in 1998, there were six
Swedish index funds in existence. We can thereby conclude that index funds could not have had
a large impact on the analyses performed by Dahlquist et al. (2000). Also, Dahlquist et al. (2000)
concluded that the Swedish mutual fund market offers an institutional setting and characteristics
similar to those of the U.S. market. This is of importance to us since we study Swedish index
funds, but gather a lot of information from studies performed on the U.S. markets.
      Elton et al. (2004) studied the performance of 54 index funds replicating the S&P 500
index over a five year period. They stated that the raw return differential is a somewhat naïve
measure of the performance of an index fund, and that a sophisticated investor should be more
concerned with the risk-adjusted return. They analyzed both raw and risk-adjusted return for the
funds and found that the funds did not replicate their benchmarks perfectly and concluded that
relative return could easily be predicted by factors like expenses and cash flow. The authors did
not state the same about index-tracking performance, upon which little focus was placed. This
publication is of importance to us since it utilizes risk-adjusted returns of index funds and
suggests potential factors affecting index fund performance.
      Frino and Gallagher (2001) examined the performance of U.S. index funds, using more
sophisticated measures of index-tracking performance based on the return differential, namely
tracking errors. They found that the index funds underperformed their benchmark indices, and
they also found seasonality effects on the tracking errors. Frino and Gallagher (2002) found the
same underperformance for Australian index replicating mutual funds, and investigated what
factors affected the tracking errors. The factors most focused upon in these studies are external
factors, and the effects of these factors on the risk-adjusted returns of the funds are not

                                                                                3
investigated. In this thesis, we investigate the effects of both external factors and fund
characteristics on index fund performance.

2.2 Index methodology and replication strategies
There is more than one strategy for replicating the benchmark index, and there is more than one
type of equity index. Indices can be capitalization weighted, which means that the weights are
updated constantly as the stocks change in value, or they can have fixed weights, revised at
regular points in time. The capitalization weighted indices are also revised regularly, when
decisions are made on what constituents are to be included or excluded.
      Some of the most common ways of replicating an index are full replication, stratified
sampling and portfolio optimization, as presented by Liu, Sheikh and Stefek (1998), and Olma
(1998). Full replication is the simplest way to replicate the index. It requires holding the exact or
nearly exact weights in the same stocks as the benchmark index. Normally, the fund manager sets
a tolerance level, letting the weight of a stock in the fund portfolio deviate by no more than X
basis points before rebalancing is required. If the fund follows a capitalization weighted index,
tracking the index perfectly requires constant rebalancing, which would impose high transaction
costs. Blume and Edelen (2004) concluded that less than half of the index funds tracking the S&P
500 always follow an exact replication strategy.
      Stratified sampling is a way of replicating an index without holding all the stocks on the
constituent list. Rudd (1980) expressed two significant advantages of replicating an index by
holding as few stocks as possible: decreased transaction costs, and decreased administrative
overhead for holding and monitoring a large portfolio. Stratified sampling might be a necessity if
the benchmark index is very large (in terms of the number of constituents) and/or is not very
liquid. There are several variants of stratified sampling, differing primarily in the risk measure and
the technique used to match the characteristics of the benchmark index. The stocks in the
benchmark index are normally characterized according to several parameters (e.g. size, dividend
yield, industry, etc.), and the fund wealth is then allocated in weights such that it replicates the
weights of those parameters in the benchmark index. This gives the fund similar risk and return
characteristics to those of the benchmark index, with the notion being that a relatively small
number of high capitalization stocks will account for a large part of the index return.
      Optimized portfolios utilize a mathematical approach to achieve the same objective as
stratified sampling, which is to create a portfolio with similar risk and return characteristics as
those of the benchmark index, given certain constraints. According to Rudd (1980), optimization
is the natural way of specifying the passive portfolio selection problem. It requires a multi-factor

                                                                                4
model describing all the stocks in a benchmark, along with several factors (e.g. size, beta, yield,
etc.), and how these factors co-move with each other. Optimization also requires history of these
attributes in order to establish the risk-return relationship between them. The fund wealth is then
allocated in a way that replicates the risk and return characteristics of the benchmark index within
certain constraints, such as the number of stocks and transaction costs.
      All the strategies mentioned above are physical replication strategies, meaning that they all
require holding stocks in the benchmark index. Another replication strategy is synthetic
replication. Hill and Naviwala (1999) described this strategy as replicating index returns by buying
stock index futures and swaps, rather than the underlying stocks that make up the index. Miller
and Meckel (1999) offered a similar description, stating that it is a derivatives-based approach
typically accomplished through the purchase of futures contracts on the benchmark index.

2.3 Asset pricing theory
In our study, we incorporate models from the asset pricing theory, and there are previous studies
within the field that are of large magnitude to our work, and to the field in general. In 1964,
Sharpe published Capital Asset Prices: A Theory of Market Equilibrium, which is the foundation of
the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM). This was later evolved by Lintner (1965) and Mossin
(1966), into the CAPM as it is known today. Next came the alpha measure, which was first
defined by Jensen (1968), and is an expansion upon the CAPM. In 1977, Roll and Ross presented
their well-known critique against the CAPM, the most important point of which being that the
market portfolio is unobservable, meaning that a benchmark error occurs when a proxy is used as
a benchmark for performance. Roll (1978) also stated that the Security Market Line (SML) is
affected by the choice of benchmark, which implies changes in the beta and thereby affects the
alpha of the asset. Because of these pointed out limitations of the CAPM, alternatives have been
suggested. The most well-known are factor models such as those presented by Chen, Roll and
Ross (1986) and Fama and French (1996).

2.4 Fund performance
This is a study of fund performance, focusing exclusively on index funds. There is a vast amount
of literature written in the field of mutual fund performance, and some of it is also specifically
aimed at index funds. However, no studies focus on Swedish index funds. Since Jensen (1968),
many studies and articles, such as Sharpe (1991), Elton et al. (1993), Malkiel (1995), Daniel et al.
(1997), have stated that on average, actively managed mutual funds have not been able to
outperform passive counterparts. Further on, according to a recent publication by Ferreira et al.

                                                                                5
(2012), mutual funds underperform the market overall. Research by Fama and French (2010) has
also shown that only a few equity mutual funds produce benchmark-adjusted returns enough to
cover their costs. Gruber (1996) also highlighted this in his assessment of why, despite it often
being a bad choice, investors in the U.S. continue to choose actively managed funds over index
funds. In the same study, the author also found that while the index funds on average
underperform their benchmark indices, they outperform actively managed funds. Similar findings
were made by Frino and Gallagher (2001), who found that S&P 500 index funds, on average,
outperformed their active counterparts after expenses. Despite Gruber et al. (1996) finding that
investors choose actively managed funds over passive alternatives, Elton et al. (2013) stated that
more than 13% of the assets managed by mutual funds are held in passive portfolios. This is a
finding which further highlights the importance of passive investment vehicles, such as index
funds.
      Two of the performance measures incorporated in this study are variations of the tracking
error, on which some influential studies have been conducted. One of the earliest were Roll
(1992), who came up with a suggestion on how to minimize portfolio tracking error volatility,
defining tracking error as the performance of a portfolio relative to its benchmark. Others are
Pope and Yadav (1994), Larsen and Resnick (1998), and Frino and Gallagher (2001). Roll (1992)
defined tracking error as the fund return relative to its benchmark index, and also stated that the
prespecified benchmark usually is a broadly diversified index of assets. In addition to this, Roll
(1992) expressed a similar sentiment to Elton et al. (2004), in that even for non-indexed equity
funds, tracking error may be an important input for fund sponsors and money managers in their
decision-making. Pope and Yadav (1994), in the introduction to their investigation of serial
correlation of returns and its effect on tracking error, stated that tracking error estimates are
particularly influential in structuring and managing index funds. In that study, Pope and Yadav
(1994), specified a more sophisticated way of measuring tracking error than Roll (1992). Larsen
and Resnick (1998) also specified another definition of measuring tracking error. In their study of
tracking errors of S&P 500 index funds, Frino and Gallagher (2001) used three different methods
to measure tracking error.
     When it comes to variations in performance over time, some studies of definitive
magnitude have been conducted. Keim (1983) documented seasonal effects in security returns in
the month of January, referred to as the January effect. He showed that over the last 70 years,
small stocks have by far outperformed larger stocks in the month of January. He stated that the
cause for this was tax loss selling by the end of the year. The January effect was also confirmed
by Thaler (1987), and Musto (1997). Musto stated that another cause for the January effect was

                                                                                6
window dressing, meaning that mutual funds shape up their balance sheets before the end of the
year when they will be inspected, in order to hide poor portfolio choices.
      In order to test our results for robustness, they have to be adjusted for potential biases.
The effects of various biases on the studies of mutual fund performance have been documented
in several previous publications. Brown et al. (1992) and Elton et al. (1996) presented empirical
evidence that funds performing poorly are more likely to be closed down or merged with other
funds compared to funds performing well. This causes the performance measures of mutual
funds to be biased upwards in much of the fund performance literature. The concept is further
described as survivorship bias by Kothari et al. (1995), meaning that only information on
surviving firms and funds is available. The effects of survivorship bias on Swedish mutual funds
were quantified by Dahlquist et al. (2000). Another bias documented is omission bias. Artega et
al. (1998) stated that return biases can be created through mutual fund incubation. They argued
that the companies offering funds can create multiple funds in the same period and run them
privately during an incubation time, letting each take different directions in their investments and
after the outcomes are known, the high performing funds will be marketed and the poor
performing ones will be dropped. Therefore, omission bias would enhance the returns of
managed portfolios relative to the index. To handle this potential bias, Prather and Middleton
(2002) excluded all funds started during the sample period. Another potential bias is the “look-
ahead”, or multi-selection bias, meaning that information not available at the beginning of the
studied period is incorporated in the study. Baquero et al. (2005) performed a study of how
survivorship and “look-ahead” biases affected hedge-fund performance and found that
performance was biased upwards.

2.5 Factors affecting fund performance
The main purpose of this thesis is to investigate what factors affect the performance of Swedish
index funds. Several studies have been conducted concerning the effect of both external and
internal factors, such as fund characteristics, on the performance of mutual funds. However, the
main part of them does not investigate the effects on index fund performance, but instead focus
on mutual funds overall, or actively managed mutual funds exclusively. When it comes to external
factors and their effect on fund performance, some literature of significance to this thesis has
been published. Keim (1999) stated that the liquidity of the index and the securities upon which a
passive portfolio is based can affect the trading costs of the portfolio, and thereby the tracking
error. Beneish and Whaley (1996) documented the “front-runner” effect, which means that
traders quickly change their portfolio positions upon the announcement of an index composition

                                                                                7
change, in the anticipation that index fund supply and demand of the stocks excluded or with
reduced weighting and the stocks included or with increased weighting will lead to profits on the
date of the index composition change. Also related to index funds, Chiang (1998) stated that how
the fund incorporates dividends have somewhat lagging effects on the index-tracking
performance. This was also supported by Frino and Gallagher (2002), who stated that the fund
manager must wait to receive the dividends in cash before being able to re-invest it. Therefore,
there is likely to be a negative relationship between the level of dividends and fund tracking
performance. Chiang (1998) also stated that the tracking error of index funds is affected by the
volatility of the underlying index, changes in the composition of the index, and corporate activity
in or related to the index constituents. The notion that the volatility of the underlying index
affects the tracking error was echoed by Frino and Gallagher (2002), who also concluded that the
choice of index replication strategy affected the magnitude of the tracking errors.
      As for fund characteristics, there are many of which the effects on fund performance have
been investigated. In one study of U.S. mutual fund performance, Ippolito (1989) found that
mutual funds with high fees performed well enough to offset these higher fees. This finding was
contradicted in later studies, where Elton et al. (1993) and Carhart (1997) both found that fees
are negatively related to mutual fund returns. As for the Swedish market, Dahlquist et al. (2000),
found that the risk-adjusted returns of Swedish mutual equity funds were negatively affected by
fees, and Choi et al. (2010) found that there was no value in investing in index funds with higher
fees based non-portfolio services bundled with the index fund.
      Closely related to fund fees are fund expenses, which incorporate the fees. Several studies
have been conducted on fund expenses and their effect on performance. Otten and Bams (2002),
in a study of European mutual funds, found that for funds in France, Germany, the Netherlands,
and the UK, the fund expense ratio had a negative influence on risk-adjusted performance. Elton
et al. (2004) found that the size of fund expense ratios may account for a large portion of the
return differentials of U.S. index funds. In line with these findings, Blitz et al. (2012) found that
also for passive European funds, expense ratios are an important determinant of returns. Frino
and Gallagher (2001) found that the margin of underperformance of index funds compared to
their benchmark index was roughly equivalent to the average expense ratio. Ferreira et al. (2012)
found a negative relation between expense ratios and net-of-fees performance, but the relation
was not statistically significant for US funds and only significant for some non-US funds. This is
not surprising, since evidence of both a negative and a positive relation between expenses and
performance has been presented. In connection to expenses, transaction costs and trading
activity leading to transaction costs is also a factor that has been investigated. In a study of the

                                                                                8
quarterly performance of U.S. mutual funds between 1975 and 1984, Grinblatt and Titman
(1989) found that transaction costs are inversely related to fund size. Dahlquist et al. (2000)
found that trading costs affected the risk-adjusted returns of regular Swedish equity funds
negatively. As for index funds, Chiang (1998) stated that transaction costs is one of the keys to
why it is so hard for indexers to match an index in performance.
      With regards to fund size and its effect on performance, Chen et al. (2004) found that fund
size erodes performance in actively managed funds. However, the authors noted that for
indexers, being large could be an asset, since it yields economies of scale. Ferreira et al. (2012)
found that size has a negative influence on fund performance in the US and a positive effect on
non-US funds. Otten and Bams (2002) found that for European mutual funds overall, fund
assets had a significantly positive relationship to risk-adjusted returns, while Dahlquist et al.
(2000) found that size had little effect on the risk-adjusted returns of regular Swedish equity
funds.
      Related to the size of funds, is the potential effect of in- and outflows of capital to the
fund. Chiang (1998) stated that the tracking error of index funds is affected by client cash inflows
and outflows. For regular Swedish funds, Dahlquist et al. (2000) only found weak evidence of a
relation between flow and risk-adjusted returns. For index funds, Frino and Gallagher (2002),
found that capital in- and outflow affected the index-tracking performance of Australian index
funds negatively. Ferreira et al. (2012) also studied the flow of capital under the hypothesis that
fund flows should have a positive correlation with future returns. In their sample of US funds
they found no evidence of this, but in their sample of non-US funds they found that funds that
received more new money subsequently performed better than those that receive less new
money. Related to capital and cash flows is also the potential impact of cash management for the
fund. Connor and Leland (1995) investigated the importance of cash management for index
funds, and concluded that a positive cash balance is often cost-efficient.
      Another fund characteristic previously studied is the fund age. Chen et al. (2004) tested if
fund age affected mutual fund performance, and found that there was a negative but statistically
insignificant relation. Ferreira et al. (2012) also investigated age as a factor potentially affecting
performance but, like Chen et al. (2004), they found no significant relationship.

2.6 Previous student work
As for work by previous students, there are some studies related to this thesis. Garber and
Logova (1999) performed a qualitative study of Swedish index funds and compared the Swedish
index fund market to foreign index fund markets. At the time there were six Swedish index funds

                                                                                9
and the authors concluded that the number would grow. Also related to this thesis is Winroth
(2007), who found that the majority of Swedish mutual funds underperformed their benchmark
index. Another student thesis that studied the effect of fund characteristics on mutual fund
performance is Jerner and Wingårdh (2011), who found that teams of two and three fund
managers outperform single-managed funds, while funds with teams of four managers or more
underperform relative to all other on a risk-adjusted return basis.

   3. Data
3.1 Data sampling and collection
This is a study of 14 Swedish index funds tracking Swedish equity indices during the period 2008
through 2012. The sampled funds are all of the funds judged by Morningstar Sweden to be Swedish
index funds tracking Swedish equity indices, with the exception of two that did not fit our criteria
for sample inclusion (see below). 12 of the funds are equity mutual funds, and the remaining two
are exchange-traded funds (ETFs). After submission of a student data request for data on all
Swedish index funds tracking Swedish equity indices, Morningstar Sweden kindly provided us with
data on the funds.
      We have obtained information on the fund characteristics included in our study from the
funds’ annual reports. Most of the funds provided the annual reports on their websites, but for
those that did not we contacted them directly to obtain the reports. The characteristics obtained
are total expenses net of transaction costs, transaction costs, portfolio turnover, total net assets
(henceforth called TNA), closing balance of cash and cash equivalents, net capital flow to the
fund, closing balance of financial securities other than stocks, and the fund age in years. While
some of the funds present interim reports, not all do. For consistency, we have therefore
gathered information on the fund characteristics solely on an annual basis.
     Each fund provides information on what benchmark index it aims to replicate in the
annual reports, and for those funds that had not yet produced annual reports for 2012, we
obtained information on which benchmark they aim to replicate from their websites. During the
studied time-period, the 14 sampled index funds tracked a total of six different equity indices.
Those were OMX Stockholm 30 (OMXS30), OMX Stockholm Benchmark (OMXSB), OMX
Stockholm Benchmark Cap (OMXSBCAP), OMX GES Ethical Sweden Index
(OMXETHICALSE), SIX Return Index (SIXRX), and SIX30 Return Index (SIX30RX). For the
OMX indices, there was variation among the funds with regards to whether they stated that they
follow the regular price index, where dividends are not reinvested, or the gross index (GI), where
dividends are reinvested. Index data for OMXS30, OMXSB, OMXSBCAP, and
OMXETHICALSE was obtained from the Nasdaq OMX Nordic Index website, and for SIXRX
and SIX30RX, index data was obtained from the SIX Trust Database. As a proxy for the risk-free
rate, we have used the return on the OMRX Treasury Bill 30 Day Index (OMRXTBILL30), and
as a proxy for monthly dividend yield, we have used the OMX Stockholm 30 Dividend Point
Index (OMXS30DVP). Index data on OMRXTBILL30 and OMXS30DVP was obtained from
the Nasdaq OMX Nordic Index website.

3.2 Fund inclusion and exclusion
Our criteria for overall sample inclusion were that the fund had not been started during 2012,
that the fund was registered in Sweden, and that the fund tracked an official Swedish equity

                            1
index. Two of the funds on which Morningstar Sweden provided us with data, were excluded from
the sample completely. One was excluded because it was started in 2012, and the other because it
was an ethical fund that made own modifications to its benchmark index, making a just return
comparison impossible. Also, in the raw dataset provided by Morningstar Sweden, some of the
funds were included more than once in different classes, namely dividend distributing and non-
dividend distributing (accumulating). We excluded those classes that distributed dividends in
order to have consistent and comparable Net Asset Values for all funds, since the funds with
only one class were all non-dividend distributing (accumulating). Other fund inclusion and
exclusion in the sample was made on an annual basis conditioned on the availability of annual
reports, and that the fund had not been started during the year. All funds that entered the sample
after 2008 did so in their first full year of existence, and any funds excluded in 2012 were
excluded because they had not yet published annual reports.
     See 9.1.1 in Appendix for summary of fund name, ISIN-number, fund type, the fund’s
stated current benchmark index, and the years the fund is included in the sample.

3.3 Data description
The dataset from Morningstar Sweden contains the name, base currency, symbol, ISIN- and CUSIP-
numbers, and daily Net Asset Values for all of the funds in the sample. The Net Asset Values are
the ones used by Morningstar Sweden to calculate fund returns, meaning that they are adjusted for
reinvested dividends (added back), and net of fund expenses. All data obtained from the annual
reports of the funds is in SEK amounts which are then used for manual factor calculation. Data
for all of the equity indices, the dividend index, and the T-Bill index was obtained in the form of
daily closing prices.

4.2 Performance evaluation
When it comes to measuring how well an index fund tracks its benchmark index, Frino and
Gallagher (2001 and 2002) showed that measures based on, but not as simple as, the return
differential are the most appropriate. The notion of index-tracking performance is that any
deviation from the return of the benchmark index, positive or negative, means that the index
fund is not perfectly replicating its benchmark index. As Frino and Gallagher (2001) stated, the
benchmark index is a “paper portfolio”, which does not truly exist. Therefore, it is not exposed
to the same frictions as real portfolios, meaning that a fund cannot normally replicate the
benchmark index without cost. With this in mind, we specify three measures in our evaluation of
the index-tracking performance of Swedish index funds. Two of them measure the dispersion of
the fund’s return around the benchmark index return, and the third measures how large a fraction
of the fund’s return is explained by the benchmark index return.
      As mentioned in the previous literature section, Elton et al. (2004) implied that for index
fund performance, the return differential between the index fund and the benchmark index (the
raw return differential) is a somewhat naïve measure, and that a more sophisticated investor
should be concerned with the risk-adjusted return. However, as is mentioned in the introduction
of this thesis, the purpose of an index fund is not to outperform the index on a raw or risk-
adjusted level, but instead to deliver raw and risk-adjusted returns as close as possible to those of
the benchmark index. This indicates that both the raw return differential and risk-adjusted
returns are of less importance, but that is not entirely the case. The risk-adjusted return can give
indications as to how passive an index fund really is. We therefore, in line with Elton et al. (2004),
present the risk-adjusted returns, as a more sophisticated return measure, but mainly as an
indicator of how passive the index fund truly is.

indication as to how passive an index fund really is. Alphas that deviate by large margins from the
difference in return between the fund and its benchmark index indicate that the index fund
actively makes portfolio composition decisions that deviate from the composition of the
benchmark index. For example, consider an index fund that, because of some friction,
underperforms its benchmark index by a certain low number of basis points on a raw return
basis, and exhibits an alpha of about the same as the raw underperformance. That fund is likely
to deviate little from the benchmark index in its portfolio composition, since the raw and risk-
adjusted returns are affected the same way by the friction. On the other hand, if the index fund
underperforms the benchmark index by the same number of basis points as in the previous
example, but exhibits a positive alpha, then that fund is likely to be making active decisions on its
portfolio composition that deviate from the composition of the benchmark portfolio. To
incorporate this in our performance evaluation, we include the alpha as an indicator of how
active the index fund is, and as a more sophisticated performance measure.
     Jensen’s alpha and beta are an expansion of the CAPM which, as mentioned in the
previous literature section, has come under scrutiny because one cannot observe the true market
portfolio, and therefore a benchmark error arises when a proxy is used. As an alternative,
multifactor models have been suggested. For index funds however, this is not an issue. Since we
know which benchmark the index fund aims to replicate, we also know which benchmark to
measure it against, and multifactor models are not necessary.

4.6 Test for external factors affecting index fund performance
Chiang (1998) stated that external factors such as dividends, index composition changes, and
index volatility have strong effects on the tracking errors of index funds. The underlying notion
regarding the dividends is that a “paper index” reinvests the dividends instantly, while there is a
delay between that date and the date when the fund receives and can reinvest the dividends. This
results in the fund performance deviating from the benchmark index performance. Frino and
Gallagher (2001) stated that the dividend yield of the index had a significant effect on the
tracking errors of S&P 500 index funds, but the authors (2002) did not find it to be significant for
Australian index funds.
      When it comes to index composition changes, there are two effects distorting the index-
tracking performance; the first one, which Chiang (1998) claims to have significant effect, is that
the necessary transactions incur transaction costs for the funds. The second one is the so-called
“front-runner effect”, as documented by Beneish and Whaley (1996). It means that when an
index composition change is announced, traders quickly change positions in speculation that
when the index funds rebalance their portfolios on the date the index composition change takes
place, the included stocks and the stocks with an increased weighting will rise in price and the
exluded stocks and the stocks with reduced weighting will fall in price. This increases the prices
of the stocks to be included in the index or that have an increased weighting, and decreases the
prices of those that are to be excluded or have a decreased weighting. Then, when the index
funds rebalance their portfolios on the date of the index composition change, they have to sell at
deflated prices and buy at inflated prices, causing the fund performance to deviate from the index
performance. The effect of index volatility is similar, in that high volatility should make it more
difficult to rebalance the portfolio and also to allocate investor capital inflows efficiently.
      We now investigate if index composition changes, dividends, and index volatility have
significant effects on the index-tracking performance and risk-adjusted returns of Swedish index

4.7 Test for fund characteristics affecting index fund performance
Also mentioned in the previous literature section is the vast amount of previous studies
documenting various significant effects of fund characteristics on mutual fund performance.
Consequently, the next step in this study is to investigate if specific fund characteristics have
significant effects on the index-tracking performance and risk-adjusted returns of Swedish index
funds. With the large variety of previous studies and publications as a foundation, we investigate
the effects of a total of seven different fund characteristics, proxied by eight different factors. All
the data on the fund characteristics has been collected on an annual basis from the annual
reports, so we therefore test their effects on the annual index-tracking performance and risk-
adjusted returns. The fund characteristics are the following:

4.7.1 Total expense ratio
The total expense ratio (TER) is a measure of the cost of a fund to an investor. It is defined as all
fund expenses except of transaction costs during a period (in our case, a year) expressed as a

                                                                               22
fraction of the average TNA that period. In other words, TER includes all expenses related to the
running of the fund, such as the fees charged by the fund management (by far the largest
component of the TER), and administration, distribution and deposition (normally at a bank)
costs. The underlying notion is that since the expenses are deducted from TNA, the ratio of
expenses to TNA should have a directly negative effect on the fund’s realized return and move
the fund return away from the return of the benchmark index, negatively affecting index-tracking
performance.

4.7.2 Trading activity
When a fund buys or sells securities, it results in transaction costs. The transaction costs are
deducted from the TNA of the funds, so higher trading activity is expected to have a negative
effect on the fund’s realized return and move the fund return away from the return of the
benchmark index. However, should trading activity have a positive effect on the realized returns,
to the extent that index-tracking performance and risk-adjusted performance are improved, this
would indicate that the fund is not as passive as it claims to be. Since the transaction costs
incurred are deducted from TNA, thereby reducing returns, an index fund that yields higher
returns when trading activity increases, cannot be tracking its benchmark index closely, and must
be making some active decisions to deviate from the index composition. In order to capture the
effect of trading activity on our sampled funds we use two proxies. The first one is the
transaction costs during the period, and the second one is the portfolio turnover during the
period. The portfolio turnover is measured as the minimum of securities bought and securities
sold, expressed as a fraction of average TNA during the period.

4.7.3 Size
The size of a fund has been documented to have various effects on the performance of mutual
funds. There are two reasons for us to include fund size as a factor potentially affecting the
index-tracking and risk-adjusted performance of Swedish index funds. The first one is that larger
size may bring benefits as economies of scale for index funds, as Chen (2004) mentions. Also,
larger size could potentially mean that rebalancing of the portfolio in connection to index
composition changes imposes more volatility than for a smaller fund. The notion behind this is
that the demand for securities added to or with increased weighting in the benchmark index
would be larger for a larger fund, since it has more capital to reallocate. The same applies to the
supply of those securities excluded from or with reduced weighting on the benchmark index. In

                                                                               23
other words, the “front-runner” effect, if significant, could potentially be larger for a large fund
than for a small fund.

4.7.4 Cash and cash equivalents
Connor and Leland (1995) found that the management of cash on the balance sheet is of
importance for index funds. Index funds tend to hold some portion of cash on their balance
sheet, as it allows them to absorb some of the transaction costs generated by temporary cash in-
and outflows. However, Connor and Leland (1995) state that holding cash when trying to
replicate an index that has no cash position should increase the tracking error, since the cash does
not have the same expected rate of return as the benchmark index.

4.7.5 Financial securities other than stocks
As has been mentioned, there are various strategies for replicating index returns. While some of
them mean only holding stocks in the index constituents, other strategies involve financial
securities other than stocks, such as swaps and futures contracts. The fraction of the portfolio
held in securities other than stocks can be seen as a proxy for fund replication strategy, in that it
measures to how large an extent the fund employs synthetic replication.

4.7.6 Flow
Flow has been incorporated in various studies of mutual fund performance. It is defined as the
growth in TNA that is not generated internally (under the assumption that dividends are
reinvested). The underlying notion is that high in- and outflows of capital force trading activity,
leading to transaction costs, which would affect the returns of the fund. While some studies, such
as Ferreira et al. (2012) have estimated the flow using a formula, our sampled funds provide
detailed information on investor capital in- and outflows in their annual report reports. We will
therefore not use a formula to estimate the capital in- and outflows, but instead calculate them
using the numbers provided in the annual reports.

4.7.7 Fund age
The fund age could potentially explain index-tracking and risk-adjusted performance of the index
funds, as it could be that older funds have more experience of how to implement index
replication strategies, and optimizing performance. On the other hand, young funds might be
more innovative, and outperform older funds with new strategies. Otten and Bams (2002) found
a significantly negative relationship between fund age and performance in the U.K. and Germany,

                                                                               24
and by incorporating the fund age in years since fund inception, we investigate if we can find
similar effects among Swedish index funds.

4.7.8 Fixed effects
Trends and external occurrences specific to a single year could potentially affect the index-
tracking and risk-adjusted performance of the funds. Macro-related events, such as the bursting
of the credit bubble, could cause large volatility in the market during specific years, potentially
leading to fund performance diverging more from the benchmark index performance than
otherwise. Also, characteristics unique to each index could have an effect. Larsen and Resnick
(1998) found that differences in market cap and index weighting affected tracking errors. To
account for effects specific to each year and each benchmark index, we incorporate dummies in
our analysis.

4.8 Robustness tests
As has been mentioned above, our dataset suffers from an “omission” bias, as it is defined by
Prather and Middleton (2002). The reason for this is that the funds started during the studied
period are excluded during their first year of existence, and three of the sampled funds are
excluded during 2012, due to them not having published annual reports for that year. Also, since

                                                                               25
funds started during the period are included, there is a certain degree of “look-ahead”, or multi-
selection bias, as is studied by Baquero et al. (2005). In addition to this, our dataset is very small,
which could lead to extreme outliers potentially having large effects that distort the results of the
analyses.
      To adjust for these biases we perform two types of robustness tests, where applicable. For
those regressions where we have data for multiple years ((10), (11), and (13)), we adjust for the
“omission” and “look-ahead” biases caused by the unbalanced dataset by re-running the
regressions and only including the funds that existed during the entire period from 2008 through
2012. We also adjust for the potential effects of extreme outliers by using 90 percent winsorizing
of all independent and dependent variables except dummy variables. For regression (12) we only
have factor data for 2012, and therefore cannot adjust for the “omission” and “look-ahead” bias.
We can, however, still adjust for extreme outliers, which we do by using 90 percent winsorizing
of the performance measures. The dividend yield is not winsorized, since in each month t, the
dividend yield is the same for all funds, meaning that no extreme outliers exist. All the regressions
in the robustness tests are run using standard OLS, and adjusted for heteroscedasticity and
autocorrelation.

add to the performance evaluation by presenting and commenting on the significance levels from
the Z-tests. After the performance evaluation, we present the results of our tests for significant
differences over time in the index-tracking performance and risk-adjusted returns. We then
present the results of our tests for external factors and fund characteristics affecting index-
tracking performance and risk-adjusted returns. Finally, we present the results of our robustness
tests.

      Looking at the average return differentials (Rp - Rb) and the average alphas, they stand in

not the same.

for the performance measures. While the results found in other markets are from other sample
periods, they still provide insight as to how index funds have previously performed in other
markets, and can act as benchmarks for performance comparison. By comparing our results to
those found by other authors, we can find some interesting contrasts; Elton et al. (2004) found

more similar among the funds for U.S. index funds than for Swedish index funds. Elton et al.
(2004) also found that U.S. index funds delivered an average annual beta of 0.998, with a range
from 0.979 to 1.005. Compared to Swedish index funds, the dispersion of return differentials,
alphas, and betas is much smaller for U.S. index funds than for Swedish ones.

As is made evident by the regression results, there are significant differences over time in the
TE2, and the alpha- and beta-values. All the F-statistics are highly significant, and the coefficients

May. While the t-statistic is not significant, the coefficient combined with Graphic 1 indicates
that TE2 increases sharply in June. TE2 also decreases quite sharply by the end of the year

seems to decrease to its lowest level in June (as can also be seen in 9.2.4 in the Appendix). The

lowest in December, and is also very different between January and February, with a sharp
increase in February, to the highest level of the year.

changes are the most significant. The TE2 is at its highest in January, while the alpha is at its
lowest. In March, the TE2 decreases while the alpha increases, and in December, the TE2
decreases while the alpha increases. This implied reverse correlation between TE2 and alpha is
important, as it indicates that when the index funds deviate significantly from the benchmark in
terms of raw returns, it has a significantly negative effect on the risk-adjusted returns.

As can be seen in table 6, regressions (11) and (12) indicate that TE2 is increased by index
revisions and index volatility. This means that the index-tracking performance is negatively
affected by those factors, as an increase in TE2 means that the fund return is moved further away
from the benchmark index return. The coefficient of the index revision dummy being so small
could be because the changes in the index composition are normally not large enough to cause
large transaction costs. Sometimes no change at all is made on the revision date. Also, there may
not be a lot of “front-running” causing portfolio rebalancing difficulties, and, some of the funds
may engage in “front-running” themselves, to mitigate the effects. Finally, the effects of the index
revisions could be carried over months, and not be apparent until the month after the revision is
made.
      The monthly index volatility most likely increases TE2 because efficient portfolio
rebalancing and allocation of new capital inflows becomes more difficult when the index is
volatile. This is since it is more difficult to buy and sell the securities at optimal prices when the
prices are very volatile, especially if you need to allocate your capital somewhat quickly. The
effect of the dividend yield on the tracking error is not significant and the coefficient is negative.
One possible cause for this could be that the lagging effect between the reinvestment of
dividends in the benchmark index and the distribution of the dividends in cash to the funds does

                                                                               33
not carry over months. This would mean that the effect of the dividend yield is not visible when
studying the absolute monthly return differential.
      While the index revision dummy has no significant effect on the alphas and the betas, the
dividend yield has a significantly negative effect on both the alphas and the betas. When the
dividends are reinvested in the benchmark index, the correlation between the fund’s excess return
and the benchmark index excess return (the beta) should decrease. This is because the dividends
are incorporated (reinvested) in the benchmark index return immediately while, as mentioned
above, there is a lagging effect until the fund receives and can reinvest the dividends. When the
fund does receive and reinvest its dividends, there is a reverse effect when the received dividends
are incorporated in the fund return but have already been incorporated in the index return. This
causes the correlation between the fund’s excess return and the benchmark index excess return to
decrease further.
      Since the alpha is the return adjusted for systematic risk, or beta (Bodie et al, 2011), the fact
that dividends have a small but significantly negative effect on the alpha, is likely to be caused by
some idiosyncratic factor. One such factor could be the time that passes between the
reinvestment of dividends in the benchmark index and the reinvestment of dividends received by
the funds. It leads to the fund not being able to reinvest its received dividends in the exact same
way as the benchmark index, since the market conditions are ever-changing, and security prices
would have changed in the time that has passed. Another factor not systematic to the benchmark
index is the potential effect of the costs incurred when the fund reinvests the dividends. For
complete regression results, see 9.3.3 in Appendix.

As can be seen in Table 7, the characteristics vary a lot across funds and, as has been shown
above, there is also a wide dispersion in the performance across the funds. Therefore, it does not
seem unlikely that some of the differences in the performance of the funds are caused by fund
characteristics. Another interesting point, shown when comparing Table 7 to Table 1, is that the
average yearly underperformance, measured by Rp - Rb, is larger than the sum of the average
yearly expenses (both TER and transaction costs).
      The results of the single panel regressions (presented in Table 8), with all fixed effects
incorporated, indicate that the index-tracking performance is indeed affected by several of the
fund characteristics. The total expense ratio (TER) has a large significant effect on the tracking
errors, in that it increases both of the measures (TE1 and TE2), and thereby affects index-tracking
performance negatively. The beta is also heavily negatively affected by the total expense ratio,
with a highly significant coefficient of –31.0994. This further fortifies the notion that expenses
affect index-tracking performance negatively. It is interesting that the TER has a larger than one-
for-one negative effect on the index-tracking performance. If we consider the fact that the TER
is deducted in full from the NAV, then we would expect the TER to increase the absolute return
differential (TE2) by the exact or close to the exact fraction of NAV that is deducted. However,
as our findings show, an increase in the TER increases the TE2 more than the increase in the
fraction deducted from the NAV. One possible explanation for this could be that the funds with
higher TERs actually do worse in tracking their benchmark index on a pre-expenses basis. This
explanation would also indicate that, since fees charged by the fund management are by far the
largest component of the TER, the funds with management charging the highest fees perform
worse on a pre-fee basis. The NAVs obtained from Morningstar Sweden are net of expenses (and
thereby also net of fees), and we do not have the exact dates when the expenses have been
deducted from the NAVs. Therefore, we have not been able to test for these potential
explanations.
      As for the trading activity, it has a significantly negative effect on two of the three measures
of index-tracking performance, with transaction costs having a highly significant coefficient of
5.0765 on TE1, and a highly significant coefficient of –75.4689 on the beta-value. Interestingly,

                                                                               36
while the coefficient of transaction costs is highly positive, the effect on TE2 is not statistically
significant. The fact that the trading activity, proxied by the transaction costs, has a much larger
than one-for-one effect on the index-tracking performance indicates that it is not only the actual
costs that affect performance. Since the trading is a result of external and internal factors, such as
the index replication strategy, the weighting of the index, and capital in- and outflows to the fund,
effects of those are likely to be incorporated in the trading activity. However, it is beyond the
scope of this study to investigate the effects of those factors on trading activity. Aside of
transaction costs, the other proxy for trading activity, turnover, has a small but significant
positive coefficient on the TE1, meaning a negative effect on index-tracking performance. The
turnover not having an effect as large and significant as that of the transaction costs could be
caused by the fact that the turnover does not reflect the full trading activity, but only the
minimum of either securities bought or securities sold.
      The final fund characteristic that is of significance to the index-tracking performance is the
fraction of financial assets held in securities other than stocks. It has a significant positive
coefficient on the TE1, and a significant negative coefficient on the beta-value. This indicates that
those funds holding other securities than the constituents in their benchmark index, deliver worse
index-tracking performance, and that partial synthetic replication thereby affects index-tracking
performance negatively.

U.S. index funds, there seems to be some other element present. If one looks at how the R (see
9.3.6 in Appendix) of the regression with alpha as the dependent variable changes as more fixed
effects are incorporated, it seems as though effects specific to each year add a lot of explanatory
power. Such effects specific to each year could be adjustments of the replication strategy and
decisions on portfolio composition that varies in line with market conditions, but these effects
are beyond the scope of this thesis, and we can only speculate on them.
      Since the average alphas of the Swedish index funds deviate largely from the differential
returns and the index-tracking performance varies largely across the funds, we would assume that
many of the funds do not employ a full replication strategy. Therefore, we expected the portion
of the portfolio held in financial securities other than stocks to have a significant effect on the
alphas, as that factor is dependent on the index replication strategy. The fact that it does not have
a significant effect indicates that this is not a strong enough proxy for the replication strategy, and
that whether or not a fund uses partial synthetic replication has little effect on the risk-adjusted
returns.

5.5 Robustness of results
5.5.1 Seasonality effects on index fund performance
As can be seen in 9.4.1 in the Appendix, after adjusting for the “omission” and “look-ahead”
biases, as well as winsorizing dependent variables 90 percent, the significance levels (F- and T-
statistics) are reduced and the coefficients are somewhat changed. This is not surprising, since the
number of observations is reduced and extreme outliers no longer affect the results. However,
the F-statistics are still significant, and the results still indicate significant seasonality effects on
the performance of Swedish index funds. The average TE2 is still the highest in January, and has
the same t-statistic as before the adjustments. Also, TE2 is still the lowest in May, with a slightly
less negative coefficient and with somewhat lower significance. The TE2 still decreases sharply in
December.
      The average monthly alpha is still at its highest in April, with a lowered coefficient, but still
highly significant. The alpha still increases significantly in July, albeit with a lowered coefficient.
Also, the average alpha still increases sharply in December.
      The average beta in January has increased, but is still highly significant. The average beta is
still the highest in February, but the coefficient is no longer significant. Also, the average beta is
still the lowest in December. The significance levels of the monthly coefficients on the beta-
values are the ones that have been the most reduced by the adjustments made for the biases.
However, the F-statistic is still significant at a 0.05 level, and the coefficients of the months are

                                                                               38
still not close to zero. So, after adjusting for the biases, the results still indicate that the
seasonality effects are significant for all the performance measures.

5.5.2 External factors affecting index fund performance
For specification of the robustness-adjusted regression results, see 9.4.2 in the Appendix. After
90 percent winsorizing, making the adjustments for the biases, and with the year dummies
included, we find that the index revisions no longer have a significant effect on TE1. The effect
of the monthly index volatility on TE1 has decreased somewhat, as has its t-statistic, but the
effect is still highly significant. The index revisions still have no significant effects on the alphas
and the betas. We can conclude that the effect of the index revisions on TE1 was not robust,
since the effect is not significant after adjusting for extreme outliers and the omission and “look-
ahead” biases that our dataset suffers from. This is not very surprising, as the effect found
initially was small.
       After 90 percent winsorizing, the dividend yield still has no significant effect on TE1, and
the effect on the alphas is identical to before. The effect of the dividend yield on the monthly
beta is very similar to the effect before the winsorizing. The coefficient has changed to become
slightly less negative, and the effect is still highly significant. From this we can conclude that the
dividend yield has a robust significant effect on the alphas and the betas.

5.5.3 Fund characteristics affecting index fund performance
For specification of the robustness-adjusted regression results, see 9.4.3-9.4.6 in the Appendix.
After 90 percent winsorizing, making the adjustments for the biases, and with the year and index
dummies included, we find that the total expense ratio has an increased negative effect on index-
tracking performance. This can be seen as an indication that the performance of the funds truly
was skewed upwards by the biases, as was found by Prather and Middleton (2002) and Baquero
et al. (2005). The coefficient of the effect of the total expense ratio on TE1 has increased quite a
lot, and is still significant. The increase in the effect of the total expense ratio on TE2 is similar to
that in the effect on TE1, and the effect is also still significant. The coefficient of the total
expense ratio on the beta has decreased to become even more negative, and the effect is still
significant.
      As for the transaction costs, their effect on index-tracking performance has also become
increasingly negative. The effect of the transaction costs on TE1 has increased quite a lot, as has
the significance level. The effect of transaction costs on TE2, has now become significant and the

                                                                               39
coefficient has increased by more than 50 percent. Finally, the effect of transaction costs on the
beta-value has also become increasingly negative, and increasingly significant.
      The fact that the effect of the transaction costs on TE2 has gone from being insignificant to
being significant at the 0.05 level indicates that our initial tests were not entirely robust. However,
we can still conclude that both the total expense ratio and the transaction costs have a
significantly negative effect on index-tracking performance, since the effects on all the measures
of index-tracking performance now are significantly negative. We may not be able to make any
strong conclusions about the exact effects, but we can tell that they are larger than one-for-one.
      As for the other fund characteristics, the effects of turnover and financial instruments on
TE1, and of financial instruments on beta, are no longer significant. Instead, fund size now has a
significantly negative effect on TE1 (positive coefficient) and beta, while cash has a significantly
negative effect on TE1 (positive coefficient), TE2 (positive coefficient), and the beta. Fund age
also has a significantly positive effect on the beta. This indicates that we cannot make any strong
conclusions on the effects of these other fund characteristics, as the differences in their effects
before and after adjusting for the biases and extreme outliers are very large.

   6. Conclusions

The main purpose of this study was to investigate what factors affect the index-tracking
performance of Swedish index funds. In the process, we have provided an overview of the
performance of the Swedish index funds, and how it varies across funds and over time. We have
used five different performance measures, three that measure index-tracking performance, one
that measures risk-adjusted return, and provides an indication of how passive the fund truly is,
and one that measures fund return relative to the benchmark index return. Our results show that
the index-tracking performance of Swedish index funds varies a lot across funds. The variation is
much larger than that previously exhibited among their U.S. counterparts, as is made evident by
comparison to the results of Frino and Gallagher (2001), and Elton et al (2004). The same
comparison also shows that Swedish index funds, on average, deliver worse index-tracking
performance than that previously exhibited by their U.S. counterparts. However, in terms of risk-
adjusted return, Swedish index funds do better than their U.S. counterparts have done. U.S. index
funds have, on average, delivered negative risk-adjusted returns, while Swedish index funds do
not deliver risk-adjusted returns significantly different from zero. The differences between the
return differential and the risk-adjusted return are much larger for the Swedish index funds than
for their U.S. counterparts. This indicates that Swedish index funds, on average, deviate more
from their benchmark index in their portfolio composition than U.S. index funds have done.
      We can conclude that there are significant differences in the index-tracking performance
across funds. Five of the funds perform significantly better than the other nine on a monthly
basis. Empirically, we have also found that there are quite large differences in the index-tracking
performance across the funds depending on what benchmark index they track. Funds tracking
the indices with fewer and larger constituents deliver better index-tracking performance. The
notion of there being large differences depending on benchmark index is further indicated by the
fact that index-fixed effects add a lot of explanatory power to the regressions testing the effect of
fund characteristics on index-tracking performance.
      Furthermore, we have found that there are seasonal effects having a significant influence
on how the performance of Swedish index funds varies over time. Our results show that the
monthly index fund performance is affected by index-volatility and dividends, so we can
conclude that these factors, at least to some extent, cause the seasonal differences. Index volatility
is likely to be affected by numerous factors specific to each benchmark index. This lends further
credibility to our empirical finding that there are quite large differences in index-tracking
performance depending on what benchmark index the funds track. Additionally, we find that
there are two fund characteristics that have a significant negative impact on the index-tracking

                                                                               41
performance. They are the total expense ratio and the transaction costs, and the negative effects
of both fund characteristics on the index-tracking performance are larger than one-for-one. We
can thereby conclude that these fund characteristics, at least to some extent, explain the
differences in index-tracking performance across funds. As for the risk-adjusted return, we did
not find the same negative relationship between expenses and the risk-adjusted return for
Swedish index funds as has previously been found by Dahlquist et al. (2000) for regular Swedish
equity funds.
      These findings allow us to reject our hypothesis. Index volatility, dividends, the total expense ratio,
and the transaction costs all have significant effects on the index-tracking performance of
Swedish index funds.
      Since an investor holds an index fund to attain a risk-return relationship as close to that of
the benchmark index as possible, it is the index-tracking performance that is of most importance
when choosing an index fund. Our results therefore indicate that, for an investor, the total
expense ratio and the trading activity are the most important criteria when selecting a Swedish
index fund. The fund’s choice of benchmark index is also of importance, since the index-tracking
performance differs depending on the number and the size of the constituents that make up the
chosen benchmark index. Also, when evaluating the fund’s reported past performance, it is
important to confirm that the fund benchmarks its performance against the most relevant form
of the tracked benchmark index. For an index fund that reinvests its dividends, the most
appropriate form is a gross return index. We have noted that some funds who reinvest their
dividends state price return indices as their benchmark. This could be seen as false marketing,
since it makes their performance relative to the benchmark appear better than it really is.

   7. Problematization and future research

The main problem with our dataset is, as mentioned, that the number of observations is very
small. This cannot in any large extent be attributed to a selection bias, since the sample includes
all the index funds classified by Morningstar Sweden as Swedish index funds tracking Swedish equity
indices except of two, which did not fit our criteria. However, it still makes our results less
reliable, and not as conclusive as they might have been, and it is possible that we had found more
factors affecting the fund performance if we would have had a larger dataset. It is evident that the
small number of observations makes the dataset less robust, since part of our results are
significantly affected by the winsorizing of outliers, as well as the adjustment for the omission
and “look-ahead” biases. In the future, more data will be available, and more index funds are
likely to be started. We have already observed a growth in funds during the period studied that
has biased our dataset, since they have entered the sample during the studied period.
Incorporating more observations is likely to provide further explanations of the performance of
Swedish index funds.
      In our gathering and incorporation of fund characteristics and external factors, we have not
been able to consult a single objective database, but have obtained the data manually. This means
that we are heavily reliant on the funds providing correct and accurate information in their annual
reports which, while we assume it to be the case, cannot be completely certain in the case of
information that the funds are not required to disclose. For this reason we have, for consistency,
re-calculated the fund characteristics whenever they are expressed as ratios. However, sufficient
information is not provided on the portfolio turnover in the annual reports for us to calculate it
ourselves, so for that characteristic we have had to rely upon the numbers presented. In addition
to this, the fact that the fund characteristics are gathered on an annual basis could mean that the
numbers for some of them are not representable of the development during the entire period.
Therefore, it is possible that more significant effects of other fund characteristics had been
observable if interim data had been used. But, as mentioned previously, all of the funds do not
provide interim reports. The alternative, which would have been to study the effects of fund
characteristics on the funds’ performance over time-periods not equally long for each fund,
would have been inconsistent and highly likely to have biased the results. Another problem
related to the reports published by the funds, is that even for index funds, one cannot be
completely certain that there is no window dressing of the balance sheets or portfolios before the
closing balance dates.
      As has been mentioned, our dataset suffers from a survivorship bias, in that it only includes
funds active at the end of the studied period (the end of 2012). Brown et al. (1992), and Elton et

                                                                               43
al. (1996) both presented empirical evidence that survivorship biases cause performance measures
to be biased upwards, distorting performance evaluation and analysis. The dataset provided to us
by Morningstar Sweden only included the funds studied, and therefore does not allow us to adjust
for the effects of the survivorship bias. Had we had access to data on all Swedish index funds
during the studied period, including those that have been discontinued or merged with other
funds, we could have performed our study without the survivorship bias. Dahlquist et al. (2000)
quantified the magnitude of the survivorship bias in their study of Swedish mutual funds and had
we had a dataset free of survivorship bias, we could have performed a similar quantification. It is
however, as mentioned by Jerner and Wingårdh (2011), not reasonable to expect Morningstar
Sweden to keep data on funds that no longer exist, since there is no gain in it for them. Morningstar
Sweden directs its services mainly to non-academic customers, for which funds no longer in
existence are of little importance.
      Our results show that both the total expense ratio and the transaction costs have a larger
than one-for-one negative effect on index-tracking performance. However, our candidate
explanations for this are only speculations, and our data does not allow for an investigation of
whether they hold true or not. Since the total expense ratio and the transaction costs are the only
fund characteristics found to have a robust significant effect on the index-tracking performance,
a thorough investigation of what factors affect them, and of how performance differs before and
after they are deducted, could be of vital importance.
      This study does not to any large extent incorporate the various types of index replication
strategies implemented among index funds. While our results indicate that some of the funds
deviate more from full replication than others, we do not have any detailed information on what
strategies they employ in tracking their benchmark index. As there are large differences in the
index-tracking performance of the funds in our study, the replication strategy seems to be of
importance. One potential topic for future research that this brings about is the effect of the
choice of index replication strategy on the performance of an index fund. This could either be
investigated by initially performing a detailed style analysis of the index funds, or through direct
gathering of information, given cooperation from the funds. Then, a test can be performed in
order to investigate if the choice of replication strategy leads to differences in the performance of
the funds. A study of index replication strategies and their impact on performance could
potentially provide explanations to the risk-adjusted returns, something this study only managed
to identify one factor that had a significant effect upon.

The Family Factor: Family Ownership and Firm Performance in the
European Market

Abstract:

   We study the impact of family ownership on firm performance within the STOXX
Europe 600 index over the period 2010 to 2022. Using a sample of 577 firms, of
which 98 are family-owned, we employ a two-way fixed effects OLS regression model
to investigate the relationships between family ownership and two firm performance
measures: stock return and return on assets (ROA). Our analysis differentiates be-
tween high and low levels of family ownership, as well as younger and older family
firms, to assess performance nuances. The findings indicate that family-owned firms
generally exhibit higher ROA than non-family firms, suggesting that family owner-
ship contributes positively to firm profitability. Family-owned firms have higher stock
returns over our studied period, however, we cannot deduce that it is the effect of
family ownership. Additionally, we present evidence that higher family ownership
significantly increases firm performance compared to lower family ownership.

   Family ownership, Firm performance, Controlling shareholder

I. Introduction

Numerous globally successful firms are controlled by families, such as Porsche, Hoffmann-
La Roche, and LVMH Mo¨et Hennessy Louis Vuitton. Bernard Arnault, the CEO
of LVMH and head of the family, asserts that family ownership endows their com-
pany with fundamental advantages. It facilitates a long-term perspective, where the
immediate financial results are overshadowed by the need to maintain an enduring
consumer desire for the brand. For Arnault, the financial performance of LVMH in
the near future holds little appeal compared to the brand’s continued allure in the
coming decade. Arnault advocates that being a family business helps them to create
stronger connections with employees (Arnault, 2018).
   A large number of studies have been made on the relation between family owner-
ship and firm performance. Anderson and Reeb (2003), in an accredited paper, study
the Standard and Poor’s (S&P) 500 firms from 1992 through 1999 and find support
for the positive effect of family ownership on profitability. In geographically simi-
lar research, Villalonga and Amit (2006) examine Fortune-500 companies in the US
during 1994-2000 and report that family ownership only creates value under specific
circumstances. Studies have also been made on the European market but primarily
on individual countries, including but not limited to France, Germany, and Switzer-
land (Sraear and Thesmar, 2007; Andres, 2008; Eugster and Isakov, 2019). Sraer and
Thesmar (2007) examine 1,000 companies listed on the French stock exchange over
the period 1994-2000. They find family firms significantly outperforms non-family
corporations in cross-sectional evidence. Andres (2008) studies a sample of 275 Ger-
man companies in the period of 1998-2004 and finds support for family ownership.
However, Andres (2008) notes that if family members are just large shareholders
without active involvement, the performance of their companies is not distinguish-
able from non-family firms. In a neighboring study, Eugster and Isakov (2019) study
195 companies listed on the Swiss stock market over the period 2003-2013. They find
that family firms have higher risk-adjusted stock returns than non-family controlled
firms.
   Contrastingly, Lins, Volpin and Wagner (2013) argue for family firms negative
effect on firm-performance in a study of 8,500 firms from 35 countries, such as Japan,
Brazil and the UK, during the 2008-2009 financial crisis. The authors find that family-
controlled firms cut investment, more relative to other firms, and underperform. In a
similarly larger study incorporating multiple countries, Barontini and Caprio (2006)
extrapolate on the mixed feelings in the European public opinion on family ownership
and examine corporations in 11 countries in continental Europe. In their sample of
675 firms over the period 1999-2001, they find a positive association between family
control and operating performance.
   Based on the earlier literature presented, we intend to expand on Anderson and

Reeb’s paper (2003) and aim to contribute two-fold to existing research. Firstly, we
extend on previous literature on the European market and contribute by studying the
STOXX Europe 600 index. Our method of proxying the European market using the
STOXX Europe 600 relates to Anderson and Reeb’s (2003) study of the S&P 500,
which represents 80% of the investable US market (S&P Global, 2024) and where our
index represents approximately 90% of the free-float market capitalization in Europe
(STOXX, 2023). Secondly, we contribute by studying the period between 2010-2022
in comparison to Anderson and Reeb (2003) that examined 1992-1999. This recent
period is less studied in family ownership literature and the results could thus help
evaluate previous findings from other periods of time. Based on this, the central
question our report aims to answer is as follows:

   Does family ownership lead to greater firm performance within the STOXX Europe
600 over the period 2010-2022?

   In our paper we study the relationship between family ownership and firm per-
formance in public companies. We investigate the stock market performance and
profitability of family firms and non-family firms listed in Europe from the start of
2010 to the end of 2022, using stock return and return on assets (ROA) as proxies
for firm performance. To examine the relationship we do a two-way fixed effects
OLS regression on family ownership’s effect on firm performance using year, country
and industry dummies as fixed effects. We further separate family firms into groups
between high and low family ownership, and younger and older firms to better under-
stand the relationship between family ownership and firm performance. Our sample
includes 577 firms and where 98 are family-owned. We classify a firm as family-owned
by studying the ownership structure to see if the level of ownership or control over
voting rights reaches our definition threshold.
   Our paper has multiple findings. (1) Family firms have significantly higher ROA
than non-family firms when controlling for relevant independent variables in the re-
gression, meanwhile the effect on stock return is inconclusive. The higher profitability,
indicated by ROA, can be due to family firms’ tendency to invest long-term, have
higher labor productivity, and lower cost of debt, amongst other potential explana-
tions. (2) Both younger and older family firms perform better than non-family firms.
Younger firms (50+ years since founding) outperform the older firms, which could
potentially be explained by founders contributing with unique valuable skills and be-
ing more prevalent in younger firms. (3) Firms with high family ownership (50%+
share) have a significant positive effect on firm performance. High ownership’s effect
on both performance measures were significant and higher than for low ownership
family firms. Our first and second findings are concurrent with results presented by
Anderson and Reeb (2003), but our third finding does not align with their results on

II. Theoretical Background

A. Potential Costs of Family Ownership
The costs of family ownership concentrate into families potentially using their con-
trol to benefit themselves and not maximize shareholder value to the disadvantage
of minority shareholders. Fama and Jensen (1985) present how large undiversified
shareholders (such as families) and diversified shareholders are incentivized differ-
ently to maximize value. The undiversified shareholders benefit from taking on less
risk compared to the diversified owners costing the minority shareholder. Eugster and
Isakov (2019) show how family ownership changes the agency problem. The conflict
between shareholders and managers is reduced by family ownership to the benefit of
all shareholders. This happens since the large shareholder is more incentivized and
capable to monitor the agent. However, another agency problem arises between the
large and minority shareholders. The large shareholder may try to extort private
benefits from the firm. This effect is present in family firms and thus holding shares
in them is awarded with a higher risk premium. Cronqvist and Nilsson (2003) find
support for this on the Swedish market where they see that controlling vote ownership
(25%+ control) negatively affects firm performance (in terms of Tobin’s Q and ROA).
However, they do not suggest it is a result of “stealing” instead they argue it is due
to suboptimal investment decisions and holding on to control for too long. Shleifer
and Vishny (1997) suggest that the greatest costs a large shareholder can create is
remaining as a manager longer than they should, pointing toward the importance of
a competitive recruiting process that family firms sometimes lack.

B. Potential Benefits of Family Ownership
Family ownership has several potential benefits such as having longer time perspec-
tive, having more control over management, and fostering better relationships with
stakeholders. As mentioned above, a large owner reduces the classic agency prob-
lem by being able and incentivized to monitor management. This effect is argued
by Andres (2008) to be even larger in family firms considering the families often are

                                    4
undiversified. Being large and undiversified is argued to be a problem by Fama and
Jensen (1985), but it also leads to a more incentivized active owner decreasing my-
opia and inertia often found in bureaucratic organizations. Families, thinking across
generations, are more likely to think long-term about profit maximization compared
to the quarter-to-quarter way of thinking created by impatient owners. Stein (1988)
presents how the long-term view will lead to not giving up long-term investments for
the benefit of short-term gains, consequently, creating future value. The longevity
also enables stability and continuity in management. Anderson and Reeb (2003) find
that the long-term relationships created by the stability allow suppliers and lenders
to offer better terms. Casson (1999) claims that families will in comparison to other
blockholders abstain from extorting private benefits as they are more concerned with
generational wealth and therefore are less likely to destroy value for the short-term
benefits. Sraer and Thesmar (2007) annotate that family firms have better labor
management and strategic capital usage. Family firms tend to pay lower wages, and,
especially those run by their founders and heirs, have higher labor productivity. Fur-
thermore, outside CEOs in family firms are more stringent in use of capital and pay
lower interest rates on debt. Anderson and Reeb (2003) also find that family firms
enjoy a lower cost of debt financing relative to non-family firms.

III. Method and Data

A. Research Focus
Our main research interest is the relation between family ownership and firm per-
formance. This question has been posed before but our research incorporates a new
dataset of firms, STOXX Europe 600, and a more recent sample period, 2010-2022.
Earlier studies on the subject have argued for both the negatives and positives of fam-
ily ownership under different circumstances. Poor corporate governance, lacking value
maximization focus, and private benefits extortion argue for the former. However, a
long-term perspective, more efficient labor management, and better capital use sup-
port the latter. The empirical evidence on the topic predominantly finds support for
the benefit of family ownership, but the question is still debated. Our central question
we want to examine is the impact of family ownership on firm performance by an-
swering: Does family ownership lead to greater firm performance within the STOXX
Europe 600 over the period 2010-2022? To better understand this relationship, we
further separate and study the impact between high and low family ownership, and
younger and older family firms.

                                    5
B. Family Firm Definition
To decide if a firm is family-owned or not there are two key questions: what it means
to be in control and what a family is. The criterion for deciding if a family is in
control of a company is borrowed from previous literature (Faccio and Lang, 2002;
Sraer and Thesmar, 2007; Eugster and Isakov, 2019). We define family-owned as
either (1) shareholding by the family of at least 20%, or (2) voting rights held by the
family of at least 20%. For families that have indirect control of a firm, we use the
same method as Faccio and Lang (2002). The method states that if Firm A is owned
to 40% by Firm B, and Firm B is owned 60% by a family, the family would indirectly
hold 24% of Firm A, thus Firm A would be classified as a family firm.
   A 20% ownership stake is generally considered sufficiently significant to allow a
family to exert substantial influence over strategic decisions and corporate governance
(Faccio and Lang, 2002). Moreover, setting a definitive percentage threshold facili-
tates more distinctive separation between family and non-family firms (Eugster and
Isakov, 2019). However, the extent of the influence can vary greatly depending on
the distribution of the remaining shares. In fragmented ownership structures, a 20%
stake could be effectively controlling and in more consolidated companies that stake
has a smaller influence. Furthermore, this threshold may risk omitting subtle forms
of control. It can neglect scenarios where family members exert influence through
considerable board representation and when they have management roles that can
induce profound impact, which others, such as Villalonga and Amit (2006) better
account for. In our study we are limited in our data collection resources and do not
consider these factors. There are examples of studies that use a higher threshold,
such as Andres (2008) and Lins et al. (2013) that use 25% or more of the voting
rights. Although this consequently decreases the pool size of family firms, it simulta-
neously increases likelihood that the family is involved in the firm’s operations and
the family ownership effect is enhanced, however, it risks omitting more family firms
where families exert subtle forms of control.
   When an owner or a group of owners that could be connected have an ownership
above the 20% threshold we have to determine if the owner/owners is a family or
not. There is a division in the literature between founding-family ownership and
family ownership. Anderson and Reeb (2003) and Eugster and Isakov (2019) among
others study founding-family ownership. Our research is family ownership, but not
limited to founding-families, which is the same as Faccio and Lang (2002) and Sraer
and Thesmar (2007). Faccio and Lang (2002) define any non-listed blockholder as
a family firm if they cannot prove otherwise. We do not assume family ownership
which should lead to less false positives, but leave some firms unclassified. Similarly
to Faccio and Lang (2002) and Claessens et al. (2000), we define individual owners
as families. Finally, in some instances a company is owned by a foundation. In those

                                    6
cases we regard the foundation as a family-entity if we determine that the family has
control over the foundation through, for instance, the family having board seats.

C. Sampling and Data Collection
The Sample
The population of our study encompasses all corporations listed on the STOXX Eu-
rope 600 index at the start of 2010 (2010-01-01), including both family-owned and
non-family-owned firms. It is a free-float market capitalization index of 600 con-
stituents representing large, mid and small capitalization companies. The index in-
cludes over 90% of the free-float market capitalization of the European stock market
and covers companies listed across 17 countries in Europe (see Appendix 1.). The
sample is representative of the entirety of companies listed on the index, with the
exception of a limited number of firms due to a lack of data on ownership structure.
Consequently, the sample closely approximates the full representation of the popula-
tion. The sample comprises 577 firms, 98 of which being family-owned. The period
of analysis, spanning from 2010 to 2022, is longer than that of some other studies on
the subject which should reduce the impact of time-varying omitted variables. The
577 sample firms and the 12 years studied generate 7,046 stock return and 6,700 ROA
observations.

Data Collection
In order to classify if firms are family-owned or not, we manually collect ownership
                                                                                                1
data on the 600 constituents using Refinitiv Eikon as the primary data source . In
instances where Refinitiv Eikon is unable to provide adequate ownership structure de-
tails, we consult the firms’ annual reports and official websites as alternative sources.
For some constituents data on ownership structure or actual owner is not available
causing us to omit 23 firms in our analysis out of the 600 companies in the STOXX
Europe 600. We assess if a firm is family-owned by examining ownership at the start
of 2010 and at the end of 2022, and then assuming that it have been family-owned
during the full period. This assumption is made due to the labor-intensive nature
of our data collection process. As suggested by earlier research on family ownership
(Anderson and Reeb 2003; Barontini and Caprio, 2006; Sraer and Thesmar, 2007; An-
dres, 2008), family firms are often characterized by long-term ownership and strategy.
Families seldom change ownership over a sample period (Andres, 2008) and on aver-
age families hold their ownership stakes for 75.9 years in the US (Anderson and Reeb,
2003). Investments are thus often held over generations, suggesting a lower frequency
of ownership turnover in family firms.
   For some companies delisted during the period (2010-2022), Refinitiv Eikon does
not furnish details on industry classification. In those occurrences, official company
websites are used to classify firms according to the Industry Classification Benchmark
(same taxonomy used by Refinitiv Eikon). In the cases where the founding year is
not provided by Refinitiv Eikon, other sources are used such as official company
websites. In a few cases where a firm undergoes changes in structure and/or merges,
the founding year is considered the main business’ founding year.
   We gather data on ROA using the information system Bloomberg. Yearly ROA is
collected from 2010-12-31 through 2022-12-31. In a few instances Bloomberg provides
a company’s ROA at a different date than December 31st because of a split fiscal year.
The companies with missing year-end data reporting ROA in the first half of the year
are allocated to the end of the year before, e.g. ROA reported on 2013-02-31 is placed
in the 2012 ROA column. For the companies with split fiscal year reporting ROA in
the second half of the year is allocated to the end of that year, e.g. a company that
reported ROA on 2013-10-30 is placed in the 2013 ROA column.

D. Variables and Measurement
Firm Performance
In our study, we use stock return and ROA (Net Income) as two proxies for firm
performance, in order to assess performance from an external investor’s perspective
and from intrinsic firm profitability. Similarly, Eugster and Isakov (2019), Lins et al.
(2013) and Villalonga and Amit (2006) also apply a comparable stock price assess-
ment measure approach. Some studies employ other measures such as ROA, ROE or
Tobin’s Q (Anderson and Reeb, 2003; Morck, Shleifer and Vishny, 1988; Barontini
and Caprio, 2006; Sraer and Thesmar, 2007). Stock return measures the firms’ total
return each year during the period between 2010 and 2022. The total return encom-
passes not only stock return but also all dividends and spinoffs. It is therefore the
true return of holding the stock during each period. See equation:

    Stock return is not used frequently in previous literature, however, a recent study
use a similar approach in the Swiss market (Eugster and Isakov, 2019). Eugster and
Isakov (2019) use a portfolio-based approach; grouping stocks based on family own-
ership in two portfolios which they reallocate each year. ROA is employed frequently
across previous literature. The use of ROA based on net income is relatively common,
as evidenced by Anderson and Reeb (2003). Noteworthy, with both measures is that
our sample size decreases each year. This is a consequence of the delistings, mergers
and bankruptcies that occur during the period.

Control Variables
Several control variables and dummies (year, country and industry) are used in the
analysis. Year dummies are used as time-specific effects (fixed between firms) and

                                    11
industry dummies are used as firm-specific effects (fixed over time). Year and industry
dummies are standard in previous literature, for instance, applied by Anderson and
Reeb (2003) in a similar matter. There are fewer studies conducted across borders, but
frequent in those is the use of country dummies for firm-specific effects, as exemplified
by Lins et al. (2013).
   The natural logarithm of market capitalization in millions of euros is used as a
control variable. The logarithm is employed to more accurately reflect the effects of
size differences and is in line with previous literature, such as Anderson and Reeb
(2003). Firm age is defined as the number of years since the founding of the firm
and also represented as the logarithm for the same reason market capitalization is.
Another control variable is long-term debt-to-assets ratio, which is used to capture
leverage. The aforementioned control variables are used to control for firm character-
istics, such as size and risk. All control variables are firm and year specific leading to
12 data points per firm (if the firm has remained publicly traded). These variables
(or close comparatives) are commonly used in previous literature.

F. Research Design
This study employs a quantitative research methodology to investigate the relation-
ship between family ownership and firm performance. The relationship is examined
over the period from 2010 to 2022. Firm performance is proxied in two ways: stock
return and ROA (as defined in section III.D.). The effect of family ownership, defined
as per our definition, is analyzed on the two performance measures and is the main
interest of the study. To better understand the dynamics of family ownership we later
in the study further separate the sample of family firms into groups based on level of
ownership by the family and age of the firm. To analyze the effect level of ownership
has on performance, we split the family firms into a high and low ownership group.
In the high ownership group, families hold over 50% of the capital or control of the
voting rights. In the low ownership group, families hold between 20-50% of the cap-
ital or control over the voting rights. To analyze the effect of a firm’s age, we split
the family firms into young and old firms. In the group of young family firms, the
companies were founded less than 50 years ago. In the group of old family firms, the
companies were founded 50 years ago or more. The split between older and younger
firms is the same method employed by Anderson and Reeb (2003).
   We study the relationship between family ownership and performance using a
two-way fixed effects model for our OLS regression analysis, which is similar to An-
derson and Reeb’s (2003) method. Our fixed effects are dummy variables for year,
country and industry. The control variables we use are firm- and year-specific. The
regressions’ significance level is adjusted for serial correlation and heteroskedasticity

G. Limitations
Our study is limited by quality of data, omitted variable bias and endogeneity. As
conferred in previous literature, (Faccio and Lang, 2002; Anderson and Reeb, 2003;
Andres, 2008, amongst others) gathering the data required to classify a firm as family-
controlled is challenging. In our sample, we fail to classify 23 companies out of the
600 constituents. The firms we cannot label fall into two types: (1) it is not possible
to find data on owners of a company or (2) it is not possible to find data on a specific
owner. Out of the firms we do not classify, a majority are delisted which causes some
concern as the sample becomes systematically biased against delisted firms. For the
second type of unclassified firms we establish that a firm has a controlling shareholder,
however, we cannot identify who that shareholder is and therefore, unable to label it

                                    15
as either family-owned or not. In these instances, there is likely an overrepresentation
of family firms not being identified. These systematic problems are a limitation to
the study, however they only account for approximately 5% of the sample.
    Another quality of data limitation to the study is missing data for certain control
variables. Most notably, we only have 5,199 data points of long-term debt-to-assets
compared to ROA where we have 6,700, making the variable less useful. From what
we have seen is that the missing long-term debt-to-assets data points are seemingly
random and thus should not introduce bias into the results. The ROA regressions are
subject to slight inaccuracies in time due to some of the firms with a split fiscal year.
As stated in section III.C., we miss calendar year-end ROA for some companies with
split fiscal year reporting. We place those ROA data points in the column at the end
of the year before or at the end of that year depending on if they reported in the first
or second half of the year. The simplification could cause some bias due to systematic
trends that could exist in firms with split fiscal years. For instance, it could be more
prevalent in certain industries or countries. However, this is only the case for a limited
number of companies and thus its effects should not be consequential.
   The results could also suffer from omitted variable bias. The relationship between
family ownership and the dependent variables may be driven by a missing variable.
Based on earlier literature, our study aims to include various different control variables
that are suitable proxies for different firm characteristics, such as the firm’s risk, size
and quality. One omitted variable that limits the study is other large blockholders.
This variable can be used to help distinguish the effects of family ownership from
the effects of a large blockholder. However, previous literature have found other
blockholders to be insignificant as a control variable when regressing for effects from
family ownership, such as Eugster and Isakov (2019).
   The study is potentially also limited by endogeneity problems most notably reverse
causality. Do family ownership cause firm performance or does firm performance lead
to family ownership? Families have better access to management and may have better
insights into the firm overall, which could mean they have an information advantage
over smaller investors and could thus avoid poorly performing stocks. Some studies
use Instrumental Variables (IV) models to debias the results from potential endo-
geneity. We use OLS models which are in line with Anderson and Reeb (2003). They
use OLS models to derive their main results and IV-model as a robustness test where
they found no significant differences compared to the OLS results. The arguments
against reverse causality are also strong. Firstly, the difficulty in predicting future
firm performance. Even though family firms may have an information advantage over
smaller investors this is likely not the case over larger institutions. Secondly, Ander-
son and Reeb (2003) find families’ average holding period to be 75.9 years. They
argue that families therefore would have to have incredible foresight to be able to
predict, on average, 75.9 years into the future. On a similar note, Eugster and Isakov

                                    16
(2019) argue the long holding periods indicate families’ goal is to preserve their firm
compared to value maximization which is another argument against reverse causal-
ity. Therefore, as accredited previous literature use OLS and the strong arguments
against reverse causality we consider the risk of biased results in OLS regressions due
to reverse causality to be low.

Studying different types of firm performance measures and types of family ownership

into two parts. This is done to see that the period we chose is not a significant factor
driving results. Thus, we divided the data into a beginning period (2010-2016) and
an end period (2017-2022) and tested both the periods on Stock Returns and ROA.

V. Discussion

The results of our study indicate that, when only controlling for time-specific effects,
family firms exhibit higher returns on the stock market and are more profitable com-
pared to non-family firms. From the perspective of an equity investor, the results
indicate that investing in family-owned firms should yield on average 2.1% better
returns each year, as demonstrated in Table VI. The higher returns of family firms
as a group is illustrated in Figure 1. However, assuming an efficient market, a con-
sequence of higher returns is increased risk. Eugster and Isakov (2019) suggest that
holding shares in family firms should be awarded with a higher risk premium due
to the increased agency costs. Still, we observe higher ROA in family-owned firms,
which should not be a consequence of added agency risks.
   When controlling for the country of headquarters in Table VII, the significant
positive effect remains with a slight decrease of families’ effect on stock return and an
increase in the effect on ROA. This suggests country-specific economic and regula-
tory environments are not an explanation that bear substantial significance for family
firms’ outperformance. When controlling for industry, the significance of family own-
ership on stock returns disappears. Indicating that a notable amount of the stock
outperformance is attributed to families owning firms in industries that have yielded
higher returns.
    The results in Table VIII indicate that when controlling for the relevant variables,
family ownership exhibits a positive significant association with ROA. This suggests
that profitability, as proxied by ROA, is 1.21% higher than in non-family firms. The
effect proves robust by our conducted tests, though some variance over time is found.
Our results concur in part with Anderson and Reeb (2003) who also find support for
higher profitability in family firms. They note, the advantages of family ownership
can include increased governance abilities, higher economic incentive due to a concen-
trated position, and a better long-term time horizon. Sraer and Themsar (2007) also

                                    23
find that family firms have higher ROA. They present potential management styles
attributed to family firms that can explain the outperformance. The authors posit
that founders are more productive, heir-managers pay lower wages due to long-term
employment contracts, and outside professional CEOs operate at lower ratios of cap-
ital to labor.
    Table VIII also presents old family ownership’s significant effect of 0.83% on ROA,
which is lower than the effect from younger family firms. The findings are similar
to Anderson and Reeb (2003) who find that younger family firms have a higher
ROA. Anderson and Reeb (2003) argue a reason could be that founders are generally
more present in younger firms and they add unique value-adding skills, but with
firm age the descendants take over and contribute less to the firm’s performance.
Indirectly Villalonga and Amit (2006), Andres (2008), and Barontini and Caprio
(2006) also examine the effect of firm age on the relation between family firms and
firm performance by studying the difference in performance between founder CEOs
and descendant CEOs. Postulating that descendant-managed firms are generally
older than founder-managed firms, these authors find that younger, founder-led, firms
perform better than those led by descendants, similar to our results in support of
younger firms.
    In contrast to family ownership’s positive effect on ROA, we do not find that family
ownership’s effect on stock returns is significantly different from 0 after controlling for
independent variables. The estimated effect is 0.64% which is not high enough to draw
any strong conclusions. There is a limited amount of earlier research conducted on
the relation between stock performance and family performance. Eugster and Isakov
(2019) argue the reason is the efficient market hypothesis and that all differences in
ownership structure should already be priced in. This is a compelling reason due to
the lack of significant returns we find, however, Eugster and Isakov (2019) themselves
find a significant effect on stock returns in the Swiss market from family ownership.
Considering the use of different control variables and markets studied there may be
unobserved differences that drive the difference in the results. In our study, we find
support for a positive effect of high family ownership on stock returns (compared to no
effect on regular family ownership). This is in line with earlier reasoning by Eugster
and Isakov (2019), as higher family ownership should increase the agency risks and in
turn increase the risk premium. This could be an explanation to our results, however,
it only explains the higher stock returns and not why we saw a similar effect on ROA.
    As illustrated in Table VIII, high family ownership is associated with a significant
(p<0.01) positive effect of 1.49% on ROA, in comparison to the total sample of family
firms, where the positive effect is 1.21%. High family ownership firms exhibiting
greater performance than low family ownership firms is consistent with Cronqvist
and Nilsson (2003) findings. They find controlling minority shareholders (25-50%)
is associated with the highest agency costs and lowest performance. Anderson and

                                    24
Reeb’s (2003) challenge these results arguing that family ownership’s positive effect
on ROA starts to decrease at 30.8%. Anderson and Reeb (2003) also find that after
60% ownership the returns of family firms perform worse than non-family firms.
   The discrepancies between our findings and those of Anderson and Reeb (2003) on
high and low family ownership could be explained by the differences in research focus
and methodology. They study founding-family ownership whilst we include additional
types of family firms. Their paper also applies control variables that we do not employ.
Variables include, but are not limited to, management ownership, CEO pay and
independent board members, which we do not account for due to data constraints.
Moreover, since they separate between founding-controlled, descendant-controlled,
and professional CEO-controlled, greater consideration is taken to the involvement
of the family in the firm operations. In our study, we apply a different commonly
used definition of a family firm, however, it does not directly take into consideration
the involvement of the family in the business. It is possible that in our sample, low
ownership family firms are less active than high ownership family firms where the
families can exercise more control. Andres (2008) argues that family involvement is
central in order for family firms to outperform non-family firms. Consequently the
poorer performance from low family ownership may instead be caused by passive
ownership compared to active involvement by the family. Despite the differences in
methodology, our divergent results on the effect of high and low family ownership are
still of interest. It is also possible these discrepancies could be attributed to differences
between family firms in the US and European markets, or alternatively, to the fact
that our more recent sample period may have coincided with a period of change in
the characteristics of family firms.

                                    25
VI. Conclusion

Our paper studies the relationship between family ownership in listed companies and
firm performance within the STOXX Europe 600 index. Studying the European
market, we find evidence that family ownership leads to better firm performance in
certain aspects, but inconclusive in others. As a collective, family firms have higher
stock returns than non-family firms over our sample period. We cannot however
deduce that the effect is due to a significant positive association between family
ownership and stock returns. We find substantial evidence that family ownership has
a positive significant effect on ROA. Finding this result in another market and over
a different time period than that of Anderson and Reeb (2003) is compelling support
for family ownership’s positive effect on profitability. The effect of ROA is more
prominent in young family firms than old and the strongest for firms with high family
ownership. The results indicate that family ownership is beneficial for profitability
and does not have negative implications for firm-value. We strengthen earlier findings
that family ownership is as good, and in aspects better of an ownership-structure
than non-family ownership. Our results on the effect of different levels of family
ownership challenge previous findings on the US market and contribute to potentially
highlighting either disparities between US and European family firms or differences
in family firms over time.
   For future researchers, we recommend further study on the effect of high family
ownership on firm performance. The existing literature is divided on this topic, but
our findings indicate a positive effect of family ownership on firm profitability. As in
the study by Anderson and Reeb (2003) it could be interesting to map out differences
in management and family-involvement in our sample of the European market. Com-
paring the dynamics of family-involvement between low and high ownership firms
could deepen the understanding of the impact of high family ownership on firm per-
formance.

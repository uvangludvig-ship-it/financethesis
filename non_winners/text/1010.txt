                          ABSTRACT

This thesis carries out an asset-pricing analysis of the Swedish single family house market. Using both
cross-sectional and panel data on municipality level we investigate the role of volatility and price
level in explaining housing returns. In the cross-sectional regressions, we derive returns from an
adjusted price measure, the K/T-ratio, and find weak evidence of a positive risk-return relationship. In
the panel regressions, we obtain returns from an investor model and by using municipality fixed
effects we unveil a negative risk-return relationship. Contrary to these ambiguous results, we find
some evidence that propose a positive price level effect on return.

                      1 INTRODUCTION

Real estate bears the twofold role of consumption and investment. For many Swedish families, the
house they live in makes up a substantial part of their private wealth and is thereby their largest single
investment. When considering when and what house to purchase, buyers are often concerned by the
investment potential of the property, e. g. the rate of return. While financial economists have had a

                                                                                 1
long interest in the role of risk and return of financial assets , the primary focus in the literature
revolve around equity and stock portfolios and less emphasis has been put on housing as an asset for
investment. Housing is, however, comparable to equities in the sense that it can offer two forms of
payment to the investor. Whereas the compensation for equities comes in the shape of dividends and
price returns, this is analogous to rents and house price returns in the real estate market. On the other
hand, because partial ownership is rare, it is not possible to eliminate the unsystematic risk associated
with the structure and location of the house, implying that housing is practically not diversifiable.
Thereby offer price for a house with riskier returns, compared to an otherwise similar house, should
be lower but also have a higher expected return.

As of 2008, 57.2% of the Swedish residents lived in a single-family housing, which is considered to

                                                2
be an either detached, row or linked houses. House prices are a common subject of debate in the
media and general public, where the risk the housing investment carries is discussed as the possible
burst of a “housing bubble”. Consequently, it is the recent housing bubbles around the world and the
price development in Swedish housing market that has inspired us to focus on the role of housing as
an asset for investment. Nevertheless, Case et al (2005) find that changes in housing prices have a
larger and more important impact in influencing household consumption than that of the changes in
stock market prices, thereby making the question of risk and return of housing an important issue also
from a macroeconomic perspective.

 When investing in an asset in financial markets, it is commonly recognized that there should be a
positive risk and return relationship. We ask ourselves:

Is there a positive relationship between risk, price level and return in the Swedish single-family
housing market?

                             1.1 PURPOSE

The purpose of this study is to apply an asset-pricing analysis and empirically examine a possible risk
and return trade-off in the Swedish single-family housing market. We also include market price to
examine price level effects. First, we decompose house price returns into risk and price level to
investigate the two variables impact on the return using a cross-sectional model. Second, we include a
set of assumptions that affect the house investment and apply fixed effects in a panel data model to
get a better investment picture. With our study we hope to shed light on the potential of investing in
Swedish property.

Readers should pay attention to that we use two separate ways to estimate housing returns in the
cross-sectional and panel data models.

                          1.2 CONTRIBUTION

This paper builds upon studies of the risk-return relationship of financial assets and extends the
analysis to the Swedish housing. To our knowledge, this is the first attempt to apply a cross-section on
risk, return and price level for the aggregate Swedish housing market. Furthermore, we extend an
investor model constructed by Wessel and Jennefelt (2007) by analyzing it in a panel.

                  2 PREVIOUS LITERATURE

The majority of research in this field of study examines the U.S. housing market. Crone and Voith
(1999) observe appraiser files data on repeat sales from 1971–1996 and investigate the joint
consumption-investment decision to see whether this link negates the risk-return relationship. First
they find that increased risk leads to a higher return but also that the risk-return tradeoff is negated by
the consumption-investment link regarding the magnitude of the house investment.

                                        2
Cannon et al (2006) carries out an asset-pricing analysis using cross-sectional regression on zip code
level data in the U. S. metropolitan single family housing market. They study whether housing returns
can be explained by volatility, price level, stock market return and idiosyncratic volatility. They find a
positive relationship between housing returns and volatility where a 10% increase in volatility leads to
a 2.48% increase in annual housing returns and that there is a positive but diminishing price effect.
These results are robust to metropolitan statistical area clustering effects and differences in
socioeconomic variables. In addition, it is showed that stock market risk is priced directionally in the
housing market.

The Swedish market for single family housing is studied by Wessel and Jennefelt (2007), who find a
positive relationship between risk and return. They construct a cross-sectional investor model that
adjusts for differences in sizes and standards for houses based on assessed values and account for
opportunity cost of renting, maintenance cost, depreciation, property tax and transaction costs. For
five and ten year investments, they find that a 10% increase in volatility leads to a 7.42% and 3.30%
increase in return respectively. Furthermore, they find that the risk of investing in metropolitan areas
is higher than in non-metropolitan areas but also that the metropolitan areas render higher returns.
They conclude by showing that risk adjusted return in metropolitan areas is higher than in non-
metropolitan.

On the other hand, Dolde and Tirtiroglu (1997) look at time-varying housing returns and volatilities in
the United States. They find a negative relationship between conditional variance and returns in
Connecticut but a positive relationship in San Francisco. But their goal is to test the information
diffusion in these two housing markets.

Lu Han (2009) show through a 4-quarter moving window of average real housing returns and
volatility that house price indices in the San Jose and Santa Clara area in the U.S. reflect a strong
positive relationship between return on housing and its related volatility. In contrast, the opposite
relationship is found in Pittsburgh.

When examining the efficiency of the U.S. market for single family housing, Case and Shiller (1989),
find that year-to-year house price changes tend to be followed by changes in the same direction the
subsequent year. In addition, predictable changes in the real interest rate do not seem to be
incorporated in the prices. This leads to the conclusion that the market is not efficient and that there
exists a profitable trading strategy for individuals that are free to time their investment in housing.
However, price changes on individual homes do not seem to be very forecastable.

Using U.S. and international panel data, Case et al (2001) find strong evidence that variations in
housing market wealth has an impact upon consumption in the developed countries. According to
their model, an immediate increase by 10% in housing wealth increases consumption by 0.4% for

                                        3
U.S. states. In contrast, they also find that financial wealth has no effect. Their results propose that
changes in housing wealth should be considered to have a large and more important effect on
household consumption in the developed countries compared to changes in the stock market.

Using a restricted error-correction model of real house price changes on Swedish panel data, Hort
(1998) finds that movements in income, user costs and construction costs have significant impact on
real house prices. Furthermore, the estimates of the dynamic equation demonstrate a rich
autoregressive structure in real house prices. An adjustment to the long-run relationship is also quite
swift. Finally, fluctuations in real house prices are well explained by development in fundamental
demand conditions, but they are also consistent with speculative behavior.

On a general level, most researchers seem to agree on the real estate’s ability to offer a hedge against
inflation and that real assets present protection against the negative effects of unexpected inflation. In
addition, real estate returns have a low correlation with returns of financial assets. These are the
primary reasons why real estate is included in many portfolios. Regarding the risk-return relationship,
however, researchers have found diverse results, making it an interesting subject of study in the
Swedish housing market.

The volatility measures the variation in returns from the expected return value. With a higher
volatility, the spread of returns will have a larger range. Accordingly, a low volatility would imply
that the returns do not fluctuate considerably around the mean.

Nevertheless, the standard deviation might not be the best measure for risk because it makes an asset
with positive but significantly fluctuating returns look risky. Likewise, an asset with constant negative
returns would have a standard deviation of zero. The implication is that all changes, despite direction,
are treated as risk. The investor is usually only concerned about the downside of risk, when returns are
below the average or negative, and not by the upside or gain. Thereby other risk measures, such as

                                                                                               3
Value at Risk (VaR), might also be applicable for measuring risk in the housing market. However,
we have chosen the volatility as risk measure, which is most common, for our models.

                  3.4 USING FIXED EFFECT ESTIMATION

It is reasonable to believe that each municipality has its own unobserved, individual and time-
invariant features that could influence our independent variables, price level and volatility. One
example of such a difference could be the geographical location, which affect house prices for each
municipality but do not change over time. It is hard to get a correct measure of these omitted and
unobserved variables, but if they are not taken into account they could impact and bias our regression
results.

                          4 DATA

         4.1 STATISTICS SWEDEN’S HOUSE PRICE MEASURES

Our main source of data is Statistics Sweden, an administrative organization who supplies all sorts of
users with statistics for decision making, debate and research. Whereas the organization provide
several different measures of the price development for single family housing, not all of them are
useful.

                       4.1.1 REAL ESTATE PRICE INDEX

The Real Estate Price Index is calculated by separating real estate’s into several classes based on
assessed value and applies a system of weighting to produce an index. However, the weakness of the
index is that it needs a certain amount of transactions to be reliable. Thereby it is not possible for
Statistics Sweden to calculate the index on municipality level or by month, thereby not applicable in
our study.

                        4.1.2 AVERAGE HOUSE PRICES

Simply the average house transaction prices at municipality level by year. The weakness with this
measure is that the properties that are included in the market for single family housing are very
different in their characteristics. They differ with regard to location, standard and size and it could be
the case that, for one year, only large houses in attractive neighborhoods are sold. Comparing prices
year-on-year in such a scenario would be misleading since the properties are not comparable and
would not reflect the actual price development in a particularly good way.

                              4.1.3 THE K/T RATIO

Using the K/T ratio is the simplest and most correct way, according to Statistics Sweden, to compare

                              6
prices across municipalities.  It is the quotient between the transaction prices (K) and the assessed
values (T) of properties. It reflects how much more than the assessed value the buyer paid for the
house. Since every property has an assessed value, this can be seen as a standardized measure “excess
price”. By comparing K/T ratios over time, users can obtain an estimate of the price development in a
municipality. Furthermore, this measure does not need the same amount of transactions to be reliable.
Nevertheless, when comparing K/T ratios over time the user has to be aware of that they are based
upon the general assessment of assessed value which occurs, at earliest, one year before the presented
K/T ratio. For example, coefficients calculated between 1990 and 1995 are based on assessed values
from 1990 and so on. The assessed values were calculated by the Swedish authorities in 1981, 1990,

6

1996, 2003, 2006. Thereby, the user has to account for changes in general assessment of assessed
values over the period where comparison is made. To get an understanding of what a property value is
at present, the user can multiply the K/T ratio by the assessed value of a property to get an estimate of
the market value.

Given Statistics Sweden’s confidence regarding the K/T ratio as well as the use of it by Wessel and
Jennefelt (2007), we believe that it is the most reliable measure for price development at a
municipality level. Therefore, we use the K/T ratio, instead of average purchase prices, for our
return and volatility calculations.

                          5.2.1 QUINTILE PORTFOLIOS

We separate the municipalities into five portfolios ranked by the natural logarithm of the average

                          5.2.2 CLUSTERING EFFECTS

By defining zip codes into neighborhoods, Goetzmann et al (1998) find that property prices fluctuate
together, even though they are separated in space, as long as they are perceived by the market as
substitutes for each other. We thereby want to see if our return-volatility-price level relationship is
robust to clustering effects. We use Local Labor Markets (LLMs), computed by Statistics Sweden

                                                                                      9
based on commuting patterns for workers, as a proxy for municipality clustering. All municipalities
belong to one of the total of 75 LLMs, which give 74 dummy variables in the cross-sectional
regression. The reasoning behind this is that houses in these LLMs are perceived by the market as
substitutes since people within them are still able to commute to their workplace.

                           5.3 PANEL DATA

When calculating return and volatility for the panel data, an investor model is constructed to take into
account factors that affect homeowners when investing. The following section describes how return
and volatility is generated using the investor model.

                           5.3.1 HOLDING PERIODS

When generating the underlying data set, we use both 10-year and 5-year investment horizons in two
different panel data sets. Annualized return and volatility are calculated for the horizons with different
initial periods of investment. Starting at 1981 we get, for each municipality, 18 investment periods
built on 10 years each, and 23 investment periods built on 5 years each.

By using this model we hope to capture and proxy the unobserved municipality specific variables that,
if excluded, would create omitted variable bias. We therefore get a pure estimate of the impact of
volatility and price level on return. This fixed effects model let each municipality have its own
intercept that is constant over time and it allows the unobserved effects to be correlated with our
independent variables since they are now included in the form of municipality dummies.

Factors captured in the municipality dummies could be geographical features, attitudes, abilities and
interest in buying single family houses. It is reasonable to believe that these factors change to a large
extent between different municipalities. Also, we assume that the changes of these factors are
negligible or nonexistent looking over our time horizon setup.

                       7 CONCLUSION

We apply an asset-pricing analysis using both cross-sectional and panel data of the Swedish single-
family housing market over the period 1981-2008. Using aggregate data in our cross-sectional model,
we find weak evidence of a positive risk and return relationship, where a 10 % increase in risk leads
to a 1.80 % increase in return. However, the price level effect is substantial showing a 2.10 % higher
return for an investment made in a house with a market price of 2,000,000 SEK compared to a house
with a market price of 1,000,000 SEK. By ranking municipalities by market price we find more
distinct evidence for a positive return-volatility-price level relationship, where the explanatory power
increases with the price level. However, the price effect seems to be diminishing. The model is robust
to local labor market clustering effects.

Using the panel models, controlling for fixed effects, we found varying price level results depending
on the time horizon. But when looking at the risk and return relationship we found that investors
obtain significantly lower returns for an increase in risk in both horizons.

For a 10 year investment, an increase in volatility of 10 % resulted in a 1.88 % decrease in average
annualized return. For the 5 year investment the same regression results showed instead a 1.33%
decrease.

The impact of price level on return was insignificant for the 5 year time horizon but showed a modest
positive price level effect on return at the 10 year investment horizon.

                                        22
From these results we find evidence of a negative risk-return relationship in the aggregate Swedish
market. However, the diverse results in price level effects indicate a small price level effect only in
the short 5 year investment horizon.

                        8 DISCUSSION

The very nature of real estate is a possible reason for our weak results for the volatility effect in the
cross-section. Housing is heterogeneous, and we can expect very large differences between
metropolitan and non-metropolitan areas of Sweden. Another possible explanation is that there might
be a varying risk-return relationship within Swedish submarkets. Furthermore, treating housing as a
financial asset analogous to equities is problematic because buyers generally do not have extensive
economic knowledge and are not concerned about price risk in the same way as for equities, as long
as they do not lose significant amounts when divesting in the future. Such a scenario has not occurred
in Sweden due to continuous appreciation of house prices over the last 30 years, with exception for
1991-1992.

In line with this, we believe that location, size and affordability are more important factors.
Considering affordability, it might be the case that people are more worried about the interest rate risk
rather than housing price risk when deciding on buying a house, because this corresponds to whether
they can afford their future living. Accordingly, in areas where incomes are higher, people have a
higher margin when it comes to affordability which might drive prices and also affect the risk and
return relationship.

Local submarkets attractiveness regarding, for example status, might also drive the prices. In addition,
inflation has a significant effect when prices appreciate. Things that are discussed above might work
to erode a risk-return relationship, at least on an aggregate level. Based on our results in the cross-
section, we suspect that metropolitan areas experience more homogeneity, and that a separating
analysis would give a better understanding of how risk and return works in the Swedish single family
housing market

Using the panel regressions, we have controlled for many of the problems discussed above. But we
still believe that further studies are required to confirm the results.

                    9 FURTHER STUDIES

     In our models, we use volatility as a risk measure. This might not be the best measure of risk
        because it recognizes positive return as risk. By using other measures of risk, such as Value at
        Risk, one might get a better estimate of the relationship between risk and return.
     In our investor model, we have not taken into account the fact that most families borrow when
        buying a house. Developing an extended model which takes into account debt and interest
        rate payments would make an interesting contribution.
     House prices are affected by inflation. Also, when having large debt on a property the
        inflation becomes even more important in the investment. Building a model that considers
        these factors would also make an important contribution.
     A major problem with the data we use is that it is not quality-adjusted or includes median
        prices. Furthermore, our figures are aggregated on municipality level and single transactions
        are not possible to follow. Developing a data set that overcomes these problems would give
        estimates of the price development in Sweden that is superior. Being able to sample house
       prices in submarkets within municipalities would also be a better way to reduce
        heterogeneity.
     In our analysis we do not include co-operative apartments, which represent the majority of
        housing in urban areas of Sweden. Analyzing the risk-return relationship in this market would
         be interesting.

                       10 LIMITATIONS

Ideally, we would use median house price data because this would reflect house price development
better and overcome some of the problems regarding size and standard of houses that are sold. Our
data is not adjusted for quality, location and other factors that might have large effects on price, and
we expect to have multiple observations that contain large structural variations. In other words we
have extensive heterogeneity in our data. This is partly countered by using an adjusted K/T- ratio in
the return calculations, but the measure is far from perfect. Moreover, we do not have data on single
transactions or submarkets within municipalities, reducing the precision of our analysis.

In addition, some house sales are made privately or in connection with some other transaction (e.g. a
tenancy agreement) which would benefit the seller. This would adjust the selling price downwards
and increase the K/T ratio. However, we have assumed that such transactions are quite rare and have
thereby not taken the issue into account.

                                        24
Furthermore, by examining one- and two dwelling buildings, we expect oversampling in proportion to
population in rural municipalities and undersampling in urban ones, e.g. the cities Stockholm,
Gothenburg and Malmo.

Another concern is that, even though we have a long time series, intra-year house prices are not
available by municipality, making it impossible to seize the intra-year return and volatility which
would be better.

                       12.1 ASSUMPTIONS MADE

Below we explain in detail the different assumptions made when calculating the returns and
volatilities used in our two panel data sets.

                                12.1.1 RENT

The average cost of renting an apartment in Sweden is assumed to be an opportunity gain for the
property investor. We assume that the investor has only two choices of living, either he lives in an
apartment or in his house investment. This implies that the investor makes a trade-off when investing
in the house, in one hand a large initial payment must be made, but on the other hand the investor
saves the rent cost.

The opportunity gain was calculated using average rent cost per year for Swedish apartments based on
the time period 1992 to 2007. Each year’s average cost was divided by the average property value for
the same year. The median value for the time period was finally used as a proxy for the opportunity
gain of making a house investment.

                              12.1.2 PROPERTY FINANCING

      We assume that the investor finance the property without taking any loan. This is a somewhat
      unrealistic assumption since most homeowners in Sweden have financed their properties with
       extensive lending. However, from an asset pricing perspective it can be argued that it is a more
       interesting way of looking at housing as an asset for investment which gives payment in form of rent
       and house price returns.

                               12.1.3 TIME OF INVESTMENT

       We assume that each investor invests in the beginning of a year and sell his investment in the end of a
       year. This coins all calculations.

                                 12.1.4 PROPERTY TAX

       At the beginning of our sample period, until 1990, Sweden had a tax rate of 2 % of the assessed value
       of a property. Although, the 50 % marginal tax rate during the same period made the effective rate on
       land 1 %. After 1990 the tax rate was changed to a fixed 1 % of the assessed value. Therefore we
       assume a fixed property tax during our sample period of 1 % of the assessed value.

       Property aged less than five years are excluded from property tax. In addition, property aged between
       5 and 10 years pay only 50 % of the property tax. We did not take this into account since the Swedish

                                                                                                                           17
      houses older than 10 years make up approximately 97 % of the single family house stock.

                            12.1.5 DEPRECIATION

We assume that a single family house has a life span of 100 years, which makes it depreciate at a rate
of 1 % per year. Since you get both land and structure when making the property investment, but there
is no depreciation on land, the property depreciation will be 0.5 % per year, assuming that land and
structure have an equal value distribution.

                         12.1.6 MAINTENANCE COSTS

Maintenance and repair costs are assumed to affect the returns for an investor negatively. Based on
the years 1992 to 2007, we calculated annual costs using the average useful floor space per dwelling,
multiplied with the average repair costs per square meter. We further divided the annual costs by the
current year’s average property value and used the median value as our proxy. A weak, but assumed
negligible link in this measure is the data on average useful floor space per dwelling, which was only
available for the year of 2003.

                         12.1.7 TRANSACTION COSTS

We assume that the investor pays a stamp duty of 1.5 % when investing and a 3 % real estate agent
fee when selling the property. The Swedish government had the duty stamp removed by the end of
2007 but since this restructuring only affect one year in our data, and by a small to nonexistent
amount, we still use 1.5 % as a proxy fee for the whole period 1981 to 2008.

                          12.1.8 CAPITAL GAINS TAX

The reason for not taking capital gains tax into account is because of the large differences in
conditions between a young and old property investor. Under Swedish law, and individual is excluded
from capital gains tax if the gains are fully reinvested into another property. However, if an individual
reinvests into a smaller, less expensive property, the difference in value is treated as capital gains and
thus chargeable. This makes younger investors better off than elder since they are more likely to
demand a house upgrading than a downgrading as they start a family. In order to get a correct asset
pricing picture regardless of investor type we therefore exclude capital gains tax from our model.

     12.1.9 BACKGROUND CHECK-, INSPECTION- AND INSURANCE COSTS

We do not take background check costs or inspection costs when selling and buying into account in
our calculations since these costs are not considered general for all investors. We assume that there
are no insurance costs neither because the property insurance depend on each corporations specific
classification of different variables, such as moisture class, which are hard to estimate.

                              12.1.10 INFLATION

Inflation is not taken into account directly in the price calculations and we use nominal values of the
underlying prices.

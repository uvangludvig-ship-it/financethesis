Abstract:
   This paper explores the potential of transfer learning with convolutional neural
   networks to improve stock return predictions. The transfer learning models are
   compared to models trained on local market data as well as the direct application of
   models trained on U.S. data. Analyzing daily data of individual stocks, across 22
   countries from 1993 to 2024, the study evaluates two transfer learning models: one
   that fine-tunes decision-making layers while retaining pre-trained patterns and another
   that fully adapts all layers to local data. The findings reveal that leveraging a model
   pre-trained on U.S. data generally enhances performance, particularly when the local
   dataset is below a certain size. However, the impact of retraining on local data is
   mixed, with results varying significantly across different markets.

1 Introduction
The prediction of future stock returns is a complex and long-standing challenge that has
captivated both academics and practitioners. Machine learning techniques have shown
promising potential in addressing this task. The impressive recent advancements in
machine learning across other domains are likely to further inspire and accelerate its
application to return prediction. This paper examines whether a transfer learning
approach, using convolutional neural networks (CNN), delivers superior Sharpe ratios
compared to a locally trained model as well as the direct application of a pre-trained
model. The analysis is conducted at the individual stock level across 22 countries,
covering the period from January 1, 1993, to September 30, 2024. The models take stock
data from the most recent five trading days as input and the output is the predicted
probability of a positive return over the following five trading days. The predictions are
then used to construct portfolios. In this study, the data is encoded as images before being
fed to the models, departing from the common practice of using numerical values as input.
This method is particularly intriguing as it allows the application of machine learning
algorithms typically used in other domains, such as computer vision tasks like driver-
assist systems in cars. Additionally, structuring the data this way may help uncover
patterns that might otherwise remain hidden. Conducting the study involved generating
stock chart images, training models on them, using these models to produce trading
signals, and constructing portfolios based on the signals. The study leverages an extensive
dataset, resulting in the generation of millions of stock chart images representing stock
charts for tens of thousands of individual companies across the examined markets. The
portfolios that are constructed are equally weighted zero-cost long-short portfolios. The
performance of these portfolios is evaluated primarily by comparing the Sharpe ratio
differences between the strategies. These differences are tested for statistical significance
using a paired studentized circular block bootstrap.
     The methodology relies on the assumption that stock prices are influenced by
human behavior, which generates complex and subtle patterns in the data. These patterns
are believed to hold some predictive power that can be modeled to a certain extent.
Machine learning is employed to capture these non-linear features, which simpler models
often fail to identify. Empirical evidence has demonstrated the value of machine learning
in stock return prediction, as shown by Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020), as well as Brogaard
and Zareei (2021). When selecting a suitable algorithm for such tasks, several factors
must be considered. The model must be flexible enough to identify intricate patterns that
simpler models overlook, yet sufficiently constrained to avoid overfitting to noise in the
data. This study focuses specifically on exploring the potential of transfer learning, rather
than identifying the optimal algorithm. Consequently, optimizing the architecture and
hyperparameters of the underlying model lies beyond the scope of this analysis.
      The use of CNNs for stock chart analysis represents a significant departure from
traditional technical analysis methods. While traditional approaches rely on predefined
patterns and indicators, often subjectively interpreted and validated anecdotally, CNNs
employ a data-driven, learning-based framework. By autonomously identifying relevant
features from large datasets, CNNs reduce subjectivity and improve predictive accuracy
by uncovering complex, nonlinear patterns beyond human cognitive capabilities.
      Machine learning aims to autonomously identify patterns and extract meaning from
data without explicit instructions from humans. However, the selection of features
provided to the model inherently reflects human assumptions about the importance of

                                   2
different inputs. CNNs address this limitation by integrating the feature selection process
into the learning itself, allowing the model to identify relevant features more
independently. To understand the research question and methodology of this study, a
basic understanding of CNNs is necessary. CNNs are a type of neural network that
includes specialized operations prior to the regular fully connected layer. These layers
automate the feature extraction process and reduce the dimensionality of the data before
passing it through the network. Unlike static filters, these layers are part of the training
process, meaning the filters are updated during training to learn and capture the patterns
with the greatest predictive power. In this application, these patterns are those most
strongly correlated with future stock returns. CNNs stack multiple layers on top of one
another, enabling the network to detect patterns at varying levels of complexity. This
depth allows the model to capture intricate patterns and is what defines “deep learning.”
One key advantage of this automatic feature extraction is its ability to uncover patterns
that are too complex or subtle for humans to identify. However, a significant downside is
the difficulty in interpreting and understanding the patterns learned by the model.
      Extensive training data to learn from is essential for effective machine learning and
performance is likely to continue to improve as more data is added to the training.
However, data availability varies widely across markets, raising the question of how to
enhance model performance in data-poor markets. Transfer learning is a subfield of
machine learning that aims to leverage knowledge from one task to improve performance
on another. This is achieved by using an existing model as the foundation for a new
model, rather than training it from scratch. By utilizing pre-trained models, this approach
enhances performance on related tasks, largely due to the base model being trained on a
larger dataset. The importance of training data size for machine learning accuracy has
been highlighted by studies such as Sun, Shrivastava, Singh, and Gupta (2017). Assuming
that international financial markets share underlying structural similarities and behavioral
patterns – shaped by economic principles, investor psychology, and macroeconomic
factors – transfer learning may constitute a way to address the issue of lacking data in
certain markets. This study evaluates two distinct approaches to transfer learning: one
that retains pre-trained patterns and fine-tunes only the decision-making layers, and
another that fully adapts all layers to better align with the characteristics of the local data.
      This study builds on the work of Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023), replicating their
approach of using CNNs for individual stock return prediction, while extending its
application to transfer learning with retraining. Their research demonstrated that CNN
models can effectively generate trading signals for constructing portfolios with high
Sharpe ratios and highlighted their potential to enhance Sharpe ratios in markets beyond
the one the model was trained on.
      The results of this study confirms the findings of Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023),
showing that incorporating a model trained on U.S. data produces superior average results
compared to using a model trained solely on local data. However, the decision on whether
to retrain the model on local data is more complex. At first glance, the fully trainable
transfer learning model performs the best compared to the local model. It achieves the
highest number of statistically significant Sharpe ratio gains across markets and the
largest average Sharpe ratio improvements, both in the full sample and the statistically
significant subsample. However, when accounting for the size of the local training
datasets and directly comparing the performance to that of the model trained on U.S. data
without local retraining, the benefits become less clear. The results vary substantially
across the examined markets, emphasizing the need to account for market-specific factors

                                   3
when considering the use of transfer learning. In one country, Australia, the transfer
learning approach outperforms both the locally trained model and the direct application
of the U.S. model, with substantial and statistically significant results. The effectiveness
of transfer learning likely varies across markets due to differences in market similarities
and the quality and quantity of local data. Transfer learning relies on a base level of
similarity between markets, which may be stronger in some cases than others. The
findings suggest that when sufficient local data is available, using a pre-trained model
may harm model performance rather than improve it. In smaller datasets, the risk of
overfitting to noise increases. Regardless of dataset size, data quality and accuracy are
crucial for uncovering predictive patterns. Transfer learning is likely to be the most
effective when the target market is similar to the base market and has limited data –
allowing it to benefit from the pre-trained model – but still providing enough data to
reduce the risk of overfitting to noise.
      In practice, the implementation of transfer learning could have meaningful
implications. It offers the potential to enhance the performance of existing models and
could enable faster deployment of models in new markets where historical data is either
limited or of insufficient quality. These findings contribute to the growing literature on
the geographical transferability of trading strategies and the effectiveness of transfer
learning in financial applications.
      The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: first, a brief summary of the
related literature is provided. This is followed by the methodology section, which outlines
the data, models, and statistical tests used. Next, the results are presented, discussed, and
concluded. Finally, the appendix includes additional examples and illustrations of key
features and concepts.

2 Related Literature

The power of machine learning in predicting stock returns has been extensively
explored in the literature. Gu, Kelly, and Xiu (2020) demonstrate its superiority in
identifying complex patterns in price data that simpler models fail to capture. Similarly,
Brogaard and Zi (2020) demonstrate the superior performance of machine learning and
other non-linear models, in relation to other simpler methods. These findings underline
the ability of machine learning techniques to uncover complex structures in financial
data, advancing return predictability. Machine learning is widely used today for a range
of tasks across many different fields, with various algorithms and techniques proving
effective in solving complex problems. One such technique, convolutional neural
networks (CNNs), has been around for decades but gained renewed attention with the
success of AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever, and Hinton, 2012). This breakthrough
demonstrated the power of CNNs in deep learning and solidified their place as a key
tool in the field.
      CNNs have become a cornerstone in tackling computer vision tasks, owing to
their ability to effectively process and analyze image data. Building on this strength,
Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023) extended the use of CNNs into the field of asset pricing by
applying the architecture to predict stock returns. Their innovative approach involved
encoding stock data as images and employing CNNs to predict subsequent returns. They
claim that their paper represents the first published large-scale systematic application of
CNNs for predicting individual stock returns, at least to the best of their knowledge.

                                   4
Through testing across various time horizons, they found that the shortest horizon - one
week - produced the best results. This finding serves as the foundation for the time
period examined in this study. Moreover, they observed that for longer time periods, the
majority of returns accrued within the first week, highlighting the significance of short-
term patterns. The authors also tested the generalizability of their model by applying it,
without retraining, to international markets. This direct inference approach
demonstrated substantial performance improvements and statistical significance across
most markets. However, their method did not include retraining or fine-tuning the
model using data from the local market. Building on their findings, this study advances
their approach by introducing retraining within the transfer learning framework, aiming
to investigate the potential to further enhance the model's predictive performance across
diverse financial markets.

When using machine learning algorithms to predict stock market returns, the input data
is typically represented as numerical values. In this approach, however, the stock data is
transformed into images instead. Why encode the data as images? First, CNNs are
specifically designed for image analysis, making this transformation a natural fit. Second,
by representing the time-series data as an image, certain features and relationships in the
data may be interpreted more easily. It is useful to think of how humans usually prefer to
look at a chart, rather than a table of numerical values. Third, through the creation of
images the price and volume data of all stocks is converted into a common scale. Jiang,
Kelly, Xiu (2023) goes into further depth in their analysis of the benefits of stock data
image encoding.
      Although stock charts are readily available on many websites, the images used in
this study are custom-generated from historical stock data using code. Each image
represents five consecutive trading days, with a resolution of 32 pixels in height and 15
pixels in width. The width allocation corresponds to three pixels per trading day. The
image is divided into two distinct sections: the top 24 pixels capture price information,
while the bottom 7 pixels represent volume information. These sections are separated by
a single row of empty pixels. To construct the images, daily traded volume and the open,
high, low, and close (OHLC) prices are used, along with a moving average calculated
from the close prices. For each day, a vertical bar representing the trading range is created
in the central pixel column by connecting the high and low values. A single pixel to the
left of the bar marks the open price, while a pixel to the right marks the close price. The
moving average is represented by a continuous line connecting the daily average values,
and a separate bar is drawn to indicate the daily traded volume. Missing data points are
accommodated by leaving the corresponding pixel space blank. The idea is that the OHLC
bar provides insights into price trends and volatility, while the moving average serves as
a reference for longer-term price movements. Traditionally, color are often used in charts
to represent the direction of intraday price changes. However, in this study, direction is
conveyed by the relative positions of the open and close pixels, allowing the images to
remain monochromatic, with white pixels on a black background, simplifying the process
by avoiding multiple color channels.
      The United States (U.S.) market data consists of daily stock data for all stocks listed
on the NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ, sourced from the CRSP database and covering the
period from January 1, 1993, to December 31, 2004. Since the U.S. data is only used for
training the models, data past this date is not necessary. For the other 22 countries, daily
stock data for all firms listed on local exchanges is obtained from Refinitiv Eikon, with
the sample starting in 1993, or the earliest date the data becomes available, until
September 30, 2024. These data sources are well-known and commonly used in asset
pricing research. The Eikon data is delivered adjusted for corporate actions. In contrast,
an accompanying factor is used for this adjustment to the CRSP data. Weekly returns,
defined in this study as returns over five trading days, are calculated from the daily data.

3.2 The CNN Architecture

   Figure 2. Overview of the Model
   This figure provides a schematic representation of the model, illustrating each stage of data processing. The
   input image, convolution filter, feature matrix, and pooled output are shown at their actual sizes. However, the
   full model includes two convolutional layers (with 64 and 128 filters per layer), two pooling layers, and the
   LReLU activation function. Additionally, the fully connected layer in the actual model contains 15,360

CNNs are modeled after the human visual system, designed to process and interpret visual
information by mimicking how humans recognize patterns and features in images. Their
architecture consists of multiple layers that automatically learn patterns at different levels,
beginning with simple elements like edges and textures in the early layers and advancing
to more complex features and concepts in the deeper layers. Each component of a CNN's
architecture serves a distinct role in the data processing pipeline, working together to
extract and analyze meaningful information from the input.
      The data enters the model through the convolutional layers, where a series of filters
are applied to the input. These filters scan horizontally and vertically across the image,
performing numerical operations to generate feature maps. These maps highlight specific
patterns, such as edges, corners, or textures, at different locations in the image. During
training, the filters are continually adjusted to improve their ability to identify the features
most strongly linked to the model's prediction task. By the end of training, the filters are
fine-tuned to detect the features most relevant to the desired outcome. In this application,
the filters are specifically trained to identify features that are most predictive of the
direction of the subsequent weeks return. The convolutional process is illustrated and
described in more detail in figure A4 in the appendix. After the convolution operation, an
activation function is applied to introduce non-linearity into the model. This non-linearity
allows the model to learn more complex and abstract representations of the data. The
output of the convolution process is a feature map illustrating where in the images the
pattern of the filter is the strongest. The filters of the first convolution layer of the U.S.
model, as well as the resulting feature maps for an example image, are displayed in figure
A5 and A6 in the appendix. Next, pooling layers are used to downsample the feature maps
produced by the convolutional layers. This step reduces the computational complexity of
the model and helps mitigate overfitting. The models in this study use max-pooling layers,
which operate by selecting the maximum value within each designated region, which not

                                   7
only simplifies the data but also enhances the network’s robustness. This process helps
the model handle shifts and distortions in the input data more effectively. Figure A7 in
the appendix provides a more detailed illustration of the pooling operation.
      The layers in a CNN are modular, allowing for flexible stacking to build complex
architectures. The CNN architecture in this study uses two sets of convolutional and
pooling layers that are applied sequentially. Thus, the output of the first pooling layer
serves as the input to the second convolutional layer. This enables the model to
progressively combine previously extracted features into higher-level features as it passes
through each layer. At the end of the CNN architecture, a fully connected layer processes
the high-level features extracted by the preceding convolutional and pooling layers. This
layer learns during training to associate specific input features from the previous layers
with the possible outcomes, enabling it to make the final prediction. In this model, the
fully connected layer uses a softmax activation function to output two probabilities: one
for a positive return and another for a non-positive return. These probabilities always sum
to 1, since the return can only be either positive or non-positive. A dropout layer is also
included in the model as a regularization technique. During training, it randomly ignores
50% of the neurons, helping to prevent overfitting and enhance the model's ability to
generalize. CNN architectures are highly customizable, allowing adjustments to depth,
filter sizes, and layer types to suit specific tasks and datasets, making them versatile across
a range of applications. However, as this paper focuses on exploring the potential of
transfer learning rather than optimizing CNN structures for stock return prediction, all
CNN models used in this study are based on the 5-day model used by Jiang, Kelly, and
Xiu (2023). In summary, the models take an image as input and process it through the
network to produce a predicted probability of a positive return for that stock over the next
five trading days. Simply put, the process works as follows: the convolutional layers learn
and extract patterns from the images, the pooling layers simplify these patterns, and the
fully connected layer uses these simplified patterns to make the final prediction.

3.3 Training the Models
The models in this analysis focus solely on predicting the probabilities of a positive or
non-positive return, without considering the size of the return. This makes it a
classification task, where images associated with a subsequent positive return are labeled
as 1, and those with a non-positive return are labeled as 0. Accordingly, the standard
objective function for classification problems, the cross-entropy function, is used to train
the model. During training, the model seeks to minimize the loss of this function which
is defined as

                     𝐿(𝑦, 𝑦̂  ) = −𝑦 log(𝑦̂  ) − (1 − 𝑦) log(1 − 𝑦̂  ),

where  ŷ  is the softmax output from the final step in the CNN.
     The CNN models were trained using stock data from before January 1, 2005. The
dataset was divided into two subsets: 70% for training and 30% for validation. This split
ensured the model had sufficient data to learn effectively while reserving a substantial
portion for an unbiased evaluation of its performance. During training, a batch size of 256
images was used, determined through trial and error to suit the specific hardware used for
training the models. The learning rate was set to 0.001, providing a balance between

                                   8
steady convergence and stability. This rate helped minimize the risk of overshooting
optimal parameter values while ensuring the model effectively reduced the loss function.
The Adam optimizer was used to update model parameters during training. Training was
conducted iteratively until the model showed no improvement in the validation error for
two consecutive epochs. This early stopping criterion helped prevent overfitting, ensuring
the model did not overly adjust its parameters to the training data at the cost of
generalizing to unseen data. In this study, the models are trained only once on the training
dataset and are not recursively retrained. Recursive retraining, where models are
continuously updated as new predictions are made, could improve performance by
incorporating more data and staying updated with the latest information. However, this
approach is computationally intensive and was not implemented in this analysis. Training
neural networks is a stochastic process, meaning that results may vary slightly between
individual training runs. In this study, however, each model was trained only once.
Training each model multiple times and averaging the results could enhance the
robustness of the analysis.

3.4 Transfer Learning
Transfer learning is a technique in which a pre-trained machine learning model is used as
a starting point for a new task, rather than training a model from scratch. This method
allows models trained on large datasets to be adapted to related tasks with smaller
datasets, improving efficiency and performance. In the context of CNNs, transfer learning
typically involves taking a network trained on a large, diverse dataset and fine-tuning it
for a specific target task. This leverages the hierarchical features learned during the initial
training, such as basic patterns in the earlier layers and more complex, task-specific
features in the deeper layers. Fine-tuning adjusts these learned weights to better fit the
specific nuances of the new task. Transfer learning addresses the common challenge of
limited labeled data, providing a robust starting point that significantly reduces training
time and computational requirements. By building on the generalized knowledge
captured by a pre-trained model, transfer learning not only speeds up convergence during
training but often leads to better overall performance compared to models trained
exclusively on the smaller target dataset. Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023) refer to transfer
learning as applying a pre-trained model directly to new data without any retraining or
fine-tuning. In contrast, this report considers all instances of transfer learning to include
some degree of retraining to adapt the model to the specifics of the new task. The
application of a pre-trained model to new data without any modifications is referred to
here as direct application. This distinction highlights the emphasis in this study on
leveraging retraining to improve model adaptability and performance across different
datasets.
      In finance, transfer learning offers a way to utilize extensive data and learned
representations from one market or asset class and apply them to another. For instance,
models trained on data-rich and highly liquid markets can be used as a basis for training
and fine-tuning models for emerging or less liquid markets with limited data availability.
This approach relies on the assumption that financial markets share underlying structural
similarities and behavioral patterns shaped by economic principles, investor psychology,
and macroeconomic factors. Transfer learning leverages these shared features by
extracting them from data-rich environments and fine-tuning them to reflect the unique
dynamics of specific target markets. For this to be effective, the target market must not

                                   9
only benefit from the additional data but also exhibit distinct characteristics not fully
captured by the base model. Additionally, the local data must be of sufficient quality and
quantity to enable the model to identify and adapt to meaningful signals rather than noise.
      In this study, the transfer learning approach begins with training a CNN on the U.S.
dataset, which serves as the base model for fine-tuning. The large size of the U.S. dataset
allows the model to capture a broad range of patterns and behaviors, providing a strong
foundation for subsequent adaptation. Next, the transfer learning models are fine-tuned
with data from local markets, enabling the network to adjust its weights and biases to
better capture and reflect local market conditions. For each market, two transfer learning
models are trained: one with frozen convolutional layers, referred to as the TL model, and
another fully trainable model, referred to as the TL FT model. The TL model retains the
patterns learned from the U.S. base model, focusing exclusively on refining the
interpretation of these patterns in the fully connected layer. In contrast, the TL FT model
is fully trainable, allowing adjustments to both the decision-making process and the
detected patterns, enabling them to better align with the unique characteristics of the local
data. The fully unfrozen TL FT model provides greater adaptability, allowing it to better
capture and align with the unique dynamics of the local market. However, this flexibility
increases the risk of overfitting, as the model may become overly specialized to the
training data, potentially incorporating irregularities or errors present in the dataset. If the
local data is insufficient in quality or quantity, this overfitting can reduce the model's
generalizability, leading to poorer performance on out-of-sample data compared to the
frozen-layer TL model. On the other hand, when the local data is robust, the TL FT
model's adaptability can be a significant advantage, enhancing its overall predictive
capabilities. This trade-off underscores the importance of balancing model adaptability
with the risk of overfitting. In this study, both approaches are systematically evaluated.

3.5 CNNs Compared to Traditional Technical Analysis
The use of CNNs for stock chart analysis marks a notable shift from traditional technical
analysis methods. Although both aim to interpret visualized market data to guide trading
decisions, their approaches, objectives, and the nature of their signals are fundamentally
different. Traditional technical analysis relies on predefined patterns and indicators
historically linked to certain market behaviors. Examples include chart patterns like head
and shoulders, support and resistance levels, and crossings and other behaviors of
technical indicators such as moving averages and the Relative Strength Index. These tools
are based on established heuristics and are often subjectively interpreted by analysts,
introducing personal bias and variability. Unlike the systematic processes of CNNs, the
signals derived from traditional technical analysis are largely qualitative, relying heavily
on an analyst's judgment and experience to distinguish meaningful trends from market
noise. Furthermore, the validity of these patterns is primarily anecdotal, drawn from
historical observations rather than being rigorously validated through empirical research.
In contrast, training a CNN represents a data-driven, learning-based approach that
eliminates the reliance on predefined patterns. CNNs autonomously identify and
prioritize relevant features from large volumes of historical price data, without requiring
explicit human input. This objective process reduces the subjectivity inherent in
traditional technical analysis, as the model's parameters are adjusted using quantitative
metrics that measure the accuracy and reliability of its predictions. The signals analyzed
by CNNs are inherently quantitative, derived directly from numerical data that the

                                  10
network processes through multiple layers of convolution and pooling to uncover
complex patterns and dependencies. CNNs rely on large datasets to uncover and validate
relationships within the data, whereas traditional methods often depend on anecdotal
evidence derived from selective historical instances. These patterns, while intuitively
appealing, may not consistently hold across different time periods or market
environments. CNNs also excel in modeling nonlinear and complex relationships that
surpass human cognitive capabilities. Constrained by the limitations of manual analysis,
traditional technical analysis often simplifies data to manage its complexity, which can
result in a loss of critical information. In contrast, CNNs can analyze vast datasets and
uncover complex patterns that are difficult for human analysts to detect manually,
significantly improving predictive accuracy and reliability. In this study, no systematic
comparison between the CNN approach and traditional technical analysis was made.
However, Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023) found that their weekly CNN model significantly
outperformed all the 7,846 technical signals they compared it against. These findings
highlight the superiority of CNNs over traditional technical analysis.

3.6 Evaluating the Models
Each model predicts the probability of positive returns for individual stocks in the
upcoming week. For each period, these predictions are used to sort the stocks by their
predicted probabilities, dividing them into deciles. An equally weighted, zero-cost long-
short portfolio is then constructed by taking long positions in the top decile stocks and
short positions in the bottom decile stocks. These positions are held for five trading days
before rebalancing. The performance of this portfolio is evaluated by calculating its
Sharpe ratio, which is used to compare the effectiveness of different strategies. The
Sharpe ratios are calculated using weekly returns and then annualized. The Sharpe ratio
is generally defined as

An analysis of the returns generated by the models reveals that they are all static, as
confirmed by the Augmented Dickey-Fuller test. Additionally, the Breusch-Pagan test
indicates that the returns of all strategies exhibit heteroskedasticity. When examining the
Autocorrelation Function (ACF) and Partial Autocorrelation Function (PACF) for the
returns, no significant autocorrelation is detected.
      Ledoit and Wolf (2008) note that serial correlation in returns is often negligible and
argue that volatility clustering, reflected in the correlation of squared returns, is more
common. When analyzing the ACF and PACF of the squared returns, a few markets show

weak indications of slight autocorrelation, but no consistent trend is observed across
markets.
      To conduct hypothesis tests on the Sharpe ratios, an appropriate testing
methodology must be chosen. Since the true distributions of the Sharpe ratios for each
strategy are unknown, they must be estimated. The central limit theorem (CLT) is often
used for large sample sizes, but it assumes that the samples are independent and
identically distributed (IID), a condition that time-series data typically does not meet.
These challenges, coupled with the need for confidence intervals and hypothesis testing
for Sharpe ratios, have prompted extensive research on the subject.
      Based on a review and comparison of various approaches by Riondato (2018), this
study adopts the methodology developed by Ledoit and Wolf (2008). Their framework
estimates the distribution of Sharpe ratio differences between two strategies, adjusts it
through studentization and computes a two-sided confidence interval for a specific
statistical significance. The null hypothesis, ΔSR = 0, is then considered rejected at the α
confidence level if the 1-α confidence interval does not include zero. This approach,
known as a studentized circular block bootstrap, offers three key features that make it
well-suited for this task. First, bootstrap sampling with replacement is used to address the
lack of a known distribution and estimate it. Second, to account for the dependence
structure in financial time series data, bootstrap sampling is performed in blocks of
consecutive returns. This helps address heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation in the
returns. The block size is chosen to minimize the mean squared error of the bias and
variance, with a penalty for small block sizes. In this study, an analysis across all
strategies and markets results in a median optimal block size of 45, which is used
consistently. Third, studentization adjusts the difference between the total sample Sharpe
ratio and each bootstrapped sample Sharpe ratio using the standard error of the specific
bootstrapped sample. The resulting distribution of studentized values is then used to
construct the confidence interval. Additionally, since the strategies compared in this study
place trades in the same markets and time periods, a paired bootstrap technique is
employed. This adjustment accounts for common market factors and enhances the
accuracy of the Sharpe ratio comparisons. While this paired bootstrap is not part of the
original framework by Ledoit and Wolf (2008), their methodology is designed for general
Sharpe ratio comparisons and does not require trades to occur in the same market or
period. Further details and examples of the framework's steps are provided in figure A8,
A9 and A10 in the appendix.

5 Discussion
5.1 The Effect of U.S. Data on Performance
Models leveraging U.S. data, either exclusively or as the foundation for transfer learning,
demonstrates improved performance compared to locally trained models in a majority of
the markets and on average across all markets. These results align with the findings of
Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023), while also confirming that the superiority of U.S.-based
models extends to transfer learning approaches as well. Unlike their study, which
included data up to 2019, this analysis incorporates nearly five additional years of data,
covering the COVID-19 pandemic, periods of heightened inflation, interest rate increases,
and geopolitical instability. Additionally, all code used in this study was developed
independently, with no reuse of prior implementations. This reinforces the robustness of
the confirmation of their findings.

                                  17
An inspection of the ΔSR for the U.S. and transfer learning models compared to the
locally trained model suggests that the size of the underlying local training data perhaps
influence the observed ΔSR. Figure A12 in the appendix provides a visual representation
of this through scatter plots. For all models, none of the five countries with the largest
local datasets show a statistically significant positive ΔSR. In contrast, several instances
exhibit statistically significant negative ΔSR values. This could suggest that when the
local dataset is sufficiently large, there is no advantage – and potentially even a
disadvantage – to using a model trained on a data-richer market, regardless of whether
transfer learning is applied or not. Recalculating the average ΔSR while excluding the
five markets with the largest local training datasets provides a clearer picture of
performance in smaller-data markets. For the total sample and the significant subset, the
recalculated ΔSR values are as follows: 0.68 and 0.98 for the U.S. model, 0.60 and 0.77
for the TL model, and 0.70 and 0.84 for the TL FT model. These results highlight the
impact of local data size on the effectiveness of models leveraging external data.
      Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023) also observed that larger local dataset sizes tended to
negatively affect the ΔSR, although this relationship was weaker in their findings
compared to mine. While they reported market size based on the number of stocks in each
market, I chose to report the number of training images instead. This metric better
captures the total data available for training, as it accounts for both the number of stocks
and the length of historical data for each stock. However, differences in the time horizons
of the datasets as well as the specific markets studied and the split dates for training and
testing most likely also contribute to discrepancies in the results. For instance, Jiang,
Kelly, and Xiu (2023) found a highly significant and substantially positive ΔSR for
Canada and Japan, whereas my findings show a substantially negative ΔSR for Canada
and a ΔSR close to zero and not statistically significant for Japan when comparing the
U.S. model to the local model. Both studies observed a negative ΔSR for South Korea;
however, in my study, this result is statistically significant, while in theirs, it is not. Both
studies found the ΔSR for the United Kingdom to be slightly negative and not statistically
significant. The indication that introducing a pre-trained model from a data-rich market
does not improve performance once the local dataset reaches a sufficient size aligns with
intuitive expectations. This result is consistent with the assumption that pre-trained
models address the lack of adequate local data. However, when local data is already
sufficient, the pre-trained model may instead reduce performance by introducing patterns
that are less relevant or accurate for the specific market. In these markets, gradually
reducing the training data and observing its impact on results could help identify the
breakpoint at which incorporating global data is no longer beneficial. It is also worth
considering that additional data might still be beneficial, but only if paired with a more
complex model with greater capacity to utilize it effectively. However, exploring
alternative CNN configurations or increasing model complexity falls outside the scope of
this paper.
      In theory, the performance of the U.S. model should serve as a lower bound for the
TL FT model, while not limiting its potential to improve. Since all layers in the TL FT
model are trainable, it has the flexibility during training to configure itself identically to
a model trained solely on local data. When examining countries with a statistically
significant negative ΔSR for the U.S. model compared to the local model, the negative
ΔSR are less severe for the transfer learning models, with Canada being the most notable
example. Although the U.S. model does not strictly serve as a lower performance limit
for the TL FT model in my results, it is reasonable to expect that with additional training,

                                  18
the TL FT model would converge toward the same weights as a model trained exclusively
on local data, thereby reducing this gap. However, this assumes that the local data is of
sufficient quality and that the training data accurately represents the evaluation data. In
contrast, this behavior is not anticipated for the TL model, as its frozen convolutional
layers are not updated during retraining. While this limitation reduces its ability to adapt
to patterns in the local data, it could, in theory, serve as a safeguard against unreliable
patterns in low-quality local data by relying on the patterns learned from the base model.
However, this potential feature was not explored in this paper, and there is no evidence
in the results to suggest that this effect occurred.

5.2 Retraining Compared to Direct Application
Moving on to analyzing the performance of the transfer learning models compared to the
U.S. model. The results suggests that the size of the training dataset perhaps is of
importance here as well, as all the statistically significant differences are positive for the
largest markets and negative for almost all the smallest markets. This suggests that
transfer learning recovers some of the performance lost in large markets when using a
U.S. model instead of a locally trained model. This is intuitive, as the local data has been
shown to outperform U.S. data in these markets, and the transfer learning model
incorporates that advantage. The reasons for inferior performance in smaller markets are
in contrast likely to be more complex and challenging to identify. Conceptually, transfer
learning is designed to enhance performance when the specific dataset is relatively small.
However, smaller datasets also carry the risk that the model learns patterns that fail to
generalize, reducing its effectiveness. Beyond quantity, the quality of the local data must
also be considered. In markets with poor data quality, retraining the model on local data
is disadvantageous. In such cases, applying the U.S. model without retraining is likely a
better option. Countries that are well-developed today may have accurate recent data, but
earlier data may suffer from issues linked to historically weak institutions. This poses a
problem if the training period includes these lower-quality data periods. In such cases,
setting a cut-off date for when data quality became reliable is essential for retraining on
local data. Unlike dataset size, evaluating the historical quality of institutions and their
data is a more complex task. Although this study does not attempt to classify markets by
data quality, it is an important factor that practitioners should consider when applying
transfer learning.
      Looking at the specific markets, we find that the TL and TL FT models significantly
outperforms the U.S. model in Canada. However, the local model performs the best.
Australia exhibits the strongest benefit of the transfer learning approach with both models
outperforming both the U.S. and local models substantially, with statistical significance.
The transfer learning approach is also superior to the U.S. approach for South Korea.
However, the performance compared to the local model is not different with statistical
significance. In contrast, some markets did not experience the same level of improvement.
In Greece, the performance of both transfer learning models was with statistical
significance substantially worse compared to the U.S. model. In Germany the same is
true, however at a smaller scale and with less statistical significance for the TL FT model.
In Italy, the TL model, but not the TL FT model, also performed statistically significantly
worse in relation to the U.S. model. In Spain, both transfer learning approaches performed
equally worse, although with the lowest level of statistical significance.
      The inherently complex nature of CNNs, as well as neural networks in general,
makes it challenging to extract meaningful insights from the model to inform future

                                  19
theory. This limitation is a well-known drawback of neural networks. While it is possible
to extract elements such as filters, feature maps, and individual weights learned by the
model, interpreting them in a way that provides meaningful theoretical insights remains
extremely difficult. Figure A5 and A6 in the appendix illustrates this well.
      A comparison of the TL and TL FT models indicates that allowing all layers to be
trainable during fine-tuning results in only small differences in performance. While the
fully trainable TL FT model achieves slightly higher ΔSRs in all markets with statistically
significant results, the number of significant markets is limited. This suggests that full
fine-tuning might be a marginally better approach, but the evidence is neither strong nor
conclusive. However, in cases where the local dataset is small and there is a high risk of
overfitting to noise rather than learning valuable patterns, it could be theorized that the
TL model may provide a more conservative alternative. By freezing the convolutional
layers, the TL model retains the foundational patterns learned from U.S. data while
allowing for localized adaptation through fine-tuning the fully connected layer. While
this approach has theoretical merit, the results of this study do not provide evidence to
support its effectiveness in practice.
      A common critique of backtested strategies is that frequent trading may cause them
to not remain profitable once trading costs are accounted for. While this study does not
specifically address trading costs, Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023) found that their
implementation of the CNN model maintained profitability even after adjusting for
standard trading costs. A note of caution is warranted regarding the high average returns
and Sharpe ratios observed in some markets. The use of advanced technology to generate
trading signals during a backtested period, when such technology was not available, may
inflate these figures, potentially overstating their practical feasibility. However, this does
not diminish the core findings of the paper. The focus is on the relative performance of
the strategies, measured through differences in Sharpe ratios, rather than their absolute
values. This relative approach ensures the validity of the comparative analysis, regardless
of potential distortions in the absolute figures.

6 Conclusion
CNN models leveraging U.S. data outperform those trained solely on local data,
on average and in the most individual markets. This confirms and extends the findings of
previous studies. However, consistent with the principles of transfer learning and its
applications in other domains, the results show that transfer learning is not a universal
solution for performance improvement. The findings further suggest that while using a
model trained on a larger dataset can be beneficial initially, the advantages diminish and
can even become disadvantages once the local dataset reaches a certain size.
      Based on the findings of this study, the TL FT model is likely the best choice if a
single model is to be used across all markets. However, the average performance metrics
are heavily influenced by individual markets. Deviations observed in specific countries
compared to the findings of Jiang, Kelly, and Xiu (2023) further underscore the risks of
relying solely on averages that are heavily influenced by a few markets when deciding to
implement these models. Instead, the decision on which model to use is likely best made
on a market-by-market basis. As general performance improvements across all markets
should not be expected, transfer learning should be viewed as a tool to selectively leverage
the strengths of both global and local data under favorable circumstances.

Transfer learning with retraining shows some promise, but its effectiveness varies
significantly across markets. Further research is needed to better understand the
conditions under which transfer learning may be successfully deployed. Among the
markets studied, Australia is the only one where the transfer learning approach is found
to outperform both the local and U.S. models with statistical significance. Notably,
transfer learning is not found to be statistically worse than both the local and U.S. models
in any market. There is no strong evidence to indicate whether freezing the convolutional
layers during retraining provides a clear advantage or disadvantage in the performance of
transfer learning models.

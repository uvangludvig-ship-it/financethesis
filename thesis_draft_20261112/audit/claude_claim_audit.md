# Claude draft claim audit and strongest defensible story

Prepared 12 November 2026. Paragraph identifiers refer to `claude_draft_text.txt`, extracted from the supplied Claude DOCX. This is an audit and editorial recommendation, not a change to any manuscript. Frozen empirical source: commit `d2d41666ed213e4cc1f6cb382313f59c29e87ea0`. All new files contain aggregate results only. No event observations were exported.

## Main finding

Claude improves the narrative by asking what remains and connecting holdings, other funds, and sister shares. Several of its strongest sentences, however, turn descriptive associations or imprecise null results into mechanisms or bounds. The defensible central claim is **positive average addition returns in 2017–2026, with the latest block unresolved, accompanied by substantial changes in the event-window length and measured fund demand**. Neither an identified Swedish elasticity nor a proven absence of reversal or a 1.2-point upper bound follows.

Use the independently checked results in `thesis_draft_20261112/audit/verification_report.md` and `replication_revision_report.md` as the empirical authority where they supersede the frozen first draft. The prior work reconstructed all 311 complete holdings numerators, all 468 stored share denominators, 327 comparison-repair checks and 419 seven-return decomposition checks. These are numerical verification checks, not new independent financial observations or authentication of every source notice or zero position.

## Useful Claude additions that pass numerical checking

### Observed fund demand gives the mechanism discussion a concrete scale

All four rows use 136 addition events in 19 reviews, 2017–2026. Units are percent of shares outstanding; positive means net buying. Intervals below all use analytical CR1 clustered by review and t(18), so readers can compare them directly.

| Observed production group | Mean | Analytical 95% interval |
|---|---:|---:|
| Covered OMXSB-linked funds, C | 1.0301 | [0.8529, 1.2073] |
| Other funds classified as passive | 0.3966 | [0.2468, 0.5464] |
| Funds classified as active | −0.2398 | [−0.5227, 0.0432] |
| All included funds | 1.1869 | [0.7508, 1.6230] |

The C subgroups are 0.6254 strict followers [0.4672, 0.7836], 0.2554 variants [0.1356, 0.3751], and 0.1493 classified balanced funds [0.1029, 0.1957]. These disjoint subgroup holdings changes sum to C, and C + other passive + active sums to all included funds event by event. “Active” is the source's residual `ej passiv` classification, not an authenticated universe of all active managers. Other passive combines OMXS30, small-cap-index, and other Swedish/foreign passive categories identified by the source's classification rules. Stable classification and two-sided reporting availability are required for each included fund-event.

The frozen active-fund interval uses wild-bootstrap inversion [−0.5227, 0.0544]. It is also retained in the detailed verification CSV; do not silently label that interval as analytical CR1. The common-method table above is a new presentation of existing means, not a new sample or discovery.

Strong wording: **“Observed funds are net buyers of additions on average. Buying is not confined to the OMXSB-linked group: other funds classified as passive also increase their holdings, while the estimated active-fund reduction is smaller and imprecise. The data do not identify who supplies the residual shares.”** This supports a measured flow description; it does not establish why prices moved or that all offsetting sales came from outside the entire fund sector.

### Event-level fit is small, not identically zero

Using precisely the frozen Figure 3 sample and its x/y definitions, OLS with an intercept yields R²=0.00277436 for 171 additions (32 reviews), and 0.02486446 for 140 deletions (29 reviews). Thus Claude's 0.3% and 2.5% are correctly rounded. They were independently checked by residual sums of squares, squared Pearson correlation, and statsmodels OLS. This is not the ratio M: the addition slope is about 0.94, whereas M=2.63 in a different, later sample and with a different estimand. For the 2017–2026 subset, addition R² is 0.2633% and deletion R² is 5.2383%; keep the all-period versus later-period distinction.

Strong wording: **“Measured purchases explain little of the overall event-to-event variation in returns in a simple linear fit. This reinforces the distinction between a ratio of sample means and a causal effect of an additional purchase.”** Do not infer the absence of any relation, use R² as a causal test, or infer a Swedish-US difference without harmonized samples and uncertainty.

### FI supports later-period measurement scale

The source step-0 comparison is exactly reproducible: 208 matched demand records, correlation 0.842379, Lipper mean 1.09185% and FI mean 1.12136%. These are signed in the direction of the index change. The 106 matched addition observations used for the FI return-demand ratio are a different, more restricted sample. Keep both denominators explicit. This is useful corroboration from a different reporting channel; it does not establish complete coverage or exogeneity.

## Strongest valid story to build the paper around

1. **Start with the replication question and a precise answer.** The broad Swedish benchmark provides 536 supplied changes over 39 reviews, with 256 additions and 215 deletions in the primary return sample. Additions in 2017–2026 have positive average market-adjusted event returns of 2.15%, but the 2022–2026 estimate is imprecise and sensitive to individual observations. The path is not a smooth decline, and deletion estimates in the later decade are inconclusive.

2. **Make institutional timing the central qualification to the historical comparison.** The announcement-to-effective interval shortens markedly. A same-cohort common seven-return diagnostic puts early and late additions near 1–2% while the large first-to-last change lies in the remainder. This is an informative measurement result: the long-window history is not automatically the history of a fixed-duration response. It neither proves time invariance nor causally explains the decline.

3. **Then show what the covered funds actually do.** On later addition events they buy approximately 1.03% of shares, with another 0.40% from other observed passive categories and a smaller imprecise active reduction. The all-included-fund average is +1.19%. The FI comparison supports the scale in a later matched subset. Historical coverage and the adjustment screen make the early ratio particularly fragile; no all-history fixed fund cohort exists.

4. **Use the ratio to describe scale rather than infer a demand curve.** On matched later events, 2.71% mean return divided by 1.03% mean covered demand gives 2.63. Observational event selection, incomplete demand measurement, changing windows and the small event-level fit prevent reading it as a causal price multiplier. Do not centre the story on seven times an old US ratio.

5. **Use the Swedish extensions to test interpretations, without claiming they identify one.** The repaired size-screen comparison remains positive. Sister-class point estimates show issuer-wide co-movement in a selected small cohort, leaving the relative component imprecise. Finite-horizon cumulative returns stay positive in the exploratory paired calculation, while post-event returns are too imprecise to establish zero reversal. These facts are compatible with cross-class transmission of demand, broader benchmark-related flows and other issuer information.

6. **End with the economic contribution, not an audit checklist.** Positive later-decade return associations coexist with concentrated implementation trading and substantial observed fund buying. The study shows which parts of the disappearing-effect comparison travel to Sweden and which are sensitive to institutional timing and investor measurement. A causal mechanism remains unresolved. Keep detailed source and inference limitations available in methods/appendix rather than burying the reader in verification counts.

1 Introduction
Gender equality in the workplace has become a prominent political issue, resulting in the implemen-
tation of board gender quotas in several countries. Historically, women have been underrepresented in
higher-level positions, commonly referred to as a “glass ceiling” (Bertrand, Black, Jensen, and Lleras-
Muney, 2019). To address this issue, European countries have passed hard laws to increase board
gender diversity, such as the ones in Norway, Italy, Spain, and France. Others, like Luxembourg, the
UK, and Sweden, have encouraged a specific percentage of female board members through soft laws
(Ferreira, Ginglinger, Paris-Dauphine, and Skalli, 2021). As stated by the directive passed by the
European parliament in 2022, starting in July 2026, all listed companies in the European Union must
have at least 40% of their non-executive directors and 33% of their executive directors of the under-
represented gender (European Commission, 2022). This new EU directive will require many firms to
replace their current board members to meet the new requirements, making it crucial how a gender
quota affects firms and their boards. There is, however, limited evidence on the effect of gender quotas
on board and firm outcomes. A prominent study by Ahern and Dittmar (2012) on the Norwegian
gender quota from 2003 found significant costs to shareholders and a decrease in board skills. As such,
this paper employs a methodology akin to Ahern and Dittmar (2012) to examine the impact of the
French gender quota imposed in 2011. The objective is to determine whether, irrespective of policy
implementation details and contextual factors, gender quotas have adverse effects on board skills and
firm value.
 Our study addresses the following research question:
 Are board gender quotas unfavorable for firm value?
 In 2011, the Copé-Zimmerman law was enacted in France, mandating that 40% of board members
must be of the underrepresented gender by 2017, with an interim target of 20% by 2014. This law
applies to all firms headquartered in France that are publicly listed and non-listed firms with over 500
employees or revenues exceeding €50m over the previous three years (Républice Française, 2011). Any
board appointment that violates the stipulated provisions or does not help remedy the non-compliance
will be considered invalid. In addition, failure to comply with any of the mandated proportion re-
quirements leads to a suspension of payment of director fees until the gender diversity in the board of
directors meets the stipulated quota threshold.
 We have used both the event study methodology and instrumental variable approach used by
Ahern and Dittmar (2012) and compared our findings to their prominent study on the Norwegian
gender quota. Thus, understanding the French context in relation to the Norwegian one is crucial.
Publicly listed companies in Norway were initially encouraged in 2003 to voluntarily meet a gender
quota threshold of 40% within one and a half years. However, when compliance fell short, companies
were granted a two-year transition period to reach the same threshold, but this time, facing penalties if
they did not comply. Upon no fulfillment in 2008, the company would be dissolved. This is in contrast
to France, where firms had six years, and not two years as in Norway, to fulfill the requirement
stipulated by the law. Additionally, the penalty for non-compliance in France, suspension of payment
of director fees, is more lenient than in Norway, where firms risk being dissolved. Not only did policy
implementation details differ between the two countries, but the female labor supply setting also
differed, with France having a less favorable situation, as found by Adams and Kirchmaier (2015).
 According to our research on the French example, the implementation of gender quotas does not,
regardless of context and policy implementation details, have an adverse effect on firm value. The
results of the regressions performed to investigate the effects of the French law showed no unfavorable
results. Our analysis first reveals that the stock market anticipated a smooth transition, as there was
no difference in industry-adjusted immediate returns for companies that were closer or further away
from the quota threshold. Secondly, our long-term study on how firm value was affected also did not
support the finding of any adverse effects. This is in contrast with the study performed by Ahern and
Dittmar (2012), which found both a negative immediate stock market effect and unfavorable outcomes
on long-term firm value following the law’s implementation. In Norway, it was found that the skill level
of board members decreased as a result of the law, which subsequently caused the decline in firm value
(Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). We attribute the lack of adverse firm value effects in France to the fact
that our regression showed that the law had no negative effect on the percentage of board members
who had industry-relevant backgrounds or strong financial skills. We argue that this was enabled by

the more extended transition period, which enabled a gradual replacement of new competent female
board members replacing the incumbent male directors.
 To conduct our analysis, we gathered data from a diverse set of sources. Data on our treatment
group, companies located in France that were listed on Euronext Paris, along with our control group,
US-situated companies listed on American exchanges, was mainly sourced from Refinitiv Eikon. For
firms that had some missing data points, we acquired the remaining data using alternative sources.
Missing financial data was obtained from CapitalIQ, and missing data on the board was gathered
from the companies’ annual reports. Our dataset included almost 100 French firms, a sample size
comparable to that used by Ahern and Dittmar (2012).
 Our research involved an event study, per the methodology of Ahern and Dittmar (2012), to
investigate the impact of the quota announcement on companies’ stock prices. We divided the French
companies into two groups based on their female board participation at the time of the event study:
Low diversity and High diversity. We chose a threshold value at the median of 8.33% in order to create
two approximately equally large groups.
 Since the law had been frequently debated in parliament prior to its enactment, it was expected
that a well-informed market would have already factored in the changes before the date when the
statute was passed. Instead, we examine the point in time when the bill was initially brought up in
parliament, January 20th, 2010. On the same day, an attention-grabbing news article called “Shaking
up the corporate world” (France 24, 2010) was published. We found no significant difference in the
average industry-adjusted stock returns between companies with fewer or more female directors in
France, as the difference of 0.83% was insignificant. This suggests that the stock market did not
foresee any negative impact on companies with fewer female board members. Our study results are
robust and consider various factors, including board size, firm size, and assets. We also had a control
group consisting of American firms listed in the US.
 In order to evaluate the long-term influence of the quota on firm value, an instrumental variable
procedure was used. Similar to Ahern and Dittmar (2012), we utilized the pre-quota cross-sectional
variation in female board representation to instrument for exogenous changes to corporate boards
following the quota. As all public limited firms were required to have at least 20% female directors
in 2014 and 40% in 2017, those companies that had a higher percentage of female directors prior
to the quota were less restricted than the ones with lower female representation. The results from
instrumental variable estimates reveal that the quota did not negatively impact Tobin’s Q, as the
point estimate -0.279% was insignificant. These results include firm and time-fixed effects to control
for both observed and unobserved heterogeneity between the firms and yearly trends. Placebo tests
run on US firms, as also done by Ahern and Dittmar (2012), show no significant connection between
the increase of women on boards due to the quota and subsequent changes in firm value. These
outcomes demonstrate that the quota mandated by the law and its subsequent board reorganizations
did not have any unfavorable effect on firm value. Furthermore, a robustness check was conducted
by examining how the law altered the firms’ return on equity (ROE), and consistent with our prior
findings, no significant effect was found.
 To gain a deeper understanding of why there was no loss in value, we analyzed how women were
added to the boards and how the quota affected the skills of directors. This is important as Ahern and
Dittmar (2012) concluded that the negative effect on firm value resulting from the Norwegian gender
quota was attributable to a decrease in board skills. In this study, we define board-specific skills as the
percentage of board members who have either an industry-specific background or a strong financial
background. Our instrumental variable regression on board skills showed a positive coefficient of
0.161%, which was insignificant, meaning that board skills cannot be concluded to have been negatively
affected as a result of the law.
 We conclude that our study did not find any negative effects of the gender quota as a result of
board-specific skills not having declined, as they did in Norway (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). This was
probably made possible by the gradual replacement of board members in France, whereas in Norway,
the negative effects were a result of firms making drastic adjustments to meet the deadline of the quota
(Ahern and Dittmar, 2012). As the female supply setting was less preferable in France than in Norway
(Adams and Kirchmaier, 2015), our study concludes that the smooth replacement was enabled by the
longer transition period and possibly the more lenient penalty for non-compliance. These results may
offer guidance to policymakers on maintaining firm value while promoting greater gender equality in
the boardroom.

 Related literature This paper contributes to the large and growing literature on board gender
quotas’ impact on firm value (Ahern and Dittmar, 2012; Adams and Ferreira, 2009; Bernile, Bhagwat,
and Yonker, 2018; Ginglinger and Raskopf, 2023; Likitapiwat, Treepongkaruna, Jiraporn, and Uyar,
2022; Greene, Intintoli, and Kahle, 2020; Burrow, Fedorets, and Gibert, 2018). More specifically,
by studying the immediate stock price reaction at the announcement of the board gender quota and
studying its long-term impact on Tobin’s Q in a Norwegian firm setting, Ahern and Dittmar (2012)
argue that forced increased board gender diversity caused deterioration of firm value. They saw a
significant negative long-term impact, as well as an adverse immediate stock market reaction. A
similar study by Burrows et al. (2018) investigated the financial outcomes of the German law from
2015 that mandated a supervisory board (two-tier system) gender quota for approximately 100 large
corporations. The authors found no significant effect on the financial outcomes of the firms. Also,
Greene et al. (2020) studied the immediate stock market reaction in 2018 to the announcement of the
first mandated board gender quota in the US. In California, firms were allowed a transition period
of a bit over one year to have at least one female on the board, and upon no fulfillment, firms were
fined $300,000. The authors (2020) found that announcement returns decreased on average by 1.2%.
We contribute to this literature by providing evidence on the impact of the board gender quota on
firm value in another context and where the policy implementation details differ. Firstly, the French
quota was implemented in a way that allowed firms a relatively longer time to comply with the law. In
comparison to Norway’s two-year transition period and California’s slightly over one-year transition
period to avoid penalties for non-compliance, French firms were allowed six years to change their
boards. Secondly, the consequence of failure to fulfill the quota was relatively softer in France than
in Norway. In France, firms were punished with suspension of paying director fees, but in Norway, it
was dissolution. Finally, France had a worse female supply setting relative to Norway (Adams and
Kirchmaier, 2015). Ultimately, we shed light on what factors play a crucial role in how firm value is
affected by the implementation of a new gender quota at the board level.
 Furthermore, we contribute to the literature on how board gender quotas affect the characteristics
of the board members. Several papers cover how the skills and experience of board members changed
as a result of the board gender quota (see Ferreira et al., 2021; Ahern and Dittmar, 2012; Bertrand
et al., 2019). In particular, Ferreira et al. (2021) compared the characteristics of the females hired
before and after the quota. The authors (2021) found that female directors hired after the quota
were more independent, experienced, internationally diverse, and no less academically qualified than
those hired before the quota. However, they make no conclusions about how the females hired after
the quota differed from their male predecessors. As such, we contribute to the literature by not only
comparing the skills of the new and incumbent females but also by comparing the new females to their
male predecessors.
2 Data and Methodology
2.1 Data Gathering
We primarily used Refinitiv Eikon to obtain the necessary data for our study. We downloaded a
large dataset containing variables for both the event study and the long-term analysis to ensure that
the same companies participated in both studies. We limited our dataset to public companies to
ensure similar characteristics and also follow the same approach as in Ahern and Dittmar (2012). For
the event study, we used a cross-sectional dataset with company names and corresponding variables’
values on the 20th of January in 2010, the main date of our event study. These variables included
GICS Sector, GICS Industry Group, board size, board gender diversity, market capitalization, and
total assets. Stock returns for a five-day period, two days before until two days after 2010-01-20, were
also acquired. We downloaded these statistics for our treatment group, companies incorporated in
France listed on the Euronext Paris, and for our control group, companies incorporated in the USA
listed on a US exchange.

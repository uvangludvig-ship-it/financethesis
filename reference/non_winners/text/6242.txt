The  Price of Emissions:  Decoding  Green Returns

Abstract:

   This study investigates how carbon emissions affect stock returns among Nasdaq
  OMX  Nordic  120 companies, and how  transparency in emissions reporting  impacts
   this relationship. We find  significant  negative effects  of  scope  1  and  2  emissions on
   stock returns,  controlling for size, book-to-market, and other known stock return
   predictors,  implying  that firms can increase  financial  returns by lowering
   emissions.  For  our  main  cross-sectional regression  sample, we  find no significant
   effects  of  scope  3 emissions,  the  emission  growth  rate  of any  scope, or the emission
   intensity of any  scope  on stock returns.  Furthermore, we find that the negative
   effects of carbon emissions on stock returns seem  to be driven by companies  with
   non-transparent  emissions  reporting, and that a firm can mitigate the negative
   impact  of carbon emissions on stock returns by increasing reporting transparency.
   Our results highlight the need for firms to consider their environmental  impact
   and reporting standards in operating decisions.

Keywords:

   Climate  change,  Carbon emissions,  Emission  reporting,  Stock return,
  Transparency

1  Introduction

1.1  Research Question

The study addresses the questions: “Does climate risk in the form of carbon emissions
affect  stock  returns in the  Nordic  financial  markets?” and “Does transparency in
emission reporting influence the carbon emission-stock return relationship?”

1.2 Background

1.2.1 Carbon Emissions and Stock Returns

The cross-sectional pattern of stock returns, i.e., the variation in stock returns across
different stocks at a particular point in time,  is  a well-studied topic,  where  returns
traditionally  are  linked to  firms’  exposure to risk factors like size and book-to-market
ratios.  An understudied variable in this regard is corporate carbon emissions, and few
studies incorporate carbon emissions as a factor  potentially explaining stock returns
in the cross-section.  Historically, its omission  is justified,  as  there  has been less
concern about  climate risks.  Today, rising global temperatures  and  stricter climate
policies  might give reasons to motivate that  carbon emissions represent a material
risk factor reflected in  asset prices.  This paper, however, shows  varying  results
regarding  carbon emissions significance in predicting stock returns.  When controlling
for  year-month  effects,  only  emissions  related to the  firm’s  core operations, scope 1
and scope 2 emissions,  have  a negative  impact  on  returns, as outlined  in Table  1.

From a theoretical perspective, there are  three  main  hypotheses  explaining how
carbon emissions may be related to stock returns.  First,  the  carbon risk premium
hypothesis suggests that  high-emitting firms face  significant risks  compared to low-
emitting ones (e.g.,  carbon taxes,  potential  environmental litigation,  operational risk
from  demand shift to  “green products”).  These  increased  risks lead  investors  to
demand higher returns  for  holding such  high-emitting  “brown”  firms. Second, the
divestment hypothesis  argues that  high-emitting firms  are like  “sin”  stocks;
intentionally  excluded  by socially  responsible and ethical investors.  This collective
divestment  leads to under-diversification of these assets  which  should result in  higher
future stock returns according to prominent finance theories, e.g., the Capital Asset

                                     3
Pricing Model (CAPM).  Third, the carbon alpha hypothesis  suggests  that the
increasing demand for  environmentally friendly products and  improved access to
capital  for  low-emitting  “green”  firms will lead to higher financial returns.  Firms that
manage their emissions are seen as more  capable of performing operationally,  thus
increasing investor  demand,  and driving up their stock prices.

The  three hypotheses, albeit in some cases contradictory, have received  support from
different academic literature.  This  paper  finds partial evidence for  the  carbon alpha
hypothesis, as  an increase  in  total  scope  1 and  scope  2 emissions  negatively affects
stock returns  for the sample used.  However,  the fact that  we find no  significant
effects for  scope  3  emissions,  emission  growth rates,  or emission intensity  suggests
that one should be cautious before fully supporting any of the hypotheses.

1.2.2 Transparency in Emissions Reporting and Stock Returns

Another important question  in climate finance  is  how  other factors  might influence
the  emission-return relationship.  As previously discussed, increased carbon emissions
induce  risks. These risks  can impact the profitability of companies, especially those
with high carbon footprints.  Emission reporting  serves as  one of the primary sources
of information  for investors  to evaluate a firm’s  exposure to these  risks,  and  might
consequently  impact  the effect carbon emissions have  on stock returns.  On  one hand,
a  high-emitting firm  disclosing its emissions  transparently  might face greater  share
price  punishment  from investors  than one that does not, as transparency makes the
risks more apparent and easier to quantify.  This suggests that thorough emissions
reporting could amplify the impact of carbon emissions on stock returns.  On the
other hand, transparency  can  reduce  perceived  emission  risks  and  signal
accountability,  as  it allows investors and stakeholders to assess a company's
environmental impact more clearly.  Thus, one could argue that transparent reporting
should  mitigate  the effect  of  emissions  on stock returns. This paper supports the
latter view: transparency significantly reduces the negative impact of emissions on
stock returns, highlighting the value of increased transparency in emissions reporting.

This research focuses on  Nordic companies  governed  by  EU regulation, including
Norway as an EEA member.  The EU, and Norway,  recently adopted the Corporate
Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD)  to large and listed companies.  CSRD
mandates  more  detailed ESG reporting,  like the inclusion of  a double materiality

                                     4
matrix and  specific  requirements  around  emission  disclosure.  Compliance  is  phased,
starting with  companies subject to the  previous reporting directive NFRD  for  fiscal
years beginning 2024, with the first  reports due in 2025.  (European Commission,
2024).  As not all companies  will be  affected in 2025,  disparities  still  occur  in the
emission reporting standards for  Nordic companies.  This makes a Nordic sample
suitable to  analyse,  as  one  can  separate  detailed emission reporters  from  less detailed
ones  and use  this  separation to reach scientific results regarding how transparency in
emissions  reporting  might affect the way carbon emissions impact stock returns.

Typically, emissions are  categorized into  three subgroups:  scope 1,  scope 2,  and  scope
3  emissions.  Scope 1 emissions consist of direct emissions incurred during business
operations.  Scope  2 emissions are indirect  emissions  resulting from the firms’
electricity purchases. Lastly,  scope 3 emissions are greenhouse gases stemming from
the  firms’  upstream and downstream supply  chains  (McKinsey & Company, 2024).
Historically,  the  EU’s  Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD)  has  not  required
comprehensive  emission disclosure for  scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions. This  has  left  room
for  corporate  interpretation,  creating  large  disclosure differences.

In this paper, it is important to note and understand the difference between
transparent-  and  low-emitting  companies. A transparent company is a firm that goes
beyond minimum requirements to provide additional information regarding its
environmental practices.  A  low-emitting  company is a firm with low carbon
emissions. This research study finds no material correlation between transparent
companies and the firm’s level of carbon emissions,  as  noted in  Table  8  Appendix  C.

1.2.3 We Don’t Have Time and Open  Sustainability  Index

We Don’t Have Time, the  world’s largest media platform for climate action, and
Open  Sustainability Index  (OSI), an open source, crowd-sourced corporate
sustainability data platform, have together released a special report,  Nordic  Climate
Transparency  Leadership, examining the CSRD readiness of Nasdaq OMX  Nordic
120 (NOMX120) companies  (Open Sustainability Index, 2024). They find that 32.3%
of these companies achieved ‘complete and transparent’  scope  1, 2, and 3 reporting.
Furthermore, they find that a  portfolio of these ‘complete and transparent’
companies  delivered a  5-percentage-point higher return compared to the NOMX120
average (22% vs. 17%) from June 2023 to June 2024. Building on these findings, this

                                     5
paper revisits the NOMX120 firms and explores the relationship between carbon
emissions and stock returns using scientifically robust methods and models, offering
deeper insights into the link between sustainability practices and financial
performance.

1.2.4 Literature Review

The thesis contributes to the developing subject of climate finance and the effects of
climate change on financial markets, and vice versa. A recent study  examines  the
relationship between climate factors and firm performance by analysing if carbon
emissions affect the cross-section of stock returns  (Bolton & Kacperczyk, 2021).
Results  show  that total carbon emissions  significantly affect stock returns;  a  higher
level of carbon emissions is associated with higher returns.  The  results support the
conclusion that investors are demanding financial compensation for their exposure to
climate risk.  Other studies related to the topic of climate change and firm
performance further problematize  the carbon emission-stock return affiliation by
showing that the relationship is only significant when using vendor-estimated carbon
emissions, not when using actual reported emissions  (Aswani, Raghunandan, &
Rajgopal, 2024).

The effects of transparency on stock returns  are  a relatively understudied area.  A
paper  studying  the  concept of transparency and price informativeness  shows  that
stock return synchronicity increases when transparency improves,  i.e.,  that stock
prices are more informative about future events in transparent  environments
(Dasgupta, Gan, & Gao, 2010). Another study  finds that firms with low corporate
transparency are more affected by investor sentiment than firms with high corporate
transparency  (Firth, Wang, & Wong, 2015).  There are,  however,  no prominent
papers studying the  financial  effects of emissions reporting, highlighting the need for
additional research in the area.

Our study contributes to the existing literature in several ways. By analysing the
emission-return relationship in a different sample period (2019-2023) and in another
market (NOMX120) we provide additional evidence  regarding  the  effects  of climate
change on financial markets.  This is important, as  previous studies—Aswani  et al.
(2024)  and  Bolton &  Kacperczyk  (2021), among  others—find  different relationships
between carbon emissions and stock returns.  The  fact that we do not find

                                     6
significance for all  total emission variables and returns  is more in line with the results
provided by  Aswani  et al.  (2024)  than  Bolton  and Kacperczyk  (2021).  As  studies
using different samples and sample periods  have  reached different conclusions
regarding the emission-return relationship, we  want to highlight the need to proceed
with caution before  incorporating  results from  a  single  paper when making  real-life
investment decisions.  Another  important  contribution  of  the paper is the  inclusion of
transparency  in  the analysis.  We  find that  transparency  in emission reporting
moderates the emission-return relationship  by mitigating the negative effect  of
carbon emissions  on stock returns.  This  translates to a  direct  recommendation  for
firms  aiming to  prevent negative effects on stock returns;  increase transparency  when
reporting  emissions.

1.2.5 Methodology

Our analysis  employs  an  ordinary  least  square (OLS)  regression model where
monthly stock returns are regressed against  scope  1-3 emissions data  to  examine
whether  firms’ differences in emission levels result in different returns  in  the cross-
section. To test the transparency hypotheses, we use an  OLS model with two
subsamples—one of transparent companies and  another  of  non-transparent
companies—and  a moderation model  to assess  whether  transparent reporting  affects
the relationship between  carbon emissions and stock  returns.

Furthermore,  the cross-sectional return regression uses control variables  that
previously have been found to affect monthly stock returns in the cross-section,
defined in  Table  4  Appendix B.  Including control variables reduces the risk of
confounding effects influencing the relationship and better avoids omitted variables
bias,  isolating  the impact of carbon emissions on monthly stock returns.  To ensure
further robustness, we  include  industry-fixed effects and year-month fixed effects in
our model.  Including  industry-fixed effects  controls for unobserved heterogeneity
between different sectors that could influence the relationship between emissions and
stock returns, while  year-month fixed effects  account for time-specific factors, such as
macroeconomic conditions or market trends, that might affect all companies similarly
over time.  In  addition, we cluster standard errors at the firm and year level  to
account for potential error correlations within firms and across time periods,
addressing possible  issues of  heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation.

2  Data and Sample

Our  sample, covering 2019-2023, is  constructed  by  combining data from Refinitiv
Eikon and  OSI.  Refinitiv Eikon provides information on stock returns, firm
fundamentals, and corporate carbon  emissions.  OSI  provides additional emission data
for NOMX120 companies.  Furthermore,  OSI  classifies  each  firm as either transparent
or non-transparent, with transparent companies providing  detailed  disclosures  of all
relevant emission categories.  This  allows  for the creation of a transparency dummy
variable used in the report.  Due to differences in reporting standards,  measurement
difficulties and less strict reporting requirements  especially related to  scope  3,  each
regression produces a slightly different  number  of unique observations. Details are to
be found under each specific regression, but all fall within the interval of 4525  and
5829  observations,  except  for  the subsample  and  robustness  regressions.

Furthermore, to mitigate the effect of outliers,  we perform two types of adjustments.
First, we winsorize  variables  at  either  the  0.5% or  2.5% level, as  shown  in  Table  4
Appendix B.  Winsorization  caps  extreme values  at specified percentiles, retaining  the
observations in the dataset  while  preventing  outliers  from  disproportionately
affecting  the overall distribution or  the  regression  results. Second, we log-transform
emissions-, market capitalization-, and property plant and equipment variables.
Naturally, variation is quite high in these variables,  as  NOMX120  consists  of
companies  of different sizes and in different industries.  Log transformation
compresses  the range,  reduces  skewness by  bringing extreme values closer to the
mean,  and  makes  the data more symmetric. This transformation also makes the
variables more suitable for regression analysis by improving the linearity of
relationships and stabilising the variance.  Ultimately, these adjustments ensure  more
robust  statistical findings.

2.1 Data on  Carbon  Emissions

The summary statistics of the  carbon emissions  are reported in  Panel  A  of  Table  4
Appendix B.  Refinitiv Eikon’s  ESG data is carefully extracted and standardised from
publicly available sources (e.g.,  annual reports, NGO websites,  and Corporate Social
Responsibility reports)  (Eikon, 2024).  OSI  follows a similar data-gathering  approach
and  collects emission data from publicly available sources. In addition,  OSI

                                     8
sometimes contact firms directly to obtain more detailed  emissions data not found in
the financial statements. Both data providers follow the Greenhouse Gas Protocol
that sets the standards for measuring corporate emissions.

In terms of  emission levels, the average  scope  3 levels are the highest, followed  by
scope  1  and then  scope  2.  The  large  scope  3 emissions are  expected,  as emissions
from  value chain  activities  often exceed  those  from  core operations.  The average
emission intensity  levels,  measured  as the ratio of emissions to revenue,  are  73.57  for
scope  1 emissions,  18.95  for  scope  2 emissions, and  1218.72  for  scope  3 emissions. The
differences are consistent with the variations in the emission totals for the
scopes.  The  very large  mean  growth  rate  of  scope  3 emissions  is a  consequence of the
ambiguous standards in  scope  3 reporting, and the  fact that  calculation methods
might be  changed  from one year to another reflects the large  average  growth.  Thus,
the  median growth rate  should be  more informative  for  scope  3 emissions.

To  ensure data validity  and to  provide robustness for  results, we also study how the
emission variables are correlated with each other, and the cross-correlations are
reported in  Panel A  Table  5  Appendix B.  As expected in this context,  all  scopes  of
total emissions  are positively correlated  with  each other.  The  same is true for each
scope’s total  emissions and its emissions intensity.  The correlation is not perfect
(around 0.75  for all  scopes), which  displays the possibility that two firms with the
same intensity might have different levels of carbon emissions, and vice versa.  The
autocorrelation coefficients for all  scopes  are also reported in Panel B. For all three
scopes, emission levels are highly consistent. The autocorrelation coefficient is  0.982
for  scope  1, 0.973  for  scope  2, and 0.882  for  scope  3, indicating a somewhat larger
year-on-year change in emissions for  scope  3 emissions.  Notably,  scope  1 and  scope  3
growth  levels  are  significant,  indicating some  consistency in the growth levels of
emissions.  The  autocorrelation coefficient for emissions intensity shows  that  the  ratio
is  persistent for all three  scopes, albeit  less  for  scope  3 emissions.

Furthermore, we examine the carbon emissions over time in  Table  6  Appendix B.
Consistent with  expectations, average emissions decrease over time for  scope  1 and
scope  2 emissions.  Technological advancements in clean energy, regulatory and policy
measures, and  companies’  sustainability investments all contribute to lowering firm
emissions  as time passes.  However,  the  average level of  scope  3 emissions  exhibits  an
upward trend  over time,  with a sudden increase in 2021.  The increase  can  be

                                     9
attributed  to several factors.  First, the sample  size  increases  in 2021,  likely  increasing
average  levels  due to some high-emitting firms  beginning  to report  scope  3 emissions.
Second, 2021 experienced a significant rebound in economic activity after disruptions
caused by the Covid-19 pandemic. Many industries ramped up production to meet
increased demand for goods and services, which may have led to higher emissions.  In
addition,  supply chain  disruptions  in  2021  might have  forced  firms  to  pursue  less
energy-efficient supply chain alternatives, thus increasing emissions. Lastly, as  scope
3 emissions have  the most ambiguous reporting standards  among  the  scopes, another
possibility is that firms switched their reporting standards of  scope  3 emissions
during the sample period, thus explaining the sudden increase.

Table  7  Appendix  B  presents the sample distribution of firms in various industries,
as categorised by the Global Industry Classifications Standard (GICS)  (MSCI, 2024).
Most firms are in the industrial sector, followed by financials and healthcare.  Our
total sample includes 114 firms, not 120 as the name NOMX120 might suggest.
There are three reasons for this. First, some large companies have both their A and
B stocks represented in the index. Second,  for  some firms,  there are no  emissions
data.  Third, for some firms, control variables  are missing  for the  entire  sample
period.

In  Panel B of  Table  7  Appendix B, we  report emission statistics for the different
industries and rank them  by average  emissions levels for each  scope.  As one might
expect, utilities, materials, and energy  rank high for all  scopes  of emissions.  This
occurs because these sectors are inherently more energy-intensive, with utilities and
energy companies directly involved in the production and distribution of energy,
often from fossil fuels. Materials companies typically require  large  amounts of energy
and raw materials, both of which contribute significantly to their emissions.

2.2 Variables in  Cross-Sectional  Return  Regressions

The summary statistics of the monthly stock return and all control variables are
reported in Panel B of Table  4  Appendix B.  The average firm’s monthly stock return
is 1.23%, with a standard deviation of 9.65%.  In addition,  both  the  average-  and
median  beta is 1.00, consistent with that of the  market.

                                     10
The control variables employed are commonly used in scientific studies seeking to
find predictors of stock returns. Beta, measured as a stock’s volatility relative to the
market, is used by  the  CAPM to explain variations in stock returns, where a stock
with a high beta should achieve greater returns to reflect its increased risk
(volatility) compared to the market portfolio  (Sharpe, 1964).  Size is included in the
Fama-French three-factor model, which incorporates firm  size as a determinant of
stock returns, suggesting that smaller firms (by market capitalization) tend to
outperform larger firms  over time.  The same model includes the book-to-market ratio
to predict returns, indicating that value stocks (those with a high B/M ratio)
generally offer higher returns compared to growth stocks (those with a low B/M
ratio)  (Fama & French, 1993).  The Carhart four-factor model extends the Fama-
French model by adding a momentum factor, demonstrating that stocks with strong
recent performance tend to continue performing well in the future  (Carhart, 1997).

Furthermore, research has shown that ROE is a significant indicator of a company's
profitability and can influence stock returns. The DuPont analysis, for instance,
breaks down ROE into components that help assess financial performance  (Hargrave,
2024). Sales growth is often considered a proxy for a company's future earnings
potential, influencing  investor expectations and stock valuations. Earnings per share
growth is also a critical factor in stock valuation. Studies have  shown that companies
with higher growth rates have higher stock returns, as investors use earnings per
share to anticipate future profitability  (Gharaibeh, Saleh, Jawabreh, & Ali, 2022).
Leverage refers to the use of debt to fund operations.  While the use of leverage might
amplify financial returns, it also increases risk. As both financial returns and  risks
affect stock prices, so  could  leverage. Capital expenditures have significant effects on
current cash flows, future earnings,  and asset growth, which in turn affects stock
returns. The same applies  to  a company's level of property,  plant,  and equipment.

2.3 Data in  Transparency  Regressions

The  paper uses the  same variables  for both the  transparency-  and  cross-sectional
return  regressions,  except for the addition of  a  dummy  variable  in the  transparency
regressions,  taking the value of 1 if the company is transparent and 0 if  it is  non-
transparent.  Using the  dummy  variable, we  create two different subsamples of
NOMX120  where we separate transparent and non-transparent companies.  Summary
statistics  are reported in Table  4  Appendix B.

                                     11
Roughly  one-third  of the NOMX120  firms  are classified as transparent  in their
emission reporting. During the 2019-2023 sample period,  the subsample of  non-
transparent companies  seems  to outperform transparent companies in terms of
average monthly stock return.  The mean stock return for a transparent company is
1.08%  compared to 1.32%  for  non-transparent companies.  Median values for  book-to-
market  and  leverage are similar for  the subsamples, but mean values differ
somewhat.  In terms of  market value  (LOGSIZE),  transparent  firms,  on average,  are
larger,  which could be expected. As companies grow, they face more investor scrutiny
and public attention, which in turn drives them to adopt  more rigorous  reporting
standards. Moreover, compliance costs related to reporting tend to be  a  smaller
burden for large companies, as they benefit from economies of scale. Larger firms
often have the resources and infrastructure in place to absorb the fixed costs of
reporting  compliance more efficiently.

3.1  Carbon  Emissions and  Cross-Sectional  Returns

Using  a pooled OLS regression model, we analyse the  connection between companies’
emissions—total  levels, year-on-year growth, and emission intensity—for all three
scopes  with their respective stock returns in the cross-section. The  model  used  is  the
following:

       (1)     RET!,#  =  "$  + "%Emissions!,#  + "&Controls!,#  + $#  + %!,#

RET!,#  is the monthly return of stock  i  at time  t,  Emissions!,#  describes  either  the
total  level, growth, or intensity for one of the three  scopes  for firm  i  at time  t.  The
Controls!,#  term  captures all  the control variables  as described in Table  4  Appendix
B.  The coefficient of interest is  "%. Results  for  the  coefficients  of interest  are  reported
in Table  1  where  columns 4-6  for all  panels  include industry-fixed effects in the
regression.  Industry-fixed effects account  for unobserved  industry-specific
characteristics  and  address  the clustering of emissions within certain industries,
preventing distortion of results.  The complete regression outputs from Tables 1 to 3,
including control variables, are presented in Tables 9 to 11 in Appendix C.

In Panel A, we find negative, statistically significant results for  scope  1 and  scope  2
emissions. A one percent increase in  scope  1 leads to a 0.081  percent decrease in
monthly stock return.  A one percent increase in  scope  2 leads to a 0.104  percent
decrease in monthly stock return.  We find no significant results for the effect of  scope
3 emissions on stock returns. Interestingly, the  scope  1 coefficient increases in
magnitude when we control for  industry-fixed  effects, making the results even more
economically relevant. The  scope  2 significance disappears when the industry variable
is included, highlighting the possibility that industry-specific factors were
confounding  the relationship between monthly stock returns and emissions in the
previous regression.  In Panel B and C, for  growth levels of emissions and emissions
intensity, we find no evidence that indicates the existence of a relationship between
these measures and monthly stock returns.

For firms, the negative impact of high  scope  1 and  scope  2 emissions on stock returns
signals a  market incentive to reduce emissions. A higher stock price allows companies

like renewable  energy adoption. This could help  decrease  the negative market

3.2  Transparency  Regressions

The first step of our transparency analysis  involves testing  the  point-biserial
correlation between the transparency variable and the levels, growth rates, and
intensities of  all  scopes.  The purpose is to provide robustness to our  results,  ensuring
that  results  in the transparency regressions  are  not driven by emission-related
differences  between transparent and non-transparent firms.  A high correlation  could
signal that results in the transparency regressions  occur because  transparent
companies have higher  or lower emission levels, growth rates, or intensities than non-
transparent companies. As illustrated in  Table  8  Appendix B, the correlation
coefficients are very low. Intuitively,  the results suggest  that transparency does not
reflect  either  higher or lower emission  levels, growth  rates, or  intensities.  Thus, we
can confidently analyse the independent effect of transparency on  the carbon
emission-stock return  relationship.

Next, we split our main sample into two subsamples—one transparent and non-
transparent sample—and perform  the same pooled OLS regression  (Equation  (1))  to
examine  the effects of carbon emissions on monthly stock returns for the two
subsamples.  This enables us to isolate the distinct effect of emissions on returns
within each subsample and determine whether the emission-return relationship  varies
depending on the transparency of the companies' emissions reporting.

reduce investor scepticism and mitigate the negative impact of high emissions on
stock returns.

3.3  Additional  Robustness

In previous regressions, we  applied  various  measures—such  as  control factors,
clustered standard errors,  and  correlation analysis—to  ensure  robust  results and
reduce  the possibility of confounding factors  affecting our conclusions.  To further
reinforce our findings, we conduct an additional robustness test  to  strengthen
arguments made in the paper.  The  NOMX120  sample  includes  firms  from  high-
emitting industries.  As  these  firms  might face more  investor scrutiny  regarding their
environmental impact, it  raises  concerns that the  observed relationship  between
carbon  emissions  and stock returns is  driven  solely by these industries.  To address
this,  we exclude the  firms from  the  three  highest  emitting  industries  for each scope as
provided in Table  7  Appendix B  and  repeat  the  cross-sectional  and transparency
regressions.  Results  of the  coefficients  of interest  are provided in Tables  12 to  14  in
Appendix D.  The exclusion of the high-emitting  industries does not affect  any  of  our
results  in a  considerable way.  In fact,  the exclusion, if anything,  seems to  strengthen
the negative relationship between  carbon emissions and stock returns for scopes 1
and 2.

                                     21
4 Conclusion

In this article, we find varying evidence for the effect of different  scopes  of emissions
on stock returns.  In both the cross-sectional-  and the transparency regressions,  we
find  support for a  significant  emission-return relationship for  total  scope  1 and  scope
2 emissions.  Intuitively, this makes sense for two primary reasons.  First, investors
utilising emission data  when making  investment decisions  are  more likely to consider
total emissions data than  emission  growth or  emission  intensity data. Total  emissions
better  represent  a firm’s contribution to climate change and the  associated  risks,  and
they  are  reported to a much greater extent  than  growth  rates and intensities.  Second,
scope  1 and  scope  2  emissions  should  be  more  indicative  of a  firm’s  direct exposure to
climate change  than  scope  3, as they are better tied to the firm’s core operations and
more reliable due to  fewer  measurement difficulties.  Thus, insignificant results  for
scope  3, emission growth, and emission intensities might be  reasonable.

Moreover, the  variability in results across different studies  underscores  the need for
future  research in the field.  Our  findings  partly  support the carbon alpha hypothesis,
suggesting  that firms with lower emissions should outperform high-emitting
companies in the stock market. However, as  results  vary, and  some  lose significance
when  including industry-fixed effects,  caution is advised  before  incorporating results
solely based on findings in one article when  implementing the results  in  real-world
decisions.  Given the novelty of climate finance, all new insights, contradictory to
previous research or not, are relevant, and future studies examining the relationship
using different samples,  sample periods, and methodologies are highly encouraged.

A significant  contribution  of our article is the moderating effect of transparent
emissions reporting on the carbon emission-stock return relationship.  Transparent
emission reporting  mitigates  the negative effect carbon emissions have on stock
returns. This creates a clear incentive for firms, especially high-emitting ones, to
adopt better and more clear reporting standards to alleviate the unfavourable impact
of emissions on  returns.  Our study can thus be used as a valuable framework for
policymakers, regulators, and businesses  to recognize the importance of transparency
in emissions reporting. By improving reporting standards, firms can  increase share
prices,  attract investment,  and better align with growing sustainability expectations,
ultimately benefiting both their financial performance and broader societal goals.

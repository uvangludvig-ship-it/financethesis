The MAX Effect and Firm-Specific Attention: How Reversals Vary with Investor
Focus

Abstract:

   We examine the MAX effect, defined as the negative relation between extreme daily
   returns and subsequent stock performance, in Swedish equities from 2011 to 2024.
   We document a robust MAX effect in both portfolio sorts and firm-level return
   predictability. Using Google search volume as a proxy for firm-specific investor
   attention, we show that the effect varies across attention levels: MAX-related
   reversals arise when extreme returns coincide with abnormally low or moderate
   attention but disappear entirely when attention is abnormally high. Although firm-
   level regressions confirm that MAX predicts next-month returns, they do not detect
   systematic moderation by abnormal attention at the one-month horizon. Taken
   together, the results indicate that firm-specific attention is an important variable
   associated with whether reversals occur after salient price jumps, highlighting the
    broader role of attention in behaviorally driven return anomalies.

Keywords:

  MAX Effect, Google Trends, Attention, Reversals, Behavioral Finance

                      I. Introduction

Large single-day stock returns are among the most attention-grabbing events in fi-
nancial markets. When a stock experiences an extreme daily price surge, it tends to
drive media coverage and trigger online search activity. Although such dramatic re-
turns may create the impression of rare, lottery-like opportunities, a growing body of
research shows that these surges are systematically followed by lower future returns.
This pattern is known as the MAX effect.
  MAX, defined as the largest daily return of a stock within a month, has been
shown to predict lower risk-adjusted returns in the following month. The effect is
linked to investors’ preference for lottery-like payoffs, where salient return spikes
draw disproportionate attention and inflate prices before they revert toward funda-
mentals (Bali, Cakici, and Whitelaw, 2011).
   The fact that the MAX effect varies in strength across markets and periods sug-
gests that investors do not respond to salient price jumps in a uniform way. Prior
research attributes this variation to market-wide sentiment (Mojtahedi, Mashhadi,
and Savin, 2025), differences in investor composition (Aboulamer and Kryzanowski,
2016), or whether an extreme return coincides with informational events such as
earnings announcements (Baars and Mohrschladt, 2021). These explanations speak
to who is trading and what information is available, but they do not capture a the-
oretically important determinant of how investors react to salient price movements:
the degree of attention directed at the specific firm experiencing the jump.
   Firm-specific attention reflects how intensely investors focus on a stock when
an extreme return occurs, shaping the speed at which price distortions may correct
(Peng and Xiong, 2006; Merton, 1987). Despite its theoretical importance, this di-
mension has not been examined in prior MAX research. Evidence on the MAX effect
in Sweden is also limited. Mojtahedi et al. (2025) document the effect in equally
weighted portfolios from 1994 to 2019 but find no significant firm-level relationship,
prompting an updated analysis.
   We address this gap by providing updated evidence on the MAX effect in Swe-
den from 2011 to 2024 and examining whether firm-specific attention conditions the
strength of MAX-related reversals. These objectives motivate two research ques-
tions:

   1. Is the MAX effect present in Sweden between 2011 and 2024?

   2. Does the MAX effect vary with firm-specific investor attention?

   Literature on attention provides several reasons why behaviorally driven anoma-
lies such as the MAX effect may vary across attention levels. Attention can signal
heightened speculative interest, which increases buying pressure and contributes to
temporary price distortions (Da, Engelberg, and Gao, 2011). Attention can also re-
flect greater investor recognition or monitoring, which accelerates information incor-
poration and reduces the persistence of distortions (Merton, 1987; Fang and Peress,
2009). Limited-attention models such as Peng and Xiong (2006) also emphasize that
the speed of mispricing correction depends on how much attention investors devote

                                    3
to processing information. Together, these frameworks suggest that attention influ-
ences both the magnitude and timing of the reversals that are central to the MAX
effect, motivating our focus on firm-level attention as a conditioning variable.
   To investigate our research questions, we construct a dataset of all firms listed
on Nasdaq Stockholm from 2011 to 2024, combining daily return data, firm char-
acteristics, and Google Trends search histories. At the portfolio level, we exam-
ine returns on MAX-sorted portfolios and on portfolios formed from double sorts
of abnormal attention and MAX, evaluating their risk-adjusted performance us-
ing Fama–French Carhart four-factor regressions. At the firm level, we estimate
monthly Fama–MacBeth cross-sectional regressions to test whether MAX predicts
next-month returns and whether this predictive relation varies with firm-specific
abnormal attention, proxied by standardized Google search volume.

extent reversals follow extreme daily gains, our findings contribute to understanding
why empirical evidence on the MAX effect varies across markets, time periods, and
portfolio constructions. This evidence complements prior research on the MAX effect
by identifying attention as a key conditioning factor and provides updated evidence
for the Swedish equity market. More broadly, our results highlight that attention
serves as a useful conditioning variable when evaluating behavioral anomalies.

theory and literature. Section III describes the data and variable construction.
Section IV outlines the empirical methodology and presents the results. Section V
reports robustness tests. Section VI concludes.

         II. Theoretical Framework and Literature

A. Asset Pricing Foundations
   Understanding return patterns following extreme price movements requires a
framework for measuring abnormal performance. Under standard asset pricing the-
ory, financial markets are informationally efficient, meaning that prices fully reflect
available information and predictable return patterns should not persist after con-
trolling for risk (Sharpe, 1964). The Capital Asset Pricing Model formalizes this by
relating expected returns to systematic market risk. The Fama–French three-factor
model extends this framework by incorporating size and value factors (Fama and
French, 1992), while Carhart (1997) adds momentum. These models provide the
benchmark for assessing whether stocks experiencing extreme daily returns earn ab-
normal subsequent returns. If extreme returns predict lower risk-adjusted returns
after controlling for these factors, it is typically interpreted as evidence of behavioral
forces rather than risk compensation.
   Behavioral finance offers such explanations by showing how psychological bi-
ases create temporary mispricings. Cumulative prospect theory demonstrates that
investors overweight small probabilities of extreme gains (Tversky and Kahneman,
1992), making rare lottery-like payoffs appear overly attractive. Barberis and Huang
(2008) apply this insight to asset pricing, showing that preference for positively
skewed return distributions can drive investors to overpay for stocks with lottery-like
characteristics. Salience theory (Bordalo, Gennaioli, and Shleifer, 2012) reinforces
this mechanism by demonstrating that vivid attention-grabbing signals distort be-
liefs and prompt overreaction. Together, these frameworks suggest that extreme
daily returns, being both salient and suggestive of lottery payoffs, may temporarily
inflate prices before subsequent correction.

                                    5
B. The MAX Effect: Evidence and Variation
   The MAX effect is well-established empirically. Bali et al. (2011) show that
stocks with the highest maximum daily return in a month (MAX ) earn significantly
lower risk-adjusted returns in the following month across US equities from 1962 to
2005. This pattern appears in multiple international markets, including Australia
(Fong and Toh, 2014) and Europe (Walksh¨ausl, 2014). However, evidence for the
MAX effect in Sweden is scarce, limited to Mojtahedi et al. (2025), who document
a significant MAX effect at the equally weighted portfolio level for the period 1994
to 2019.
   Beyond its pervasive presence, the magnitude of the MAX effect varies across
contexts. Research examining variation in the strength of the MAX effect highlights
conditioning factors at multiple levels. At the market level, Mojtahedi et al. (2025)
show the effect in Sweden strengthens when aggregate sentiment is low. At the
firm level, Aboulamer and Kryzanowski (2016) demonstrate that the MAX effect
weakens with higher institutional ownership, while Baars and Mohrschladt (2021)
show that MAX-related reversals weaken substantially when extreme returns coin-
cide with earnings announcements. These findings establish that the MAX effect is
conditional, with market-wide sentiment, investor composition, and informational
events all identified as moderators.

C. Investor Attention and Mispricing Persistence
   Theoretical models establish that investor attention determines when mispric-
ings persist. Limited attention frameworks emphasize that investors cannot con-
tinuously monitor all stocks, causing gradual information incorporation (Peng and
Xiong, 2006). Merton (1987) demonstrates that stocks with limited investor recog-
nition exhibit slower price adjustment regardless of available information. These
models predict that both the magnitude and timing of mispricing correction depend
systematically on attention levels.
   Empirical evidence supports this view. Fang and Peress (2009) show that stocks
receiving more media coverage incorporate information more quickly. Da et al.
(2011) introduce Google search volume as a direct measure of investor attention and
find that attention spikes precede short-run price increases and longer-run reversals,
consistent with attention-induced trading pressure (Barber and Odean, 2008). These
frameworks collectively imply that attention influences the timing of price correction,
which is central to understanding variation in MAX-related reversals.
   These empirical findings point to two theoretical mechanisms through which
attention may influence the persistence of mispricing. Under an information hy-
pothesis, elevated attention reflects monitoring and news processing that facilitate
faster correction, implying that reversals should be stronger when attention is low.
Under a price-pressure hypothesis, elevated attention reflects speculative demand
that temporarily pushes prices away from fundamentals. In this case, mispricing
may not correct immediately but instead unwind gradually.

                                    6
D. Research Gap and Contribution
   Prior research shows that the MAX effect depends on market-wide sentiment, in-
vestor composition, and firm-level informational events. Yet no study has explored
how the effect varies with a theoretically foundational determinant of behavioral
anomaly persistence: investor attention. We address this gap by employing a di-
rect firm-specific attention measure based on Google search volume, capturing the
demand for information about individual stocks experiencing extreme returns.
   Our attention measure captures overall investor focus without distinguishing
between underlying sources, such as news processing, monitoring, or speculative in-
terest. We can therefore document how the MAX effect varies across attention levels
but cannot identify which mechanisms drive this variation. We interpret our findings
as associational evidence showing that attention conditions the strength and timing
of MAX-related reversals. This helps explain why empirical evidence on the MAX
effect varies across markets, time periods, and portfolio constructions, and highlights
the broader role of attention in conditioning behaviorally driven anomalies.

                         III. Data

   Our dataset includes all stocks listed on Nasdaq Stockholm between January
2011 and December 2024. Daily stock prices, trading volume, and accounting data
are obtained from the SHOF FinBas database. Market returns are retrieved from
Nasdaq’s OMXSCAPGI total return index, and the Riksbank’s repo rate serves as
the risk-free rate. Values reported in foreign currencies are converted to SEK using
daily exchange rates from the Riksbank. To ensure reliable estimation of monthly
variables, we exclude firms with fewer than 24 months containing at least 10 valid
daily price observations. Survivorship bias is mitigated by including all stocks ever
listed during the sample period, including new listings, relistings, and delistings.
The Swedish Fama–French Carhart equity factors used in the asset pricing analyses,
namely size (SMB ), value (HML), and momentum (MOM ), are obtained from the
AQR data library and matched to our sample at a monthly frequency. The market
factor (MKT ) is constructed from daily returns of the OMXSCAPGI total return
index and the Riksbank’s repo rate.
    Daily closing prices, adjusted for corporate actions including stock splits, rights
issues, and dividends, are used to compute daily stock returns. From these returns,
we construct our main predictor, MAX, defined following Bali et al. (2011) as
the largest single-day return for each stock in each month. Firm characteristics
follow established definitions in the empirical asset-pricing literature. Firm size
(SIZE ) is the natural logarithm of market capitalization in SEK. Book-to-market
(BM ) is measured as book equity divided by market capitalization, with book equity
lagged six months to account for reporting delays, consistent with Fama and French
(1992). BM ratios above 10 are excluded to remove unrealistic valuation outliers.
Momentum (RET12 2 ) is the cumulative return over months t−12 to t−2, following
Jegadeesh and Titman (1993), while short-term reversal (REV ) is the return in
month t − 1, following Jegadeesh (1990). Illiquidity (ILLIQ) is the Amihud (2002)
ratio, computed as the monthly average of the absolute daily return divided by
daily trading volume. Market beta (BETA) is estimated each month from a rolling

                                    7
regression of daily excess stock returns on daily excess market returns, requiring at
least 15 daily observations. Idiosyncratic volatility (IVOL) is the standard deviation
of the rolling regression’s residuals. To limit the influence of extreme observations,
we winsorize all variables at the 1st and 99th percentiles. After applying these
data-cleaning steps, we obtain our full sample.
   Investor attention is measured using monthly Google Search Volume Index (GSVI),
manually collected from Google Trends for each firm from January 2010 to Decem-
ber 2024, with searches restricted to Sweden. Search terms are based on cleaned
company names, with non-distinctive suffixes such as “AB” removed. Ticker-based
searches were considered but yielded insufficient data. Because GSVI values are nor-
malized relative to each firm’s own search history over the whole sample period, we
follow Da et al. (2011) in constructing a standardized abnormal attention variable
(ATT ), ensuring comparability across firms. We define it as:

    This measure captures how unusual a firm’s search activity is in month t rel-

value indicates unusually low attention.
   Proxying attention by GSVI introduces multiple measurement challenges: First,
because search activity is sparse for many small firms, we require at least 15 monthly
non-zero GSVI observations to ensure a reliable attention measure. Our GSVI fil-
ter applies only to analyses that include attention measures and is referred to as
the GSVI-filtered sample. Baseline tests of the MAX effect use the full sample to
establish the effect’s presence before examining conditioning factors. Second, be-
cause the filter may unevenly affect different stock types and risk making the sam-
ples non-comparable, we compare variable characteristics, correlations, and main
results across both samples. Third, GSVI is inherently noisy and does not guaran-
tee to solely capture financial attention. This concern is especially pronounced for
consumer-facing brands, since their search histories may reflect general web traffic
rather than investor interest. For instance, higher search activity for SAS does not
necessarily indicate greater financial attention, as searches may relate to airline tick-
ets instead of its stock. To address this issue, we run robustness tests in Section V
by excluding consumer-facing firms and randomizing search histories.
   We merge daily price data, accounting data, Google Trends series, and factor
returns into a monthly panel. After all filters, the full sample contains 64,725 firm-
months and 543 unique firms, while the GSVI-filtered sample contains 47,965 firm-
months and 394 unique firms.

capture distinct cross-sectional information and that multicollinearity is unlikely to
affect subsequent empirical tests.
    Although these correlations strengthen the reliability of our variables, one chal-
lenge is the contemporaneous measurement of MAX and ATT within the same
month, which limits our ability to establish whether ATT is an independent condi-
tioning factor or simply an endogenous response to extreme returns. Several features
of our data help to limit this concern: First, the correlation between MAX and ATT
is modest at 0.14 (see Table II), indicating that the two variables capture largely
distinct sources of variation. If attention simply responded to MAX events, we
would expect a much stronger correlation. Second, our standardization approach
isolates unusual monthly search spikes relative to each firm’s own historical baseline,
which helps separate temporary attention changes from persistent differences in firm
visibility. Third, including comprehensive firm-level controls reduces the likelihood
that our attention measure proxies for other known return predictors. However,
important limitations remain. Our attention measure cannot distinguish whether
search activity reflects investor interest per se or differences in investor composition,
for instance, firms with high retail ownership may exhibit more search activity re-
gardless of returns. Moreover, monthly data aggregation prevents us from observing
the precise timing of attention changes relative to MAX events within each month,
hindering definitive statements about causal direction.
   Taken together, the dataset is extensive, economically representative, and well

suited for studying the MAX effect and its variation with investor attention. We rely
on widely used data sources and standard variable definitions, and we address key
data challenges by filtering firms with insufficient observations, winsorizing extreme
values, and incorporating standard control variables in our empirical specifications.
These steps ensure that the subsequent analysis rests on a reliable and stable dataset.

                  IV. Empirical Analysis

   We examine the presence of the MAX effect in Sweden and whether firm-specific
attention conditions this effect in four main stages: First, in Section IV.A.1, we
run portfolio-level tests to evaluate the MAX effect by sorting stocks each month
into decile portfolios based on prior month MAX and estimating their abnormal
performance using the Fama–French Carhart four-factor model. Second, in Section
IV.A.2, we turn to firm-level evidence using monthly Fama–MacBeth regressions,
where next month returns are predicted by MAX and standard firm characteris-
tics. Third, in Section IV.B.1, we examine whether firm-specific abnormal investor
attention conditions the anomaly by double sorting stocks into ATT quintiles and
MAX deciles. Within each ATT quintile, we form long–short MAX portfolios and
estimate Fama–French Carhart alphas. Finally, in Section IV.B.2, we extend the
firm-level Fama–MacBeth model by including abnormal attention and the interac-
tion between ATT and MAX. All significance tests employ two-tailed tests, with
significance at the 5% level requiring |t| > 1.96.

A. The MAX Effect in Sweden
   This section concerns the first two stages, exploring the MAX effect in Sweden
at the portfolio and firm level. The findings from both approaches provide support
for the MAX effect in Sweden during the sample period 2011 to 2024, with statis-
tically significant results in the equally weighted portfolio analysis as well as in the
firm-level regression structure.

on their MAX of the previous month and sorted into ten equally sized decile port-
folios. These portfolios are rebalanced monthly so that portfolio 1 always contains
the stocks with the lowest MAX values in the previous month, while portfolio 10
always contains those with the highest.

B. The MAX Effect Across Attention States
   With evidence of a the MAX effect in Swedish equities, this section examines
whether the effect varies with firm-specific abnormal attention. In the final two
stages of the four-stage approach, we first run four-factor regressions on double-
sorted portfolios, then estimate a firm-level predictive model adding ATT and its
interaction with MAX (ATT×MAX ) to Equation (2). The portfolio-level results
show a consistent pattern: the MAX effect appears in low and moderate attention
states but is absent in high-attention states. At the firm level, the interaction term
is positive but statistically insignificant, providing no direct evidence of moderation
in cross-sectional regressions. To explore whether this insignificance reflects delayed
rather than eliminated reversals, we additionally examine the MAX×ATT interac-
tion over extended return horizons, offering a direction for future research on the
timing of attention-related corrections.

month, while Q5 always contains those with the highest. Within each ATT quin-

decile portfolios, from portfolio 1 (lowest MAX ) to portfolio 10 (highest MAX ).

                    V. Robustness

   The empirical results demonstrate that the MAX effect in Sweden is present,
yet absent in higher attention states. To ensure that these findings are not driven
by specific measurement choices, we perform a series of robustness tests. These
address three practical concerns: First, MAX may be overly influenced by a single
extreme daily return, so we evaluate a broader MAX definition. Second, periods of
unusual market conditions may distort our results, so we re-estimate our specifica-
tions excluding the COVID-19 shock. Third, since Google Trends data may contain
noise or reflect non-financial interest, we validate the attention measure using both
randomization and targeted sample adjustments.

A. Redefining MAX
   Following Bali et al. (2011), we redefine our MAX variable to the average of the
five highest daily returns in a month (MAX5 ) to reduce the influence of one day’s
extreme movement and capture broader tail behavior within the month.

B. Excluding COVID Period (2020)
    Next, we test whether our results are robust to excluding periods characterized by
extreme market uncertainty. We re-estimate our specifications from Section IV.B.1
using the same GSVI-filtered sample but exclude observations from 2020, since these
coincide with the spike in average MAX documented in Figure A.2. This allows us
to verify that the strong jump in average MAX during the pandemic shock does
not disproportionately drive our findings and that the relation between MAX and
future returns continues to hold in more typical market environments.

C. Attention Measure Validation
   Finally, to verify that our attention measure captures investor attention rather
than unrelated search behavior, we conduct three additional validation tests. First,
we increase the minimum Google Trends data requirement from 15 to 100 observa-
tions to ensure that our findings are not driven by sparse search activity. Second, we
implement a Monte Carlo randomization procedure to assess whether our measure
reflects economically meaningful variation rather than patterns arising by chance.
In each of one hundred iterations, firms are randomly assigned another firm’s GSVI
history from a similar period, preserving the temporal structure of search data while
breaking the link to the underlying firm. This procedure generates a distribution of
placebo estimates against which we benchmark our true results. Third, we exclude
firms whose search histories are prone to capturing general web traffic rather than
financial attention, such as firms whose retail websites share the same name as the
company.

                     VI. Conclusion

   This paper examines the MAX effect in Swedish equities from 2011 to 2024 and
whether it varies with the level of firm-specific attention. We combine portfolio-
level tests with firm-level Fama–MacBeth regressions and proxy attention using
standardized Google Search Volume Index.
   We document two main findings. First, the MAX effect is present in Sweden
during our sample period. Stocks with higher prior-month MAX earn lower risk-
adjusted returns in equally weighted portfolios, and MAX significantly predicts lower
subsequent stock returns. This firm-level evidence complements prior Swedish re-
search, which found directionally similar but statistically insignificant results for
earlier periods.
   Second, the MAX effect varies systematically with firm-specific attention at the
portfolio level. When we double-sort stocks on abnormal attention and MAX, the
long–short return spread appears in low and moderate attention quintiles but is
absent in high-attention states. This pattern clarifies why our unconditional tests
show a MAX effect only in equally weighted portfolios. The weak unconditional
value-weighted result does not reflect the absence of the effect among larger firms,
but rather dilution from pooling heterogeneous attention states. At the firm level,
however, the MAX-attention interaction does not significantly predict next-month
returns. This divergence may reflect methodological differences, as portfolio sorts
condition on discrete attention states while regressions impose linear interactions,
or temporal dynamics where attention influences reversal timing across multiple
months. Our contemporaneous measurement and lack of control variables for po-
tential confounding factors prevent distinguishing between these explanations.
   Our study has several limitations that restrict causal interpretation. Because
MAX and attention are measured in the same month, we cannot determine whether
attention moderates reversals or whether both variables respond to common factors
such as firm-specific news or investor composition. The modest correlation between
the two variables rules out a purely mechanical link but does not establish causal

                                   31
direction. Additionally, Google search data are noisy and may capture non-financial
interest, although robustness tests excluding consumer-facing firms and randomizing
search histories support their interpretation as investor attention. Monthly aggre-
gation prevents us from observing the timing of attention changes relative to MAX
events, and our measure cannot distinguish between different sources of attention,
such as news processing, speculative trading, or monitoring. These limitations mean
we document when the MAX effect varies with attention, rather than why it does
so or through which mechanism attention influences reversals.
   For investors, our results highlight that MAX-related reversals occur in low- and
moderate-attention periods with substantial abnormal returns. However, practical
implementation poses significant challenges. The strategy requires monthly rebal-
ancing across numerous positions in relatively illiquid small stocks, likely causing
transaction costs and price impact to erode returns significantly, particularly at in-
stitutional scale. Our findings thus document a persistent behavioral pattern rather
than providing a ready-made investment strategy.
   For researchers, our evidence demonstrates the importance of conditioning be-
havioral anomalies on firm-specific attention. Attention-based heterogeneity helps
explain varying empirical evidence on the MAX effect across markets, sample peri-
ods, and portfolio constructions, including why value-weighted tests show weaker re-
sults than equally weighted ones. Future research could employ higher-frequency at-
tention data to examine intra-month dynamics and incorporate direct news controls
to better distinguish attention-based from information-based mechanisms. While
our one-month firm-level interaction is insignificant, we find the MAX-attention in-
teraction is significantly negative over 2- to 12-month cumulative returns, suggesting
attention may influence reversal timing and persistence. Establishing causality and
identifying mechanisms will require higher-frequency data, non-contemporaneous
measurement, and direct controls for news flow and ownership composition.

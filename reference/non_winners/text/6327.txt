                           Abstract
Deep learning models have recently emerged as a promising approach to finan-
cial distress prediction. In particular, the CatBoost model—a gradient boosting
algorithm optimized for categorical data—has shown promising results. This re-
port evaluates the effectiveness of CatBoost in predicting active S&P credit rat-
ings using financial data for public and private companies. SHAP (SHapley Ad-
ditive exPlanations) is used to improve model interpretability and analyze fea-
ture importance, and SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique)
is explored to address class imbalance. Results show that the model performs
well, with most misclassifications occurring within one or two adjacent rating
categories. Key predictors include categorical variables like sector and country,
as well as financial ratios like net margin, return on equity, interest coverage
ratio and debt-to-assets. Additionally, these findings offer some insight into the
otherwise proprietary credit rating methodologies employed by rating agencies.

Keywords: S&P Credit Rating, Distress Prediction, Financial Ratios, Deep
Learning, CatBoost, Feature Importance, SMOTE, SHAP.

1 Introduction

Modeling credit risk and financial distress prediction has long been areas of inter-
est for financial institutions, investors, and rating agencies alike. Financial distress
is defined as a situation where cash flow is insufficient to cover current obligations
(Wruck 1990). This can result from a combination of economic, financial, legal, and
managerial factors, making credit risk evaluation a complicated task. In the seminal
paper by Altman (1968) a discriminant model is developed using financial indicators
as predictors of distress, but the increasing complexity of financial markets and the
growing availability of data necessitates a new modeling approach.

Credit rating agencies like S&P, Moody’s, and Fitch assign credit ratings to large cor-
porations, financial institutions, and sovereign governments. These ratings are widely
regarded as reliable indicators of default risk, guiding investors on financial stabil-
ity and influencing interest rates on debt financing. Predicting financial distress is,
therefore, a heavily researched topic among financial institutions and investors (Tang
2009). Both quantitative and qualitative methods are used for bankruptcy prediction,
but the methodologies employed by credit rating agencies are often proprietary. This
creates information asymmetry between firms and creditors which further complicates
the assessment process, often leading to inefficiencies in financial distress prediction
(Huynh et al. 2020).

Recent studies suggest that deep learning models offer a promising approach to
bankruptcy prediction by addressing the limitations of traditional models. In par-
ticular, a class of tree-based ensemble models referred to as gradient boosting models
have gained attention in financial distress prediction and credit scoring, as it is an ef-
fective machine learning technique that introduces non-linear interactions, eliminates
multicollinearity and provides high predictive accuracy (Jabeur et al. 2021). These
frameworks enhance the reliability of business failure prediction, making them valu-
able tools for improving transparency of credit ratings.

This paper aims to validate and extend the findings of previous studies on gradient
boosting algorithms for financial distress prediction by applying CatBoost to predict
active S&P credit ratings using relevant financial metrics from a dataset of public
and private companies. Despite many gradient boosting models showing promising
results, Jabeur et al. (2021) finds that CatBoost in particular exhibits the best perfor-
mance compared to other models, including discriminant analysis, logistic regression,
support vector machine, neural networks, random forest, gradient boosting, deep neu-
ral networks and XGBoost. This motivates the selection of the CatBoost model.

Motivated by the promising results of CatBoost for credit rating prediction in pre-
vious studies, this report aspires to evaluate the model robustness and usefulness by
performing multiclass classification on active S&P credit ratings. Furthermore, the
paper aspires to evaluate the effect of individual financial metrics on credit ratings,
and by proxy default prediction, by utilizing the feature importance functionality.
Finally, a complementary purpose is to validate the S&P credit rating process itself
to see whether a clear distinction can be made based on the financial properties of
companies with different credit ratings.

                                    1
As an initial approach, a CatBoost multiclass classification model is developed to pre-
dict credit ratings based on financial metrics, using a dataset comprising both private
and public companies. A feature importance analysis is conducted to identify the key
financial ratios affecting credit ratings and their relevance to financial distress predic-
tion. To address class imbalance, the Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique
(SMOTE) is applied. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) are used to evaluate
the model and provide deeper insights into the prediction process. Therefore, the
following research questions are proposed:

   1. Is the CatBoost model able to effectively predict current S&P credit ratings
      with high accuracy based on underlying financial metrics?

   2. Which financial metrics have the highest predictive value for classification of
      firms into different S&P credit rating groups?

2 Literature Review

Previous studies on financial distress prediction span from Altman’s seminal Z-score
model to more recent work by Jabeur et al. (2021), who compared the performance of
eight machine learning models for bankruptcy classification. To maintain coherency,
the literature review covers core concepts such as credit rating agencies and financial
ratios, followed by a discussion on the application of deep learning models.

2.1 Credit Ratings
Standard & Poors (2022) define credit ratings as independent assessments of an is-
suer’s ability and willingness to meet its financial obligations, in full and on time.
They provide investors and financial institutions with insights into the creditworthi-
ness of corporations, governments, and financial instruments. By offering a standard-
ized measure of credit risk, credit ratings help facilitate capital market transactions,
enabling entities to raise funds through debt issuance while allowing investors to as-
sess risk before purchasing bonds or other fixed-income securities. Unlike investment
recommendations, credit ratings strictly measure credit quality and do not guarantee
an issuer’s financial stability.

Credit rating agencies operate under either an issuer-paid model, where issuers pay
for their ratings, or a subscription model, where investors pay for access (Standard
& Poors 2022). As a result, credit ratings are typically obtained by well-established
companies, where the potential reduction in cost of debt outweighs the expense of
acquiring a rating.

Credit ratings are typically expressed as letter grades, for example from ‘AAA’ to
‘D’ to communicate the rating institute’s opinion of the relative level of credit risk.
These differ slightly across rating institutes, but follow the same pattern from prime
debt to default grade. Bonds issued by governments, banks or companies with high
credit rating are referred to as investment grade, while debt with lower credit rating
is referred to as speculative grade or high yield, often issued by emerging market gov-
ernments or corporations. In the following figure, credit ratings from the three most
prominent credit rating institutes are shown.

Each rating agency applies its own proprietary methodology to assess creditworthi-
ness. However, the general approach—outlined in the official ’Guide to Credit Rating
Essentials’ by Standard & Poors (2022)—can be summarized as follows.

   1. Request and Initial Evaluation
      Issuers request a rating, and the agency conducts a preliminary assessment.

   2. Information Gathering
      Review of financial statements, industry conditions, and macroeconomic factors.

   3. Issuer Meetings
      Analysts meet issuer’s management to discuss financial & strategic aspects.

   4. Analysis and Committee Review
      A rating committee reviews the findings and votes on the final rating.

   5. Publication and Surveillance
      The determined credit rating is published, while agencies continue monitoring
      for potential adjustments.

rating category. This provides clear insight into the relationship between the credit
risk and default rates for investment-grade and speculative-grade issuers of bonds or

2.2 Financial Models
Modeling corporate failure is essential to provide investors with an accurate represen-
tation of credit risk, potentially representing a significant loss for high for creditors
and banks in the event of partial or total default. Credit ratings influence issuers’
credit quality, where downgrades can increase borrowing costs and trigger regulatory
restrictions. Due to the proprietary nature of the rating process performed by the
rating institutes, there are few insights into the underlying financial ratios and anal-
ysis that support the credit rating, despite extensive academic research published on
the subject of financial distress prediction.

In the original paper by Altman (1968), a total of twenty-two variables are selected
from the five standard ratio categories: liquidity, profitability, leverage, solvency, and
activity ratios. The selection is based on (1) popularity in the literature and (2)
relevancy to the study. After performing a Multiple Discriminant Analysis (MDA)
analysis, five of the most indicative variables for corporate bankruptcy are included
in the final model.

indicate that company size, financial structure, performance, and liquidity measures

financial ratios alone may be overstated, suggesting that incorporating market-based
indicators could further enhance accuracy.

Many studies have been made on corporate bankruptcy preduction, including the
pioneering discriminant analysis model (Altman 1968) and logistic regression (Ohlson
1980). Son et al. (2019) criticized these types of statistical models for being con-
strained in their ability to increase predictive power. This necessitates the use of
more advanced statistical methods, including deep learning models—a subset of ma-
chine learning that employs artificial neural networks.

                                    5
2.3 Deep Learning Models
Recent literature delves into the application of deep learning models for improving
predictive accuracy for default risk. In particular, a subclass of models utilizing gradi-
ent boosting have been shown to be the most effective and interpretable. Zięba et al.
(2016) were the first to explore this approach, developing a novel bankruptcy predic-
tion model by leveraging Extreme Gradient Boosting. The model is evaluated on a
dataset of Polish companies, predicting bankruptcy across different time horizons (1
to 5 years before failure), demonstrating superior performance to traditional models.

Further studies suggest that XGBoost offers significant advantages in financial dis-
tress prediction, particularly in handling high-dimensional datasets and identifying
key risk factors (Jones 2017, Carmona et al. 2019). Unlike traditional parametric
models, XGBoost accommodates a large number of predictors and ranks them based
on predictive power, allowing for more comprehensive risk assessment (Jones 2017).
Additionally, it has been successfully applied in bank failure prediction, where it
demonstrated superior accuracy in identifying leading indicators of distress, such as
financial leverage and profitability ratios (Carmona et al. 2019).

Jabeur et al. (2021) highlights the effectiveness of CatBoost, a gradient boosting model
developed by Prokhorenkova et al. (2019) which improves categorical feature handling,
reduces overfitting and enhances classification accuracy. The authors propose a novel
approach using CatBoost to address the classification problem of bankruptcy pre-
diction based on financial factors. First, the importance of the financial features is
investigated. Second, the model is benchmarked against eight reference machine learn-
ing models that are applied and tested using corporate default data from 2014–2016
from France. The results show that CatBoost had the highest performance out of all
implemented models.

The authors argue that most previous bankruptcy prediction studies lack interpretabil-
ity. This supports the case for using CatBoost, as it offers greater transparency com-
pared to ’black box’ deep learning models. It provides variable importance measures
and partial dependence plots, offering insight into individual predictions and inter-
actions between variables. These features make it an adequate choice for enhancing
interpretability, combined with providing superior classification performance in rela-
tion to other models.

Coşkun & Turanli (2023) examined credit risk assessment using gradient boosting
algorithms CatBoost, XGBoost, and LightGBM, leveraging data on Home Credit De-
fault Risk. Their findings support the robustness of these methods, once again with
CatBoost outperforming other gradient boosting models when incorporating credit-
ing tendency. Furthermore, a recent study by Akinjole et al. (2024) highlights the
importance of addressing data imbalance in credit default risk modeling using a re-
sampling method, Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique (SMOTE), for data
augmentation in conjunction with XGBoost to enhance classification results.

2.5 Gradient Boosting

Friedman (2001) that builds an ensemble of decision trees in a sequential manner

2.6 CatBoost
Categorical Boosting (CatBoost) is an open-source gradient boosting model devel-
oped by Prokhorenkova et al. (2019). This model has native support for categorical
features, implements a novel gradient boosting scheme to improve accuracy and offers

                                    7
hyperparameter optimization. These improvements have been made possible by using
two key refinements: ordered target statistics and ordered boosting.

CatBoost encodes categorical variables using ordered target statistics, avoiding target
leakage by ensuring the target value yk of an instance is never used in computing its
categorical encoding. Given a dataset D, the algorithm assigns a random permutation
σ and constructs subsets Dk = {x1, x2, . . . , xk−1} for encoding. The encoded value
for the i:th categorical feature of the k:th sample is computed as

where p is a prior, and a > 0 prevents division by zero (Prokhorenkova et al. 2019).

Standard gradient boosting suffers from prediction shift, where target encoding affects
gradient estimates, leading to biased updates. This is mitigated using ordered boost-
ing, where the same examples in Dk that were used in the categorical encoding are
used to compute estimates for ht+1. CatBoost builds multiple independent permuta-
tions σ1, σ2, . . . , σs of D to ensure that residual estimates use only past observations
in each permutation. This maintains robust gradient estimates

benchmarks.

3 Method

As outlined in the introduction, the main purpose in this paper is to evaluate whether
CatBoost is an efficient deep learning framework for predicting S&P credit ratings
based on underlying financial metrics. Therefore, the methodology is comprised of
the following main steps to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the research ques-
tions: (1) data collection, (2) feature selection, (3) model development, and (4) model
evaluation.

A structured approach is employed to ensure consistency and replicability of results.
An iterative development process was pursued to enhance interpretability and ro-
bustness, allowing for necessary adjustments to maintain coherence in addressing the
research questions. Next, we consider the general outline for data collection and
developing the model framework.

3.1 Data Collection
Training and evaluating a deep learning model requires a significant amount of data
to generate accurate and generalizable results. Therefore, to ensure that a sufficient
number of companies are present in the dataset, there is no geographical restriction
imposed. Instead the dataset contains all global companies that currently have an
active S&P credit rating as of 2024-12-31.

                                    8
In order to compute the underlying financial metrics to be used in the model, we
need to impose restrictions to ensure that the dataset contains sufficient information
on company financials and credit ratings. Setting these delimitations ensures that we
are able to reliably compare financials across different firms. We utilize the screening
feature on S&P Capital IQ to select companies based on the following criteria:

            Company on S&P Capital IQ
                AND Private or Public Company (No Municipalities)
              AND S&P Credit Rating <= ’AAA’
               AND S&P Credit Rating <> ’NR’
               AND Has Current Financials == True
               AND Cash and Equivalents <> ’NA’

This selection was iteratively developed to comply with aforementioned restrictions
and ensure completeness. Only private and public firms are included, eliminating any
municipalities and other governmental bodies to ensure comparability across compa-
nies. Each company in the selection has an active credit rating, excluding any non-
rated (NA) companies. Finally, we exclude any companies that have not reported
their cash and equivalents, used to ensure availability of relevant financial data.

3.2 Feature Selection
Determining what financial metrics to include in the dataset is heavily dependent on
availability of underlying financial data for the selected companies. There is a trade-
off between including as many companies as possible, and computing a wide range of
financial metrics that may have a predictive value for the S&P credit ratings, and by
proxy credit default risk. Therefore, a few different metrics from each of the following
categories are selected, including liquidity, profitability, efficiency and solvency ratios.

This selection is partly inspired by Jabeur et al. (2021), but is complemented with
other ratios that are qualitatively deemed to have a potential predictive effect on the
credit rating. After downloading the raw financial data for each of the companies,
these ratios are computed manually while ensuring data coverage. Selected financial
ratios and their definitions are presented in Table 3.

In addition to the numerical financial ratios, there are categorical data points in-
cluded in the dataset. Features such as country, company type, sector and industry
may have a systematic effect on the S&P credit rating. Financial markets generally
differentiate between developed and emerging markets, the latter often representing
high-risk investments with uncertainty about repayment prospects (Galizia & Lund
2024). Thus, country may therefore be an important property for determining credit
risk. CatBoost natively supports encoding of categorical features, making the imple-
mentation process is straight-forward.

In multi-class classification, we need to provide special attention to the target vari-
able S&P credit rating. In the raw dataset, there are 22 unique categories ranging
from AAA, AA+, AA, AA- to CCC+, CCC, CCC-, D and SD. Having this many
categories with few data points in each makes classification difficult, motivating the
definition of S&P credit rating group which maps these to 10 distinct categories: AAA,
AA, A, BBB, BB, B, CCC, CC, D and SD. This is used as the target variable.

     3.3 Model Development
      In the model development stage, the CatBoost framework is imported and imple-
      mented using Python. Following feature selection and data cleaning, the final dataset
       is split into training and testing subsets to prevent overfitting and ensure the gener-
       alizability of results. 90% of the data is used for training, while the remaining 10% is
      reserved for testing. A CatBoostClassifier is trained to perform multiclass classifica-
      tion on the S&P credit rating group, capturing non-linear relationships between the
       input features across the various rating categories.

      Hyperparameter optimization of the learning rate, tree depth and number of iter-
      ations is performed to identify an adequate model specification with high predictive
      accuracy and interpretability. Once trained, the CatBoost model is used to predict
      the testing dataset. Evaluation tools include the feature importance plot, identifying
      the most influential predictors, and the confusion matrix, which provides a detailed
       view of classification accuracy across rating groups. Together, these offer valuable in-
       sights into the model’s strengths, as well as areas where misclassifications may indicate
       potential limitations or bias in the data.

                                          10
Performance of multiclass classification models is limited by imbalanced datasets,
occurring when some classes are significantly underrepresented compared to others.
This can lead to biased models that favor majority classes, often at the expense of
correctly identifying minority classes. In this case, companies with low credit ratings
are severely underrepresented in the dataset, which is particularly problematic as they
represent the “interesting” outcome of financial distress prediction.

One technique to address this issue is the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Tech-
nique (SMOTE). It works by generating synthetic examples of the minority class
rather than duplicating existing instances. It creates new samples by interpolating
between existing minority class examples, effectively increasing their representation
without simply repeating data. When combined with under-sampling of the major-
ity class, SMOTE can help rebalance the dataset, improving classifier sensitivity to
underrepresented classes to enhance overall model performance (Chawla et al. 2002).

Missing values ("nan") in the dataset introduces problems when implementing the
SMOTE algorithm as it requires complete data to interpolate and generate synthetic
examples of the underrepresented classes. Further problems are created when at-
tempting to synthetically generate categorical features, e.g. it is tricky to interpolate
the midpoint between a public and private company, or the average of Sweden, France
and the US. To remedy these limitations, missing values can be replaced with zeros,
mean values of each ratio, or mean values within each credit rating group. This sig-
nificantly affects model performance, in the latter case introducing backtracking bias
where the model is able to predict categories in the testing set based on averages
observed in the training set. Further discussion on this topic is provided later.

3.4 Model Evaluation
Evaluation of multiclass classification models involves assessing both predictive per-
formance and interpretability. Standard metrics such as accuracy and the confusion
matrix provide an overview of how well the model distinguishes between different
credit rating categories. However, we are interested in why the model makes specific
predictions to understand the importance of individual financial metrics for predict-
ing the credit rating and their default risk implications. Additionally, such insights
may expose aspects of the proprietary credit rating methodologies employed by rating
institutions.

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is a unified framework designed to explain
the output of machine learning models, including gradient boosting models like Cat-
Boost. SHAP assigns each feature an importance value for individual predictions,
based on principles from cooperative game theory. This allows users to evaluate how
much each feature contributes to the prediction of a specific class, offering insights
into the model’s internal decision-making. SHAP values help reconcile the trade-
off between accuracy and interpretability, making it possible to trust and validate
high-performing but complex models (Lundberg & Lee 2017).

Liquidity This category of ratios showcase intuitive patters across credit ratings.
Higher-rated firms, particularly those rated ’AAA’, tend to have stronger liquidity
positions, as indicated by significantly higher current and quick ratios (4.50 and 1.97,
respectively). As credit ratings decline, these ratios generally decrease, with the lowest
values observed among companies rated ’CC’ and ’SD’, suggesting a weaker ability to
meet short-term obligations. Cash ratio does not exhibit the same pattern.

Solvency Equity ratio is quite consistent for groups ’AAA’ to ’B’, but is significantly
lower for ’CCC’ to ’SD’, indicating that solvency shows a clear deterioration as credit
ratings decline. Debt-to-assets and debt-to-equity ratios are generally lower for mid-
rated firms, with the exception of ’AAA’. This is mainly due to a high prevalence of
financial firms in this group. Interest coverage ratio is largest for high-rated firms,
indicating their ability to repay obligations arising from debt. In contrast, lower-rated
firms exhibit weaker solvency, with higher leverage and low interest coverage.

Profitability All profitability ratios consistently decline as credit ratings worsen,
with high-rated firms showing stronger gross, operating, and net margins. Particu-
larly, net margin becomes negative for firms rated ’B’ and below, reflecting operating
losses and financial strain. Return measures such as ROA and ROCE are relatively
stable across mid-tier ratings but drop sharply in the lowest-rated categories. Anoma-
lies like the high ROE for ’CC’ rated firms may be due to extremely low or negative
equity values, inflating the ratio and skewing the interpretation.

Efficiency These ratios display less consistent patterns across credit ratings com-
pared to other financial metrics. Asset turnover tends to improve with lower ratings,
peaking in mid-tier categories, suggesting higher sales relative to assets in lower-rated
firms. Receivables and payables turnover are generally higher for firms rated ’A’ to
’B’, indicating more active working capital management. Exceptionally high inven-
tory turnover for ’AAA’ rated firms reflects sectoral differences arising from financial
institutions with minimal inventory, rather than operational efficiency.

4.2 CatBoost
As the initial model, a CatBoostClassifier was trained for multiclass classification
using 1,000 boosting iterations, a learning rate of 0.1, and a tree depth of 8. Best
performance was achieved after 243 iterations, after which the model training was
stopped to prevent overfitting. On the test set, the model yielded an accuracy of
63.7%, with a mean squared error (MSE) of 4.47 and an R-squared value of 0.13,
indicating some predictive ability, although with room for further improvement.

4.3 CatBoost & SMOTE
To address class imbalance, we apply the Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Tech-
nique (SMOTE) to generate synthetic samples of the underrepresented credit rating
categories. This helps mitigate the bias toward majority classes in the training pro-
cess, in theory improving model prediction rate for minority classes, that are often
not predicted in imbalanced settings.

A limitation of SMOTE is its inability to process missing values, requiring imputation
prior to oversampling. We test three fill strategies: (1) replacing missing values with
zeros, (2) with the overall dataset mean, and (3) with the mean of the corresponding
rating category. While the first two methods yield only marginal improvements, us-
ing the rating category mean leads to noticeably better performance. This approach,
however, introduces backtracking bias as the imputation uses label information as-
sociated with the target variable, potentially leaking data from the test set into the
training process. This may create a risk of overfitting to the test set.

4.4 SHAP
While feature importance provides some insight into model prediction, we seek an even
more detailed view of the prediction process. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)
enables this by quantifying the contribution of each feature to individual predictions,
generating further insights by evaluating the extent to which each feature contributes
to a specific prediction. A SHAP summary plot visualizes these contributions across
all observations, showing both the magnitude and direction of each feature’s impact.

Each SHAP value quantifies the contribution of a feature to shifting a model’s pre-
diction away from or toward a specific class, relative to the average prediction. A
high positive SHAP value indicates that the feature increases the probability of the
corresponding class, while a large negative SHAP value suggests the feature decreases
the likelihood of that class. Values close to zero imply minimal influence.

are gray as they are not quantifiable. Regarding financial ratios, a high (red) debt-

somewhat complex to interpret, but still provides a visual demonstration of how the

5 Discussion

Overall, the model results are reasonable and, given the complexity of credit rating
prediction, quite strong. It is important to acknowledge that S&P credit ratings are
not purely based on quantitative financial metrics; they often incorporate qualitative
assessments, non-financial indicators, and direct interactions with company manage-
ment. These discretionary elements can introduce variation that is difficult for any
data-driven model to capture, thereby explaining some of the observed discrepancies
between predicted and actual ratings.

Nevertheless, the CatBoost model effectively captures clear dependencies between
financial ratios and S&P credit ratings. In particular, solvency ratios such as debt-
to-assets, equity ratio, and interest coverage ratio emerged as strong predictors, often
varying significantly between high and low credit rating categories. Profitability ratios
such as net margin and return on equity provide some predictive power, with lower-
rated firms frequently exhibiting negative margins. Additionally, categorical features
proved important as country helped capture differences in credit risk between devel-
oped and emerging markets, while sector reflected the influence of industry-specific
financial stability.

SMOTE addressed class imbalance in credit ratings, improving predictive perfor-
mance for underrepresented categories. However, the use of rating group means for
imputation introduced backtracking bias, potentially inflating performance metrics
and overfitting the model. Imbalances are also present in other dimensions, such
as country and sector. Companies in the financial sector are overrepresented in the
dataset, skewing the model perception to associate high debt-to-asset with high credit
ratings, which is counterintuitive. US firms are also overrepresented in the dataset, af-
fecting model results. There is an inherent trade-off between the quantity and quality
of input data, which is problematic as deep learning like CatBoost generally require
large, well-balanced datasets to perform reliably.

SHAP plays an important role in enhancing the interpretability of the model results,
particularly in complex applications such as credit rating prediction. In addition to
evaluating model performance, SHAP provides insight into which financial features
drive predictions, offering a means to assess whether S&P credit ratings align under-
lying financial characteristics. This framework not only supports model validation,
but also provides some insight into the otherwise proprietary methodologies used by
rating agencies, revealing systematic patterns in their decision-making process based
on underlying financial metrics.

5.1 Future Research
Rating Changes Credit ratings are not necessarily constant over time. Another
research approach is to consider data on S&P Credit Rating Action, containing events
such as upgrade, downgrade, new rating and monitor. Evaluating the recent develop-
ments of financial ratios would be valuable to identify patterns that precede rating
changes. Investors in debt instruments may be able to preemptively adjust their port-
folio by predicting these changes prior to publication. This approach is coherent with
previous research using financial metrics to predict defaults prior to their event.

                                    23
Scope of Companies Given the wide variation in sectors, industries and countries,
future research could benefit from restricting the dataset to a more homogeneous sub-
set, such as firms within a specific region or sector. While this would reduce the total
number of observations, it would improve comparability and may enhance the accu-
racy of the model by minimizing noise. There exists a trade-off between dataset size
and consistency across included firms to ensure comparability. Separating financial
firms from other sectors, like industrials or manufacturing, would be interesting to
create more specialized prediction models.

Additional Metrics Incorporating non-financial metrics such as ESG scores, gov-
ernance indicators, and headcount data is another possible direction for future re-
search. These factors are often considered in credit rating assessments but were ex-
cluded from this study due to the complexity of sourcing consistent data across thou-
sands of companies. Credit rating institutes rely on qualitative information gathered
through issuer meetings and other discretionary processes, which cannot be captured
by a purely quantitative model. Additionally, expanding the range of financial ratios
or incorporating macroeconomic variables could further enhance the model’s predic-
tive power. Instead of relying on static, most-recent-year data, a time-series approach
could be implemented to capture trends in financial ratios over time.

Model Comparison Following the approach of Jabeur et al. (2021), future research
could benchmark credit rating prediction models by comparing the performance of
various machine learning frameworks alongside traditional models such as Altman’s
Z-score and Ohlson’s probabilistic method. In addition, combining models through
ensemble techniques could enhance classification accuracy, particularly in the presence
of imbalanced and high-dimensional financial data. Future work might also explore
models that generate probabilistic predictions with calibrated confidence scores, pro-
viding a more nuanced assessment of classification uncertainty.

5.2 Practical Applications
In general, this study showcases the potential of CatBoost for credit rating classifi-
cation, and by proxy, financial distress prediction. This framework can be used by
fixed income investors, banks and regulators for modeling the credit risk of non-rated
companies by using financial ratios. This could also be used as a complement to of-
ficial S&P credit ratings, which may be slow to update due to organizational inertia
or reputational concerns of downgrading companies. An accurate measure of credit
risk is essential for monitoring portfolio exposure and taking preemptive actions to
mitigate potential rating downgrades, or in the worst case failure to meet the legal
obligations, e.g. default.

6 Conclusion

This paper has been guided by the following research questions:

   1. Is the CatBoost model able to effectively predict current S&P credit ratings
      with high accuracy based on underlying financial metrics?

   2. Which financial metrics have the highest predictive value for classification of
      firms into different S&P credit rating groups?

One of the main purposes of this paper is to evaluate whether the CatBoost model can
effectively predict S&P credit ratings based on a company’s financial characteristics,
and additionally to determine which financial metrics contribute most significantly
to these predictions. Based on strong performance of CatBoost for financial distress
prediction in prior research, the model is applied to a dataset of public and private
firms, using financial ratios and categorical variables to classify credit ratings.

After selecting a subset of companies with an active S&P credit rating, financial
ratios were computed as part of the feature engineering process. Using Python, a
CatBoost multiclass classification model is developed, later evaluated using SHapley
Additive exPlanations (SHAP). An attempt was made to address class imbalance
for lower rated firms like ’CCC’ and ’SD’ using Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling
Technique (SMOTE). However, while this improved the representation of minority
classes, it introduced backtracking bias due to label-informed imputation. SHAP was
employed to further interpret model predictions and to identify which financial ratios
had the greatest impact on credit rating classification.

These results indicate that CatBoost performs reasonably well in predicting S&P
credit ratings, with most misclassifications occurring within one or two adjacent cate-
gories. Categorical features like country and sector emerged as important predictors,
in addition to financial ratios such as net margin, return on equity, interest coverage
ratio and debt-to-assets. Additionally, the study provides insight into the proprietary
rating process used by rating agencies by identifying patterns in financial ratios across
S&P rating categories.

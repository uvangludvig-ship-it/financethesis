Abstract:

   This paper investigates whether gradient-boosted decision trees can offer both
   predictive performance and economic interpretability in default prediction for Swedish
   SMEs. Using firm-level accounting data from the Serrano database, we compare
   LightGBM with  logistic regression and apply SHAP and ALE, two explainable AI
   methods. LightGBM achieves stronger predictive accuracy  and calibration than
   logistic regression. ALE analysis shows that both models generally agree on the
   direction of effects, but also reveals meaningful non-linearities such as threshold
   effects,   diminishing   marginal   returns,   and   non-monotonic   patterns   that   linear
   specifications   struggle   to   capture.   Interaction   effects   are   modest   overall   but
   concentrate along the industry dimension, indicating that moderation is predominantly
   sector-driven rather than arising from complex nonlinear interactions among financial
   variables. The findings indicate that machine-learning models, when paired with
   interpretability  tools,  can  uncover  economically  meaningful  non-linearities  and
   interaction patterns in addition to achieving superior predictive performance.

   Credit Risk, Default Prediction, Explainable Machine Learning, LightGBM,  SHAP,

                      I. Introduction

Predicting corporate default is a central concern in modern finance. Accurate default-
prediction models underpin capital requirements under Basel III, inform loan pricing
and portfolio allocation, and enable early warning systems for distressed firms. Small
improvements in model discrimination translate into meaningful financial gains: loan
pricing and regulatory capital are direct functions of estimated probability of default,
meaning that prediction errors compound into mispriced risk and inefficient capital
allocation. The choice of modelling approach thus carries consequences well beyond
academic interest.
   This paper investigates whether machine-learning methods combined with ex-
plainable AI techniques can advance both the predictive accuracy and economic
understanding of corporate default among Swedish SMEs. We construct a LightGBM
gradient-boosted model using publicly filed financial statements, corporate registry
information, and macroeconomic data benchmarking it against logistic regression
and applying SHAP and Accumulated Local Effects methods to interpret the learned
relationships. This approach simulates the information set available to external
stakeholders, suppliers, customers, and alternative lenders who lack the granular
loan-level data possessed by primary banks. Our analysis addresses three research
questions: (1) Can we construct a competitive default-prediction model for SMEs
using only publicly filed accounting information? (2) What can a gradient-boosted
model reveal that is difficult or impossible to learn from logistic regression? (3) Do
the model’s predictive features vary across time or different macro-financial regimes?
Beyond these empirical questions, the analysis also illustrates how gradient-boosted
models paired with explainable AI methods can function as tools for exploratory
empirical research, surfacing patterns that inform the specification of traditional
regression models.
   Our empirical setting comprises approximately 300,000 firm-year observations of
Swedish SMEs from the Serrano database spanning 1998–2023. We restrict attention
to independent limited-liability companies satisfying European Commission size
criteria, excluding financial firms and group entities. The feature set draws on the
foundational variables from Altman (1968) and Ohlson (1980), augmented with
growth indicators, volatility measures and macroeconomic controls.
   The results yield several findings of both practical and methodological interest.
First, we demonstrate that publicly available accounting data contain substantial
predictive signal for SME default. As reported in Table I, our LightGBM model
achieves an AUC of 0.897 compared to 0.869 for logistic regression, a gain of
approximately 2,8 percentage points in discriminatory power. The 7% reduction in
Log Loss indicates that LightGBM also produces more accurate probability estimates.
Crucially, both models are well-calibrated, with expected calibration errors below
0.5%, meaning that predicted probabilities correspond reliably to observed default
frequencies (Figure 1).
   Second, we use SHAP to examine what each model relies upon. Both models
agree on the primacy of Dividend Payer and Cash Ratio as top predictors, but
the distribution of importance differs structurally (Figure 2). Logistic regression
concentrates predictive power in the top two features, while LightGBM distributes

                                    2
importance more evenly across the feature set, extracting signal from variables
whose effects are conditional or non-linear. Notably, we find that liquidity buffers
and dividend signalling, rather than profitability metrics, emerge as the dominant
predictors for Swedish SMEs (Figure 3).
    Third, we analyse the functional forms through which features influence default
risk. The logistic regression coefficients (Table II) broadly align directionally with
the classical specifications of Altman (1968) and Ohlson (1980): working capital,
retained earnings, and firm size reduce risk, while leverage increases it. We find
Total Asset Turnover to be positively correlated with default, unlike Altman (1968),
though this is likely a consequence of dataset composition. The ALE analysis
reveals that LightGBM captures important non-linearities that linear specifications
miss (Figure 9). Interest Coverage exhibits diminishing marginal returns beyond
approximately three times coverage. Net Profit Margin operates almost as a binary
signal, with the critical transition occurring between −5% and −18%; positive
margins provide negligible additional protection. Company Age displays a striking
non-monotonic pattern: newly founded firms carry the lowest predicted default risk,
risk peaks between one and ten years, then declines thereafter. Equity Ratio and
Return on Equity both exhibit U-shaped relationships, patterns that cause logistic
regression coefficients to collapse toward zero as opposing effects cancel.
    Fourth, the SHAP interaction analysis reveals that the model’s learned structure is
predominantly additive (Figure 5). Pairwise interactions account for approximately
30% of total model output on average (Figure 6), but this interaction budget
concentrates heavily along the industry dimension: the predictive signal of financial
ratios is sector-dependent, while ratio-on-ratio interactions are sparse. This finding
suggests that much of the performance gap between linear and ensemble methods may
be recoverable by introducing industry-conditional slopes into linear specifications, a
tractable extension that would retain interpretability.
    Fifth, we examine whether predictor importance varies across economic regimes.
Contrary to prior work documenting pronounced temporal shifts (Cascarino, Moscatelli,
and Parlapiano, 2022), we find remarkable stability in feature importance ranks
across six periods spanning 2004–2023 (Figure 7). The top-ranked features, Dividend
Payer, Cash Ratio, and Industry (SNI) maintain their positions from the pre-crisis
environment through the post-COVID rate-tightening period. This stability may
reflect either genuine constancy in the fundamental relationships between financial
health and default, or methodological dampening from our cumulative training
approach.
    The remainder of this paper proceeds as follows. Section II reviews related work
on default prediction and positions our contribution. Section III describes the data
and methodological framework. Section IV presents the empirical findings. Section V
concludes.

                   II. Related Works

The literature on corporate default prediction has evolved through several method-
ological traditions. We situate our contribution relative to three strands: traditional
accounting-based models, market-based structural approaches and recent machine-
learning applications.
   The foundational work in the accounting tradition is Altman (1968), whose
Z-score combined financial ratios into a linear discriminant function for bankruptcy
classification. Ohlson (1980) subsequently introduced logistic regression to estimate
default probabilities, offering calibrated outputs rather than binary classifications.
These approaches remain widely used because coefficients directly quantify each
variable’s contribution to risk, facilitating the transparent assessments that regulators
require. The core insight, that standard accounting ratios capturing profitability,
leverage and liquidity contain substantial predictive signal motivates our feature
construction.
   An alternative tradition originates with Merton (1974), who recognised that
equity can be valued as a call option on firm assets. Jackson and Wood (2013)
compare market-based models against accounting-based approaches and find that
the former outperform when market prices are observable, though accounting-based
models still achieve meaningful discriminatory power. The critical limitation is data
availability: structural models require publicly traded equity, which SMEs lack.
    The limitations of linear specifications have driven interest in flexible methods
that capture non-linear relationships without manual feature engineering. Gradient-
boosted decision trees demonstrate strong performance in credit-risk settings (Car-
mona, Climent, and Momparler, 2019), though opacity conflicts with regulatory
requirements for explainable assessments. Methods such as SHAP (Lundberg and Lee,
2017) and Accumulated Local Effects (Apley and Zhu, 2020) address this tension by
decomposing predictions into interpretable components. Another related application
is Brunlid (2025), whose bachelor thesis applies CatBoost, a gradient boosting model
together with SHAP to predict active S&P credit ratings.
    The most relevant prior work is Cascarino et al. (2022), who apply XAI techniques
to default prediction among Italian non-financial firms over 2009–2020. Combining
balance sheet data with credit behavioural indicators from the Italian Credit Register,
including credit utilization ratios, overdraft status, and non-performing loan flags they
demonstrate that gradient boosting outperforms logistic regression while revealing
how predictor importance shifts across the economic cycle. Our work takes a similar
methodological approach but differs in setting and data. We focus on Swedish SMEs
over a more recent period (2004–2023), covering the post-COVID environment and
subsequent monetary tightening that existing studies have not examined. Where
Cascarino et al. (2022) supplement balance sheet ratios with credit behavioural
indicators from the Italian Credit Register, including utilization ratios and non-
performing loan status, our feature set instead emphasizes growth dynamics, revenue
trends, drawdowns and earnings volatility alongside a dividend payer indicator that
emerges as highly predictive. We extend their analysis in two directions: a more
detailed examination of functional forms through ALE analysis, and a systematic
decomposition of the interaction effects captured by the gradient boosting model.

               III. Data and Methodology

A. Data
Firm-level data are obtained from the Serrano database (Swedish House of Finance,
2025). The Serrano database includes information on firms’ income statement and
balance sheet, as well as data from Bolagsverket, such as the firms’ sector, date of
registration and dates of other notable events such as mergers, reconstructions and
bankruptcies. To aid comparability, especially over time, Serrano adjusts, corrects,
and handles things like broken accounting periods, short and long accounting periods,
etc. From this raw accounting material, financial ratios are constructed to capture
liquidity, profitability, solvency, efficiency and growth characteristics. To account
for macroeconomic conditions, the dataset is augmented with data on lending rates
from SCB (Statistics Sweden, 2025). Other macro-economic data from SCB and the
Riksbank (Sweden’s central bank) on GDP growth, unemployment, and inflation
were experimented with, but eventually excluded from the final analysis due to low
predictive power.
    The initial dataset contains around 12 million firm-year observations (1998–2023).
It is then restricted to active Swedish SMEs as defined by the European Commission
(European Commission, 2003). Small enterprises have fewer than 50 employees
and turnover or total assets below €10 million, while medium-sized enterprises
have fewer than 250 employees and turnover below €50 million or assets below
€43 million. Micro enterprises have fewer than 10 employees and turnover or total
assets below €2 million and are not included in the sample. To ensure cross-firm
comparability and avoid intra-group distortions, only independent firms are retained,
and financial and real estate firms are also excluded, leaving roughly 300,000 firm-
year observations. The final dataset focuses exclusively on active Swedish SME
limited-liability companies (AB) and excludes all other legal forms, inactive firms
and group entities. In short, only standalone active Swedish SME ABs remain.
    Restricting the analysis to a single country is partially down to convenience,
but also has benefits in increasing comparability, thereby possibly allowing cleaner
inference. Similarly, analysing the full SME population rather than only firms large
enough to obtain external credit ratings ensures that the model captures the broad
spectrum of financial conditions among Swedish firms. Excluding non-independent
firms is also necessary for comparability and generalisation, as group effects otherwise
end up dominating the dataset.
    The dependent variable, Distress Event, equals 1 if a firm enters bankruptcy or
reorganisation in a given year, and 0 otherwise. Following standard practice in the
credit-risk literature, the model predicts distress one year ahead, using financial and
macroeconomic data from year t − 1 to forecast outcomes in year t. The dataset is
split into an 80% training set and a 20% test set for model evaluation.
    After training some initial models, a significant data leakage issue was identified in
the data. Firms about to go bankrupt had repeated financial statement information,
resulting in year-over-year metrics all being zero. This likely stems from the Serrano
dataset’s handling of, and adjustment for, fiscal versus calendar years. The issue was
rectified by removing the duplicated year, and transferring the target variable to the
previous year, thereby restoring the integrity of the predictive framework.

                                    5
B. Feature Selection Strategy
Extensive feature engineering was conducted to capture the dynamic nature of firm
distress. This process began with a core set of financial ratios established in the
foundational literature by Altman (1968) and Ohlson (1980), including standard
measures of liquidity, profitability and leverage. To augment these static snapshots,
we generated features capturing temporal dynamics, such as year-over-year changes,
multi-year compound growth rates and volatility measures.
    Over 100 variables were considered initially. This extensive search was necessitated
by the limitations of the available credit usage data. Cascarino et al. (2022), for
instance, utilise data from the Italian Credit Register to construct behavioural
variables like drawn-to-granted ratios, which often rank among the most important
predictors in banking models. Although the raw source material for the Serrano
database contains fields for granted and utilised bank overdrafts, these variables are
not retained in the final, processed Serrano dataset. We exclude them here due to
significant underlying data quality issues, combined with the practical infeasibility
of accurately replicating the complex standardisation procedures required to merge
them into the processed sample. To compensate for the exclusion of these granular
credit-line metrics, our strategy relied on extracting maximum signal from public
financial statements through feature engineering.
    The final feature set was constructed through a holistic process combining domain
knowledge, multicollinearity diagnostics, stability selection and out-of-sample perfor-
mance evaluation. While Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDTs) are theoretically
robust to uninformative features and capable of handling correlated predictors, the
inclusion of irrelevant variables can degrade generalisation performance and ob-
scure interpretation. Furthermore, logistic regressions struggle significantly with
multicollinearity. Statistical diagnostics addressed these concerns: we iteratively
removed features with extreme Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) and utilised the
Boruta algorithm (Kursa and Rudnicki, 2010) to establish a formal significance
benchmark. Conflicts between statistical selection and domain theory were resolved
by prioritising features with robust economic definitions, for example, ratios with
stable denominators (e.g., Total Assets) were preferred over those prone to explosion
due to the ”small denominator problem” common in SME data.

C. Feature Composition
The final model comprises 22 features, fully detailed in Table IV in Appendix A.

C.1. Traditional Accounting Ratios.
From Altman (1968)’s Z-score, we retain Working Capital / Total Assets (X1),
Retained Earnings / Total Assets (X2), and Total Asset Turnover (X5). From Ohlson
(1980)’s O-score, we include Log Total Assets (SIZE) and Debt Ratio (TL/TA). These
are augmented with standard ratios: Cash Ratio and Quick Ratio for liquidity; Net
Profit Margin, Gross Margin, and Return on Equity for profitability; and Equity
Ratio and Interest Coverage for leverage. In total, these account for 13 of the 22
features.

                                    6
C.2. Efficiency Metrics.
Beyond Total Asset Turnover, we include Days Sales Outstanding and Days Payables
Outstanding to capture the working capital cycle and cash conversion speed.

C.3. Growth and Volatility Indicators.
Year-over-year revenue growth is augmented by Revenue CAGR (3Y) to measure
medium-term trajectory. EBITDA Volatility (3Y), defined as the standard deviation
of EBITDA scaled by total assets, captures earnings instability. Revenue Drawdown
(5Y) measures the maximum peak-to-trough decline in net sales over five years,
identifying firms that may appear stable currently but carry latent risk from recent
shocks.

C.4. Structural and Contextual Controls.
Company Age addresses the “liability of newness” (Stinchcombe, 1965). Dividend
Payer is a binary indicator serving as a summary statistic for cash-flow capacity.
Industry (SNI) controls for sector-specific baseline risk. The Term Spread captures
macroeconomic conditions via the slope of the yield curve.

D. Methodological Framework
A fundamental distinction exists between the objectives of classical econometrics
focused on causal modelling and machine learning focused on prediction. This
dichotomy was articulated by Shmueli (2010) and is also partially related to the
”two cultures” of statistical modelling identified by Breiman (2001). In traditional
empirical finance, the primary objective is causal explanation: the isolation of
unbiased parameter estimates (β ˆ) with the objective of testing hypotheses about
causal or structural relationship between covariates and a dependent variable. This
paradigm relies very strongly on the underlying assumption that the data are
generated from a stochastic process with some known functional form, typically
linear plus some error term. Using this framework, model selection is driven by
in-sample goodness-of-fit as well as the statistical significance of individual coefficients
under these assumptions.
    The machine learning paradigm, however, prioritises prediction, i.e. the generali-
sation error of the model on unseen data ( yˆ). This shift allows for the functional
form of the data-generating process to be treated as unknown rather than imposing
a more rigid structure on the data. The ML algorithms then approximate the
underlying function f (x) by learning directly from the data. This shift in objective
from explaining the past to forecasting the future is subtle but very significant, and
therefore necessitates a different set of tools and validation protocols.
    In the context of corporate default prediction, this shift is not merely academic.
The primary objective of financial institutions is to obtain an accurate model for
estimating the probability of default of future firms. A model that yields statistically
significant coefficients but fails to discriminate between solvent and insolvent firms
therefore offers very limited practical value for the actual operator of the model.
Superior predictive accuracy directly translates to more efficient capital allocation,

                                    7
as real-world loan pricing and regulatory capital requirements (e.g., under Basel III
(Basel Committee on Banking Supervision, 2011)) are functions of the estimated
probability of default (PD). Consequently, a model that reduces prediction error
improves the pricing of risk and enhances the stability of the financial system.
   Crucially, however, prediction and causal explanation need not be mutually
exclusive. As Shmueli (2010) emphasizes, predictive models can inform and refine
theory by revealing patterns that simpler specifications miss. The main challenge
then becomes methodological, more expressive models are almost by definition
harder to interpret, and standard machine learning tools are effectively ”black boxes”
whose internal mechanisms are near impossible to interpret. This opacity is not only
unfortunate from an academic perspective, but also has real world consequences, with
both regulators and the operators of requiring (European Banking Authority, 2021)
model transparency to be confident in their use. Explainable AI (XAI) techniques
address this tension by providing post-hoc methods which decompose predictions
into interpretable components. By applying such methods, namely SHAP values and
Accumulated Local Effects to a high-performing GBDT model, this paper aims to
recover economic insights from a high-performing model without sacrificing predictive
accuracy.

E. Model Specifications
E.1. Logistic Regression
To provide a benchmark for performance and interpretability, we employ a stan-
dard Logistic Regression model estimated via a Generalized Linear Model (GLM)
framework.
    To address the statistical heterogeneity typical of firm-level financial data, we
apply a preprocessing pipeline specifically for the logistic regression model, following
established practice in the default prediction literature (Nyitrai and Vir´ag, 2019).
Interest Coverage, which tends toward extreme values when interest expense ap-
proaches zero, is first clipped to the range [−5, 20] based on domain knowledge.
Beyond these thresholds the ratio loses economic meaning, and leaving such values
untreated would disproportionately influence coefficient estimates given the linear
specification. Remaining predictors exhibiting extreme outliers or heavy tails are
then Winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles to reduce the influence of extreme
observations while retaining sample information. Numeric variables are scaled using
a robust scaler based on the median and interquartile range. The Industry (SNI) is
encoded using Weight of Evidence (WoE) transformation with Laplace smoothing
(α = 0.5), where each category is replaced by the log-ratio of the within-category
event distribution to the non-event distribution (Siddiqi, 2006).
    The target value is transformed such that the model estimates the log-odds of
default as a linear combination of input features (logistic regression). To control for
potential heteroscedasticity, we report HC3 (Heteroscedasticity-Consistent) robust
standard errors. The implementation is done in Python using scikit-learn for
feature transformation and statsmodels for GLM estimation.
    In industry practice, credit risk modelling with logistic regression is done using
Weight of Evidence (WOE) binning. This enables logistic regression to capture

                                    8
non-linear patterns (Siddiqi, 2006). For this analysis, we avoid doing this deliberately,
applying minimal preprocessing to isolate the architectural differences between linear
and tree-based models.

E.2. Gradient Boosted Decision Trees
To capture non-linearities and interaction effects without manual specification, we
employ Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT). These models are, unlike logistic
regression, invariant to monotonic transformations of input features, meaning that
predictors can be left in their raw form to preserve interpretability.
Decision Trees
The fundamental building block of this approach is the classification and regression
tree (CART) (Breiman, Friedman, Stone, and Olshen, 1984). CARTs are able to
approximate arbitrarily complex relationships by recursively partitioning the feature
space into J distinct, non-overlapping regions (leaves) Rj . For an input vector x, the
CART T predicts a constant value cj corresponding to the region into which x falls:

These regions are able to be effectively constructed via a greedy algorithm which, at
each step, splits the feature set on the single variable xk at a point s which partitions
the data into two half-planes that maximise the reduction in impurity.
Gradient Boosting
While single CARTs are able to approximate arbitrarily complex functions and remain
very understandable while doing so, single CARTs are extremely prone to over-fitting,
and are therefore not used in practice. Ensemble methods which combine many weak
learners (shallow trees) into a single predictive model Fm(x) have, however, been
shown to be extremely performant, especially on tabular data. Gradient Boosting
(Friedman, 2001), is one of the most popular and powerful ensemble methods, and
it works by building the tree sequentially. At each iteration m, a new tree hm(x)
is fitted to the negative gradient of the loss function with respect to the previous
ensemble’s prediction Fm−1(x). The model updates in a stagewise fashion:

                        Fm(x) = Fm−1(x) + η · hm(x)

where η is the learning rate and scales the contribution of each tree. Our implemen-
tation minimizes the Logloss (1). This objective can be shown to provide unbiased
estimates of the real probabilities. Because the negative gradient of the LogLoss is
the residual (y − p), the boosting algorithm effectively fits trees to the errors of the
probability estimates at each step. This formulation allows the GBDT to be inter-
preted as an additive logistic regression model, where the log-odds are constructed
as a sum of weak learners rather than a single linear equation.

                                    9
   We choose to implement this algorithm using LightGBM (Ke, Meng, Finley,
Wang, Chen, Ma, Ye, and Liu, 2017) in Python. This choice is primarily motivated
by the great computational efficiency of this implementation. LightGBM utilizes
histogram-based algorithms and a leaf-wise tree growth strategy, which allows for
faster training speeds and lower memory consumption on large tabular datasets.
Furthermore, LightGBM can natiavely handle both missing and categorical values.
   Hyperparameters are selected using the Optuna framework (Akiba, Sano, Yanase,
Ohta, and Koyama, 2019), which employs Bayesian optimisation to efficiently search
the parameter space. Because gradient-boosted trees can approximate arbitrarily
complex functions, regularisation is essential to ensure the model generalises to unseen
data rather than memorising the training sample. Our specification incorporates three
forms of regularisation: L1 and L2 penalties on leaf weights, which shrink coefficient
magnitudes and encourage sparsity; a maximum tree depth constraint, which limits
the complexity of individual trees; and row and column subsampling, which introduces
stochasticity by training each tree on a random subset of observations and features,
thereby reducing variance.

F. Evaluation Criteria
The distinction between prediction and causal explanation, as well as the weaker as-
sumptions on the underlying data, shapes how models should be evaluated. Standard
econometric practice emphasizes in-sample fit and coefficient significance. These
metrics are designed to assess whether estimated relationships are statistically robust,
and less about evaluating overall model power. Predictive modelling, by contrast,
requires metrics that assess overall model performance on unseen data, and which
relate to the operational objectives of credit risk management. We therefore evaluate
models along three complementary dimensions: discrimination, probabilistic accuracy,
and calibration.
ROC-AUC
The Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve is designed to measure
discriminative ability, or the ability of the model to distinguish high PD and low
PD firms. Concretely, it measures the probability that the model assigns a higher
risk score to a randomly chosen defaulting firm than to a randomly chosen solvent
firm. A value of 0.5 indicates no discriminatory power, while 1.0 represents perfect
separation. Credit-scoring models typically achieve values between around 0.75
and around 0.90 (Lessmann, Baesens, Seow, and Thomas, 2015). The downside of
ROC-AUC, especially when considering the objective of estimating PD is that it
only evaluates rank-ordering, not the accuracy of probability levels. This is a critical
limitation, as a model which perfectly separates defaulters from survivors but assigns
arbitrary probability values would receive a ROC-AUC of 1.0 while being useless for
pricing or capital allocation.
Probabilistic Accuracy: LogLoss and Brier Score
For this model, perhaps the most important metrics are ones which directly evaluate
the assigned probabilities of default for the model. Since loan pricing and regulatory
capital requirements under Basel III are direct functions of the estimated probability

G. Explainable AI Methods
While it is effectively impossible to directly examine the contents of ML models to
derive insights about the functionality of the models, post-hoc methods for recovering
interpretable insights have recently been developed and gained prominence. We
employ two of these methods which together end up being complementary, namely
SHAP which provides local explanations by decomposing individual predictions into
feature contributions, and ALE which reveals the average functional relationship
between each predictor and the target across the entire dataset.

G.1. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)
A central challenge in interpreting predictive models is attribution: when a model
predicts a 15% probability of default, which features drove this prediction, and by how
much? For linear models, coefficients provide a direct answer; for non-linear models

                                   11
with interactions, attribution becomes ambiguous since a feature’s contribution
depends on the values of other features.
   SHAP resolves this ambiguity using a solution from cooperative game theory
(Lundberg and Lee, 2017). Each feature is treated as a ”player” in a coalition game
where the payout is the model’s prediction; Shapley values provide the unique fair
allocation of this payout among features. Concretely, the Shapley value for a feature
is computed by considering all possible subsets of features and measuring how much
the prediction changes when that feature is added to each subset. This marginal
contribution is then averaged across all subsets, weighted by the number of ways
each subset can form. For feature j, the Shapley value is:

             ϕj (v) =                         [fx(S ∪ {j}) − fx(S)] (2)
                                          |F |!

where F is the set of all features, S is some subset excluding feature j, and fx(S)
denotes the model’s prediction using only features in S. The SHAP value ϕj can thus
be interpreted as the average marginal contribution of feature j to the prediction,
controlling for all possible interactions with other features.
    In econometric terms, SHAP values represent the local marginal effect of each
variable on log-odds for the specific observation. To construct global importance
measures, SHAP values can be aggregated; the mean absolute SHAP value indicates
a feature’s average impact across the dataset.

decision trees to compute exact Shapley values efficiently. For our logistic regression,

SHAP Interaction Values
Machine learning models often also rely on complex interaction effects between
variables. SHAP is also a powerful tool for computing the importance of pairwise
interaction terms. For any two features j and k, the SHAP interaction value ϕj,k
captures the portion of the prediction attributable to their joint effect, that is, the
effect which cannot be separated into the sum of their individual contributions. This
value is computed by distributing the Shapley value of feature j across its interactions
with other features. A non-zero value of ϕj,k indicates that the effect of feature j
depends on the value of feature k.

G.2. Accumulated Local Effects (ALE)
While SHAP explains features’ contributions to individual predictions, it is often
of use to obtain a global view of how the model’s output changes, on average, as a
given input feature varies across its range. Partial Dependence Plots (PDP) are a
common tool for this purpose, but they suffer from a critical flaw when predictors
are correlated (as financial ratios typically are). The PDP for variable j is computed
as the average prediction across all the observations while varying variable j across
its entire range. This can easily result in unrealistic combinations of features (e.g., a
firm with very low assets but very high revenue), meaning that the model will not
have been trained on such inputs. This extrapolation produces misleading estimates
of feature effects.
   Accumulated Local Effects (Apley and Zhu, 2020) is a method developed specif-
ically to solve this problem. Instead of averaging across the entire dataset, ALE
considers only observations that naturally occur near each feature value. The method
works by (1) partitioning the feature’s range into small intervals; (2) for observations
in a given interval, calculate the local effect by taking the difference between the
prediction at the interval’s upper and lower boundaries, and then averaging these
differences across all observations within the interval; (3) accumulate these local
effects from the lowest feature value upward. Formally, the ALE function is defined

In this section, we present the empirical findings from running our models and
applying explainable AI. The analysis proceeds in five stages. First, we compare the
actual predictive performance of our LightGBM model to our logistic regression model
to quantify how much additional performance the increased expressiveness of GBDT’s
yield. Second, we examine global feature importance for both models via SHAP to
understand what each model relies upon to make its predictions. Third, we analyse
the functional forms of the features in the models to reveal how features influence
default risk. Fourth, we examine the feature interactions in the LightGBM model
to try to understand what portion of its advantage can be attributed to capturing
feature dependencies which have not been added to the logistic regression model.
Lastly, we look at how feature SHAP importance varies over time for LightGBM to

B. Global Feature Importance
Figure 2 contrasts the mean absolute SHAP values of the LightGBM model against
the logistic baseline. While both models agree on the primacy of the Dividend
Payer and Cash Ratio variables, the distribution of importance reveals a structural
divergence. The logistic model concentrates the vast majority of its predictive power
in the top two features, whereas LightGBM exhibits a flatter importance profile,
distributing weight more evenly across the feature set.
    This difference is a consequence of model architecture. The logistic regression is
constrained to additive, global coefficients, forcing it to rely exclusively on features
with strong, monotonic linear relationships to the target. In contrast, LightGBM
is not bound by this linearity constraint. It can extract value from features that
exhibit complex, non-monotonic patterns which a linear model might discard as
noise. Crucially, the tree-based structure allows the model to capture high-order
interactions, assigning significant importance to variables that may possess little
predictive power in isolation but become highly relevant when considered in concert
with other features.
    Comparing our findings to the existing literature highlights the importance of
the specific economic context. While Jabeur, Gharib, Mefteh-Wali, and Ben Arfi
(2021) employed CatBoost on French corporate data, they found profitability metrics,
specifically return on shareholder funds and return on capital employed to be the
dominant predictors. Conversely, our analysis of Swedish SMEs indicates that
liquidity buffers and dividend signaling are the primary determinants of default risk.

C.1. Comparison with Classical Models
The signs on our core variables align with both Altman (1968) and Ohlson (1980).
Consistent with Altman’s X1 and X2, we find that Working Capital/TA and Retained
Earnings/TA carry negative coefficients, indicating that liquidity and accumulated
earnings reduce default risk. Similarly, Log Total Assets and Debt Ratio align with
Ohlson’s SIZE and TL/TA variables.
    However, coefficient magnitudes differ substantially from the classical specifica-
tions. Debt Ratio carries a coefficient of just 0.17 versus Ohlson’s 6.03, and Working
Capital/TA is only marginally significant (p = 0.08) with a coefficient of −0.14
compared to Ohlson’s −1.43. These attenuations likely reflect our richer feature
set: Interest Coverage and Cash Ratio absorb solvency signals, while Days Sales
Outstanding and Days Payables Outstanding decompose working capital into its
constituent timing dynamics. In contrast, Log Total Assets (−0.53) aligns closely
with Ohlson’s SIZE coefficient (−0.41), suggesting the firm size effect is robust and
not absorbed by other variables.
    Several coefficients carry signs that appear counterintuitive relative to classical
specifications but reflect the distinct economics of SME populations. Total Asset
Turnover is positive, contrary to Altman’s original model where higher turnover
indicated operational efficiency and reduced default risk. This discrepancy likely
reflects differences in sample composition: Altman’s model was estimated on large
U.S. manufacturing firms, where high asset turnover signalled productive capital use,
whereas among SMEs it may instead indicate asset-light operations with limited
buffers or unsustainable sales generation relative to the asset base. Similarly, both
Revenue Growth (YoY) and Revenue CAGR (3Y) associate with higher default
probability. Though variables absent from the classical models but consistent with
the “overtrading” phenomenon whereby rapidly growing firms exhaust working
capital faster than they generate cash (Delmar, McKelvie, and Wennberg, 2013).

C.2. Beyond Linear Specifications
The ALE curves reveal that LightGBM largely corroborates the directional rela-
tionships identified in the logistic regression, but captures important non-linearities
that help explain several coefficient patterns. For Debt Ratio, LightGBM exhibits a
substantially steeper slope in the low-to-moderate leverage range before plateauing
around a ratio of 10 (Figure 9); this pattern explains the attenuated logistic coeffi-
cient, as the linear specification averages over a steep initial region and a flat tail,
producing a coefficient that understates the risk gradient where most observations
lie. Total Asset Turnover displays similar behaviour, with a steeper initial slope
followed by a plateau around a ratio of 4. For Working Capital/TA, the models
agree over the economically meaningful range, though LightGBM identifies a sharp
discontinuity around −0.30, though this may be an artifact of sparse data rather than
a genuine threshold. Retained Earnings/TA presents a starker divergence: while the
logistic coefficient (−0.39) aligns with Altman’s X2, LightGBM exhibits step-change
behaviour around zero, with negative retained earnings actually associated with
lower default risk than modestly positive values.
    A distinct class of non-linearity appears in Equity Ratio and Return on Equity,
both of which exhibit U-shaped relationships with elevated default risk at extreme
values (Figure 9). For Equity Ratio, this non-monotonicity explains its statistical
insignificance in the logistic regression (β = −0.02, p = 0.87): forced to fit a single
slope through a U-shaped pattern, the linear model estimates a coefficient near
zero as positive and negative effects cancel. Return on Equity displays a similar
pattern, with elevated risk at both very negative values (indicating distress) and
very high values (above approximately 0.7). The elevated risk at high ROE may
reflect volatile or highly leveraged business models, though it could also stem from
dataset composition: firms with very high ROE often have small equity bases, making
them disproportionately young or thinly capitalised. These U-shaped relationships
represent a class of non-linearity that tree-based methods can exploit but that require
explicit polynomial or spline terms in parametric specifications.
    Exhaustive treatment of every feature, while admirable in principle, exceeds our
present ambitions; Figure 4 highlights four with particularly instructive non-linear
patterns.
Interest Coverage.
Panel A shows that default probability decreases steeply as interest coverage rises
from negative values toward approximately 3, then flattens considerably for higher
values. This diminishing marginal effect broadly validates findings from Blume, Lim,
and MacKinlay (1998), who, in their analysis of corporate credit ratings, documented
that interest coverage improvements from 0 to 5 strongly improved ratings while
increases beyond 10–20 provided negligible incremental benefit. Although their study
examined credit ratings rather than default prediction directly, the convergence
of findings across outcome measures and institutional contexts suggests this non-
linearity reflects a genuine feature of how debt-servicing capacity relates to firm risk.
The logistic regression, constrained to a linear specification, captures the average
gradient but cannot represent this threshold structure.

                                   20
Net Profit Margin.
Panel B reveals that LightGBM exhibits near-binary behaviour with respect to
profitability. The transition occurs between approximately −5% and −18% margin,
where default probability rises steeply; beyond −18%, further deterioration adds
little incremental signal, while positive margins have essentially no marginal effect.
The logistic regression, by contrast, naturally imposes a linear slope.
   This threshold behaviour connects to a modelling distinction between Altman
(1968) and Ohlson (1980). Altman treated profitability ratios as continuous predictors
where every marginal decrease corresponds to a proportional increase in default risk.
Ohlson, by contrast, augmented standard ratios with a binary indicator for negative
net income two years in a row (INTWO), explicitly recognising that the transition
into loss-making represents a qualitatively distinct regime. LightGBM appears to
have organically rediscovered this insight, treating net profit margin as a regime
indicator where the sign of profitability contains the primary signal.
    Interestingly, Gross Margin exhibits a more linear relationship across most of
its range (Figure 9), though LightGBM identifies a step change around 10%. This
linearity may reflect that gross margin captures more fundamental firm economics:
pricing power, operational efficiency and differentiated products, where each incre-
mental improvement provides additional buffer against adverse shocks. Net margin,
by contrast, determines ultimate viability; crossing zero represents a regime change
that gross margin does not.
Days Payables Outstanding.
Panel C displays a non-linear relationship rarely examined in traditional default-
prediction studies. LightGBM reveals a muted effect up to approximately 40 days,
with even a slight uptick in default probability for firms paying very rapidly (< 20
days). Between 40 and 80 days, the effect rises sharply.
    This pattern resonates with established findings on trade credit. The slight risk
elevation for rapid payers, if genuine, aligns with Deloof (2003), who characterises
accounts payable as flexible, inexpensive financing. Underutilising this credit window
may indicate suboptimal liquidity management. The muted risk profile up to 40
days is consistent with Jacobson and von Schedvin (2015), who document average
trade credit durations of approximately 31 days in Sweden, suggesting that payment
behaviour within this window reflects normal operations rather than distress. The
sharp increase beyond 40 days corroborates Deloof (2003)’s finding that less profitable
firms systematically delay payments, making abnormal DPO a proxy for liquidity
constraints.
Company Age.
Panel D shows that LightGBM identifies firms between approximately 1 and 10
years old as carrying elevated default risk, consistent with the liability of newness
documented in organisational ecology (Stinchcombe, 1965). Risk then decreases
with diminishing returns, stabilising for firms beyond roughly 30 years, reflecting
survivorship bias, whereby persistent firms are systematically healthier (Brown,
Goetzmann, Ibbotson, and Ross, 1992).
    Strikingly, firms at year zero exhibit by far the lowest predicted default probability.
This likely reflects that newly founded firms possess starting capital and have not yet

normal operating cycles in construction. LightGBM captures these sector-specific

as illustrated in Figure 6, interactions typically account for 20% to 40% of the total

et al. (2021) rely exclusively on financial ratios, explicitly omitting industry indicators.
Without industry features, ratio-on-ratio interactions may emerge as proxies for
sector-specific capital structures. By explicitly modelling industry, our specification
absorbs sectoral heterogeneity directly, reducing both the structural need for and
the appearance of complex ratio-on-ratio interactions.

D.1. Implications for Model Choice.
The sparse, structured interaction pattern we observe has encouraging implications
for the interpretability-accuracy trade-off in credit risk modelling for SMEs. We find
LightGBM’s learned structure to be approximately additive: even a fully additive
model without any interactions would perform reasonably well. Because the interac-
tions that do occur concentrate along the industry dimension rather than densely
among arbitrary feature pairs, predictions can be meaningfully explained as sums of
individual feature contributions, with industry providing necessary moderation.
    This finding connects to broader evidence on model complexity. Lou, Caruana,
Gehrke, and Hooker (2013) demonstrate that models restricted to main effects and
pairwise interactions achieve accuracy comparable to full-complexity random forests
across a range of prediction tasks, establishing that higher-order interactions (in-
volving three or more features simultaneously) contribute negligibly to ensemble
performance. Our analysis suggests that credit risk prediction may require con-
siderably less complexity even within the pairwise space. Moreover, the pairwise

                                    24
structure that does exist is concentrated along the industry dimension, representing
a particularly tractable form of non-additivity.
    Importantly, the industry interactions we observe reveal that industry’s effect on
default risk is more complex than what standard approaches such as fixed effects or
weight-of-evidence encoding can capture. These methods allow industry to shift the
baseline risk level, but our results show that industry also moderates the effects of
other variables, the predictive signal of a given financial ratio differs across sectors.
This moderation can be introduced into linear frameworks via frequentist random-
slope models (Gelman and Hill, 2007) or Bayesian hierarchical specifications with
group-level slope parameters (Gelman, Carlin, Stern, Dunson, Vehtari, and Rubin,
2013), both of which permit feature coefficients to vary by sector. If the pattern
we document generalises, a substantial share of the performance gap between linear
and ensemble methods attributable to interactions could be closed by this single,
principled extension.
    Gradient boosted decision trees nonetheless retain practical advantages beyond
exploratory analysis. In prediction, GBDTs will most likely outperform even carefully
specified linear models: they capture non-linear main effects automatically, and while
ratio-on-ratio interactions appear weak on average, they may contribute meaningfully
in tail cases where multiple risk factors compound. The contribution of the present
analysis is not to argue against ensemble methods, but to characterise the structure
of what they learn. Our results suggest that for practitioners operating under inter-
pretability constraints, augmenting linear models with industry-conditional slopes
offers an empirically motivated path to recovering much of ensemble performance.

E. Feature Importance Over Time
To examine whether the predictive drivers of default vary across economic regimes,
we compute SHAP importance for models trained on cumulative historical data
and evaluated on six distinct periods: Pre-Crisis (2004–2006), Financial Crisis
(2008–2010), Sovereign Debt Crisis (2011–2013), Recovery (2014–2018), COVID-19
(2020–2021), and Post-COVID (2022–2023). Figure 7 presents a heatmap of feature
importance ranks across these periods.
   The most striking finding is the lack of meaningful temporal variation. The
top-ranked features, Dividend Payer, Cash Ratio, and Industry SNI maintain their
positions with remarkable consistency across all six periods. While modest shifts
appear in the middle ranks, the overall hierarchy remains stable from the pre-crisis
environment through the post-COVID period.
    Two features warrant specific discussion. Term Spread exhibits the largest
variation, jumping from rank 22 in the pre-crisis period to rank 7 during the 2008–
2010 financial crisis. This shift likely reflects a mechanical effect: the yield curve
exhibited substantially greater variation during financial stress, providing more
discriminatory power. During stable periods when the term spread is compressed,
the feature contains less information. Total Asset Turnover shows a gradual increase
in importance over time, rising from rank 18 in the pre-crisis period to rank 8 in
recent years, though the economic interpretation of this trend is unclear.
    Several factors may explain the overall stability. First, our methodology trains
each period’s model on all preceding data, meaning that crisis-period models are

                                    25
dominated by pre-crisis observations. This choice is driven by data constraints. With
approximately 10,000 firm-year observations per year and a default rate of 1.8%,
each year contains fewer than 200 default events, insufficient for stable estimation of
a gradient-boosted model with 22 features. Cumulative training ensures adequate
sample size, particularly for earlier periods, and reflects realistic deployment scenarios
where practitioners would train on all available history. However, this approach
dampens regime-specific signals, as crisis-period models inherit relationships esti-
mated predominantly from pre-crisis data. Second, the relatively low base rate of
defaults (≈1.8%) limits statistical power to detect subtle shifts in feature importance
within shorter time windows. Third, the fundamental relationships between financial
health and default may genuinely be stable: highly leveraged firms with poor liquidity
face elevated default risk regardless of the macroeconomic environment, even if the
level of risk varies cyclically.
   These findings contrast with Cascarino et al. (2022), who document more pro-
nounced shifts in predictor relevance across the Italian economic cycle. Using a
one-year rolling window approach, whereby they train on year t − 1 and evaluating
on year t, they find that liquidity and indebtedness indicators become particularly
influential during financial stress. This methodological difference is consequential:
their approach allows the model to adapt more rapidly to changing relationships,
while our cumulative training smooths over regime-specific patterns. Whether our
stability reflects genuine constancy in the underlying relationships or limitations
in statistical power given our training approach remains an open question. The
Italian context may also differ substantively, with potentially higher default rates and
different firm characteristics enabling sharper identification of temporal variation.

                     V. Conclusion

A. Summary of Findings
This paper investigated whether machine learning methods combined with explainable
AI techniques can advance both predictive accuracy and economic understanding of
corporate default among Swedish SMEs.
   A competitive default-prediction model for SMEs can indeed be constructed using
only publicly filed accounting information. Our LightGBM model achieves strong
discriminatory power that exceeds a logistic regression baseline, with both models
exhibiting reliable calibration. These results suggest that external stakeholders
lacking granular credit-line data can nonetheless build effective default-prediction
systems from financial statements alone.
    Gradient-boosted models reveal several patterns that are difficult or impossible
to learn from logistic regression. The SHAP importance analysis shows that logis-
tic regression concentrates predictive power in its top features, while LightGBM
distributes importance more evenly, extracting signal from variables whose effects
are conditional or non-linear. The ALE analysis uncovers threshold effects in inter-
est coverage and profitability, non-monotonic relationships in firm age and capital
structure, and U-shaped patterns that cause certain logistic regression coefficients to
collapse toward zero. The interaction analysis reveals a predominantly additive model
structure, with pairwise effects concentrating along the industry dimension rather
than diffusing across financial ratios, suggesting that much of the ensemble advantage
attributable to interactions could be recovered by introducing industry-conditional
slopes into linear specifications.
   The model’s predictive features remain largely stable across time and macro-
financial regimes. Contrary to prior work documenting pronounced temporal shifts in
predictor relevance, feature importance ranks exhibit remarkable consistency across
six economic periods spanning two decades. The top-ranked features maintain their
positions from the pre-crisis environment through the post-COVID period, though
our cumulative training methodology may dampen regime-specific signals.

B. Methodological Reflections
The findings carry implications for how machine learning might be positioned within
empirical finance research. We identify two complementary roles: as an exploratory
tool for informing traditional econometric specifications, and as a direct prediction
engine in domains where forecasting accuracy is the operational objective. For the
exploratory use case, it is worth noting that careful data analysis could uncover the
same relationships. The advantage of the ML + XAI approach is that it surfaces
relevant patterns systematically without requiring the researcher to know where to
look in advance. Three observations develop these themes.
   First, gradient-boosted models combined with XAI methods offer a form of
automated specification search. Rather than manually testing quadratic terms,
threshold dummies, and interaction effects, researchers can fit a flexible model and
examine SHAP dependence plots and ALE curves to understand what patterns
the data support. Our analysis provides three concrete illustrations. The binary

                                   28
Dividend Payer variable, which emerged as one of the most important predictors,
was itself identified through preliminary ALE analysis of ratios involving dividend
payouts; examination revealed that the only relevant signal was whether a dividend
was paid at all, not its magnitude, prompting the construction of a binary indicator
that would not have been specified ex ante. The Company Age ALE plot reveals a
year-zero effect that the linear specification missed; having identified this pattern, a
researcher could construct a binary indicator for newly founded firms and incorporate
it into a logistic regression, recovering the non-linearity within a fully interpretable
framework. Similarly, the near-binary behaviour of Net Profit Margin suggests that
Ohlson (1980)’s approach of including an indicator for consecutive losses captures
the essential signal, with continuous margin adding limited incremental information.
More generally, observing diminishing returns, plateaus, or U-shaped relationships
in ALE plots can motivate experimenting with feature transformations such as
logarithms, polynomials, or piecewise splines. The machine learning model thus
serves as an exploratory tool that informs subsequent confirmatory analysis.
   Second, SHAP interaction values provide a principled method for identifying
which variable pairs exhibit meaningful dependencies, and how those dependencies
operate. Where traditional approaches require researchers to specify interactions
ex ante based on theory or intuition, interaction heatmaps reveal empirically which
combinations matter and which do not. Once identified, these interactions can be
encoded into linear specifications through interaction terms, hierarchical random
slopes, or stratified estimation preserving interpretability while capturing the relevant
structure. This workflow addresses a long-standing challenge in applied econometrics:
the combinatorial explosion of possible interactions makes exhaustive testing infeasi-
ble, yet omitting important interactions biases coefficient estimates and degrades
predictions.
   Third, these methods warrant attention because prediction is often the opera-
tionally relevant objective across many domains in finance. Credit-risk modelling
is one example, but the same logic applies to fraud detection, where identifying
suspicious transactions matters more than understanding why fraud occurs; to al-
gorithmic trading, where forecasting short-term price movements is the objective;
and to insurance pricing, where accurate loss predictions determine premium-setting.
In such contexts, using tools optimised for prediction and being able to understand
what those tools learn is directly valuable. The growing availability of XAI methods
makes this combination increasingly practical across applied finance research.

C. Limitations
Several limitations warrant acknowledgment. Most fundamentally, gradient-boosted
decision trees and their associated XAI methods do not provide p-values or confidence
intervals in the traditional econometric sense. While SHAP values quantify feature
contributions and ALE plots reveal functional forms, these tools do not directly
address statistical significance. We cannot say, in the formal sense, whether the
threshold effect observed in Interest Coverage is “significant” or whether it might arise
from sampling variation. The machine learning paradigm optimises for predictive
accuracy, not inferential validity. The appropriate question shifts from “is this effect

                                   29
statistically significant?” to “does incorporating this pattern improve predictions on
unseen data?”, both questions are valuable, but they are not equivalent.
    Our reliance on publicly filed accounting data imposes several constraints. Market-
based structural models, which have shown strong performance for listed firms
(Jackson and Wood, 2013), require observable equity prices unavailable for privately
held SMEs. Primary lenders possess granular loan-level data and transaction histories
which they can use for internal models that we also lack access to. Additionally,
although the raw Serrano database contains fields for granted and utilised overdrafts,
we exclude these due to merging difficulty and data quality concerns, variables that
proved important in Cascarino et al. (2022).
   The temporal analysis is constrained by our cumulative training methodology
and by sample size. Alternative approaches such as rolling windows or period-specific
models might reveal more pronounced temporal variation, though at the cost of
smaller training samples. The relatively short post-COVID period (2022–2023) also
limits conclusions about the current high-interest-rate environment.
    Certain modelling choices warrant scrutiny. Industry SNI is encoded at the
3-digit SNI level, a granularity selected via analysis of variance but which may
introduce overfitting relative to coarser 1, or 2-digit classifications. The Term Spread
variable, while intended to capture macroeconomic conditions, takes a single value
per calendar year, effectively encoding time into the model. Its ALE plot exhibits a
counterintuitive slope, suggesting that the variable may be proxying for unobserved
temporal trends rather than capturing the intended yield-curve signal.
    Finally, our analysis is restricted to Swedish SMEs. While this focus enables clean
inference by holding institutional and accounting frameworks constant, it limits the
generalisability of specific findings. The importance rankings, threshold locations,
and interaction patterns we document may differ in other national contexts with
different bankruptcy regimes, accounting standards or firm characteristics.

D. Directions for Future Research
Several extensions appear promising. First, the concentration of interactions along
the industry dimension suggests value in developing sector-specific default models
or hierarchical frameworks that allow coefficients to vary across sectors. Such an
approach would clarify whether the non-linear patterns identified here are consistent
across sectoral contexts or reflect industry-specific dynamics.
    Second, the temporal dimension warrants further investigation. Our cumulative
training approach, while necessitated by sample size constraints, may dampen regime-
specific signals. Alternative methodologies such as time-weighted estimation that
downweights older observations, or rolling-window approaches applied to larger
datasets could assess whether the stability we observe reflects genuine constancy
in the underlying relationships or is an artifact of our estimation strategy. More
granular examination of the post-COVID period as additional data accumulate might
be particularly valuable.
    Third, while this paper estimates one-year default probabilities, many credit
decisions require longer-term assessments. Comparing one-, three- and five-year
prediction horizons would reveal how predictor importance and functional forms
evolve with the forecast horizon.

                                    30
   Fourth, replication on other national datasets would establish which findings
generalise and which are specific to the Swedish institutional context.

E. Concluding Remarks
This paper demonstrates that explainable AI methods can render gradient-boosted
decision trees interpretable in economically meaningful ways. The combination of
strong predictive performance with transparent explanations, revealing thresholds,
functional forms and interaction structures addresses the tension between accuracy
and interpretability that has limited machine learning adoption in regulated financial
contexts.
   The broader implication is that machine learning and econometrics need not be
viewed as competing paradigms. When the objective is prediction, flexible models
combined with post-hoc explanation methods offer capabilities that linear specifica-
tions cannot match, and crucially, XAI tools allow practitioners to understand what
these models learn, not merely to trust their outputs. Beyond prediction, such meth-
ods can also serve an exploratory function, surfacing non-linearities and interaction
structures that inform the specification of traditional econometric models. When
the objective is causal identification and hypothesis testing, traditional regression
with careful identification strategies remains essential. The growing maturity of XAI
tools makes this integration increasingly practical.

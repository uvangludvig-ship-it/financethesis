Abstract:
This study examines the return predictability of exchange-traded funds (ETFs)
across the U.S., European, and Asia-Pacific markets, based on primary market fund
flows, using both static and dynamic portfolio strategies. We show that the arbi-
trage activities of Authorized Participants (creation and redemption of ETF shares)
signal non-fundamental demand shocks and predict subsequent ETF price and NAV
returns. In the U.S., static portfolio analysis reveals significant variations among
ETF returns in terms of fund flow patterns, where ETFs with high share redemp-
tion outperform those with high share creation. At the same time, Europe exhibits
weaker return predictability, which can be reflected by higher arbitrage barriers
and lower market liquidity. The Asia-Pacific market shows weaker short-term pre-
dictability and stronger long-term effects, indicating more effective price correction
mechanisms compared to Europe but less efficiency than the U.S. We also show that
ETF price reversal mechanisms in the short-term can be used in hedging trading
strategies between ETFs and their underlying asset baskets.

1 Introduction

Exchange-traded funds (ETFs) have experienced tremendous growth since the first
                                                                               1
one launched in 1990. The global ETF market was worth $12 trillion   at the end
of 2023, growing from $5 trillion in 2016 (BlackRock, 2024). This rise in popularity
can be attributed to skepticism about active management, and that most investors
would benefit from low-cost, diversified portfolios that track broad market indices,
rather than attempting to outperform them (Duffy, Rabanal and Rud, 2021).

The primary advantages of ETFs, unlike mutual funds, are their lower fees and
greater trading flexibility. Unlike mutual funds, which are priced once daily after
market closing, ETFs can be bought and sold throughout the trading day, just like
individual stocks. Hence, the number of shares outstanding in ETFs can fluctuate.
This intraday liquidity allows investors to react quickly to market events, employ
advanced trading techniques, or hold their positions during short-term volatility in
hopes of profiting from long-term index tracking (Mazumder, 2014).

The ETF market is divided into two sub-markets: the primary market, where ETF
shares are created and redeemed by Authorized Participants (APs), and the sec-
ondary market, where retail investors buy and sell ETF shares on exchanges. The
creation and redemption activity in the primary market, known as ETF Flows, plays
a crucial role in the functioning of ETFs (Tsalikis and Papadopoulos, 2019).

The value of an ETF is determined by the underlying basket of securities that com-
prises its net asset value (NAV). While ETFs are designed to track specific indices,
their market prices can also be influenced by supply and demand dynamics. This
occurs due to the shared trading characteristics with stocks, which can lead to devi-
ations in an ETF’s price from its NAV (Ben-David, Franzoni, and Moussawi, 2018).
When the NAV and the market value of the ETF diverge, often because of fluctua-
tions in secondary market trading, this creates an arbitrage opportunity due to the
violation of the law of one price. APs play a crucial role in restoring equilibrium by
exploiting these arbitrage opportunities; they create or redeem ETF shares in the
primary market to bring the market price in line with the NAV. This process, known

Several studies have suggested that the layered trading structure of ETFs intro-
duces noise into the underlying asset pricing, creating non-fundamental demand
shocks that can drive the market value of an ETF away from its NAV (Ben-David
et al., 2018). This phenomenon has been researched for U.S. ETFs, particularly
using data from 2007 to 2016 (Brown, Davies and Ringgenberg, 2020).

Our primary research objective is to examine how trading in the primary market,
ETF flows, impacts ETF trading in the secondary market and their underlying asset
baskets, their NAV. Also, we aim to examine whether ETF flows are symptomatic of
non-fundamental demand shocks on either the ETFs or their underlying asset bas-
ket. More specifically, we investigate how non-fundamental demand shocks, signaled
by ETF flows, affect the ETF and its underlying securities and how it can serve as
a predictor of returns. Further, we explore this phenomenon in various markets,
mainly the U.S., Europe, and Asia-Pacific, to understand how regional differences
in market structure, investor behavior, and regulatory frameworks influence fund

Our research question is thus:

  How do ETF flows signal non-fundamental demand shocks to the ETF and its

              therefore predict subsequent ETF and NAV returns?

2 Literature Review and Contribution

2.1 Literature Review
Brown, Davies, and Ringgenberg (2020) find that trading by APs in the primary
market signals non-fundamental demand shocks in their study ”ETF Arbitrage,
Non-Fundamental Demand, and Return Predictability”. Brown et al. (2020) empir-
ically and theoretically study how ETF fund flows affect ETFs and their underlying
assets. The authors sort ETFs based on end-of-month values of primary market
fund flows and create portfolios with Decile 1 and Decile 10 ETFs. They hold these
portfolios for 1, 3, and 6 months. Results show significant return predictability of
ETF flows on subsequent returns of U.S. ETFs and their underlying assets. Brown
et al. (2020) conclude that creation and redemption activities in ETF flows act
as indicators of non-fundamental demand shocks and that their theoretical model
(from now on referred to as Brown’s Model ) is therefore correct. In this paper, we
build upon Brown’s model, which is further explained in Section 2.2.

Further, in a study done on mutual funds, Ben-Rephael, Kandel, and Wohl (2011)
examine intraday arbitrage in Israel. Authors find that mutual fund flows create
temporary price pressures, where approximately half of the price is reversed within
a month period. This shows that fund flows can work as a non-fundamental driver
for mutual funds in markets outside the U.S. Our study builds on this by examining
whether a similar pattern can be found in non U.S. markets for ETFs.

Similarly to Ben-Rephael et al. (2011), Box, Ryan, Evans, and Lynch (2021) examine
intraday ETF arbitrage in the U.S. market. The research concludes that we cannot
generally prove that intraday trading in ETFs transmits price noise or affects the
returns of their underlying portfolios through arbitrage mechanisms. Our study will
examine the transmission of price pressure through the arbitrage channel through
dynamic portfolios, where the effect of ETF flows, unlike in the static portfolios in
Brown et al. (2020), is studied on a short-term basis.

                                    4
With our dynamic portfolio strategy, we also intend to explore a hedged strategy
that incorporates long-short positions in both the ETF and its underlying assets.
Authors Huang, Shiyang, and O’Hara (2020), find that hedge funds increase hold-
ings in stocks with impending positive earnings announcements while simultaneously
shorting related industry ETFs, particularly for stocks with high industry exposure
(Huang et al., 2020). This ”long-the-stock/short-the-ETF” strategy enhances price
discovery by reducing the post-announcement drift commonly observed after posi-
tive earnings announcements.

Ben-David, Franzoni, and Moussawi (2018) find a systematic return premium linked
to ETF ownership. The authors argue that ETFs tend to attract high-frequency
investors, and ETF arbitrage activities create a new source of systematic risk, where
liquidity shocks in ETFs are transmitted to their underlying securities. As a result,
ETF increase the non-fundamental volatility of the securities in their underlying bas-
kets, concluding that increased ETF ownership in a stock induces higher volatility
(Ben-David et al., 2018). This insight contributes to our discussion of the trans-
mission of non-fundamental demand shocks between ETFs and underlying assets
caused by ETF flows.

To better understand the non-U.S. markets we include studies, by Lee, Tseng, and
Yang (2014) and Hilliard and Jitka (2014), to discuss intercontinental differences
in the ETF ecosystem between the U.S. and European markets. Lee et al. (2014)
observes that European markets and financial instruments are comparatively less
liquid, contributing to increased volatility. Hilliard (2014) find that European ETFs
have higher premiums, driven by limited arbitrage opportunities. Building on these
findings, Bertone, Paeglis, and Ravi (2015) establish a link between price efficiency
and liquidity and conclude that higher liquidity and lower market frictions enable
investors to exploit arbitrage opportunities and correct ETF mispricing. Lastly, Pan
and Zeng (2016), provide a foundation that the liquidity mismatch can the impair
market efficiency of the ETF pricing.

Lastly, Staer and Arsenio (2010) observe a correlation between ETF flow and price
pressure. Clifford, Fulkerson, and Jordan (2014) investigate what drives ETF flows
and find that past returns and high volumes are good predictors of ETF flows. We
build on these studies while focusing on APs and how their activity are signals of
non-fundamental returns.

2.2 Contribution
Our study builds on Brown et al.’s findings, extending the analysis to broader mar-
kets, more recent time frames, and additional portfolio strategies. We introduce a
new sample period spanning from 2016 to 2023, compared to Brown et al.’s earlier
focus on U.S. data from 2007 to 2016. This extension allows us to examine whether
major global events, such as the COVID-19 pandemic and the Russia-Ukraine war,
have altered market dynamics and assess whether Brown’s model remains robust
both before and after these disruptions. By expanding the geographic scope to nine
markets across three continents including; Canada, Ireland, Luxembourg, Germany,

                                    5
France, Switzerland, United Kingdom, Hong Kong and Singapore, we aim to pro-
vide a more comprehensive evaluation of the model’s applicability. Additionally,
we broaden the scope of return and holding period intervals by adding nine- and
twelve-month periods to the existing one-, three- and six-month time horizons.

A significant contribution of our work is an additional approach to the portfolio
construction strategy by Brown et al. (2020), addressing limitations in Brown et
al.’s methodology. Their portfolio strategy is static –meaning portfolios are created
based on information during a certain month and remain unchanged, regardless of
subsequent changes in fund flows for the return period. This strategy is incorporated
in the first part of our study, while in the second part, the portfolios are recalibrated
monthly. This is to ensure that each decile consistently reflects the current levels of
ETF flows. By updating the portfolios monthly, we capture shifts in flow patterns,
and non-fundamental demand shocks that may impact ETF performance. This
dynamic approach addresses the limitations of Brown’s model, which assumes ETFs
revert to their fundamental value without additional shocks. In reality, an ETF
may appear overvalued due to a non-fundamental shock and fall into the Decile 10
portfolio with high creation activity; however, within a few months, new activity
could undervalue it, moving it to Decile 1. This dynamic portfolio structure enables
a more precise examination to address the research question, of whether ETF flows
can predict the returns of ETFs and their underlying assets, as well as address
questions related to price pressure induced by AP activity.

3 Theory and Model

In this section, we establish the theoretical foundation by drawing on Brown et al.
(2020) and other essential sources. This foundation will provide a comprehensive
framework for understanding the core concepts underlying this research.

3.1 ETF Flow and the Law of One Price
In Brown et al. (2020), ”ETF flows” are defined as the arbitrage activities in the pri-
mary market, executed through the creation and redemption of ETF shares. Unlike
mutual funds, ETFs are open-ended, allowing for an unlimited issuance of shares
and enabling fluctuations in their assets under management (AUM). This flexibility
in share issuance, driven by ETF flows in the primary market, is prompted by dis-
crepancies in pricing.

According to the law of one price, two assets: A and B, that consist of the same
underlying assets should have the same fundamental value, such as A = B. When
this equality is violated, resulting in A ̸= B, the absolute difference (|A–B|) signals
a non-fundamental shock, which leads to mispricing (Brown et al., 2020). In the
context of ETFs, asset A represents the ETF’s market price, and asset B represents
its intrinsic or fundamental value (NAV, or its book value). The underlying assets
that back the ETF shares are designed to mirror a benchmark index. Each unit of
these underlying assets has an unobservable fundamental value at time t, denoted
as χt (i.e., NAV), and a tradable value, denoted as Pt at time t. The difference
(|Pt − χt| = α), known as the ETF premium, provides a basis for arbitrage trades

The non-fundamental shock causing this mispricing can arise from several sources.
It may originate from an over- or under-reaction to earnings-related news (Brown
et al., 2020). Alternatively, the shock may be driven by non-fundamental factors,
such as liquidity trades and market noise (Ben-David et al., 2018).

3.2 Authorized Participants
When price discrepancies between the ETF and its underlying asset basket are ex-
ploited as arbitrage opportunities, non-fundamental shocks are corrected over the
long term, restoring the ETF’s price to its fundamental value, see Figure 3 (Brown
et al., 2020). These trades are executed by APs, typically institutional investors,
such as hedge funds, in coordination with ETF issuers as BlackRock.

The redemption/creation process is initiated when APs identify that the NAV of
the ETF’s underlying assets, denoted as χt, is higher/lower than the market price
of the ETF, represented as Pt. If χt < Pt, the ETF is overvalued relative to its
fundamental value and is trading at a discount. In this case, there will be creation
of ETF shares. APs buy the underlying securities in the secondary market and
sell them to the ETF issuer in the primary market, in exchange for newly created
ETF shares as seen in Figure 2. Subsequently, APs sell these ETF Shares in the
secondary market, where their higher price relative to χt generates arbitrage profits.
This creation increases the total number of ETF shares, decreasing its share price,
at the same time the buying pressure on the underlying assets further reduces the
gap, ultimately restoring χt = Pt, see Figure 3 (Brown et al., 2020). Conversely,
when χt > Pt, the opposite will apply for redemption; where the number of ETF

shares decreases, the NAV increases, ultimately restoring χt = Pt.

3.3 Brown’s ETF model and model limitations
With Brown’s theoretical ETF model described above, a couple of remarks are noted,
among others the following:

   1. ETF flows are symptomatic of non-fundamental demand distortions.

   2. There are two potential sources of price distortions for the ETF share price
      and the ETF underlying asset prices. The first is latent demand shocks. The
      second is a transmission mechanism via the primary market arbitrageurs.

   3. ETF flows signal a fundamental mispricing which almost always reverses sub-
      sequently.

   4. If ETF share demand is more sensitive to shocks compared to the demand
     for underlying assets, then ETF flows will negatively predict returns. The
      opposite also applies.

   5. The return predictability of ETF share returns and its underlying asset basket
      (NAV) returns should be in the same direction. Either both are positive or
      both are negative.

However, the model acquires assumptions and limitations discussed below.

   1. The model assumes that the cost of arbitrage is zero. However, arbitrage
      opportunities might be costly for APs and therefore not all arbitrage opportu-
      nities are restored once they happen (Brown et al., 2020). APs might have to
     choose between multiple opportunities and they will choose the opportunity
      with the highest return for them. This has geographical implications and may
      explain differences in results between countries.

   2. Different ETFs may hold the same security. Arbitrage opportunities in one
     ETF can hence affect another ETF.

   3. The model cannot differentiate between different types of non-fundamental
     demand. ETF flows signal non-fundamental demand without telling us about
     the nature of that demand.

   4. In Brown’s model, an ETF is assumed to have zero, or almost none, fund flows
      in the long-term after t = 2 in Figure 3 (Brown et al., 2020).

4.2 Data Cleaning
We follow a data cleaning methodology similar to that of Brown et al. (2020). First,
we address potential issues of illiquidity and non-synchronous pricing due to infre-
quent trading by restricting our sample to ETFs with at least $1 million in market
capitalization and 12 consecutive months of data. This approach allows us to as-
sess returns after holding periods of 12 months or less. Unlike Brown et al., who
set a $50 million threshold in market capitalization, we adopt a lower threshold to
accommodate the broader global market we cover. This adjustment helps us retain
a more comprehensive data set so that we do not lose coverage in the regions with
smaller ETFs.

The ETF data from Refinitiv Eikon is provided at a daily frequency. However, as
Brown et al. (2020) emphasize, it is preferable to aggregate this data into monthly
intervals for ETF arbitrage studies. This approach accounts for the fact that APs
may delay necessary creations or redemptions of ETF shares for up to six days, po-
tentially causing temporary misalignment between ETF prices and their underlying
asset values. This waiting period allows APs to benefit from failure-to-deliver rules,
thus delaying the appearance of arbitrage in daily data. Hence, daily fund flow data
have implied noise and will not always reflect arbitrage opportunities that occur on
the same date and the fund flow.

In cases of missing or anomalous data, where for instance monthly values increase
by more than 100% and then revert to normal, the affected values are replaced using
backward and forward averages, as described by Brown et al. (2020). Further, the
dataset from Refinitiv Eikon includes multiples of the same ETF, as certain ETFs
trade on multiple stock exchanges globally and therefore appear in Eikon under var-
ious listings. For example, ETF A may appear as ETF A (LON), ETF A (LIM),
and ETF A (SGO), with the stock exchange tickers being the only differences in
the names. To address this, a script is implemented where a dictionary collects all
occurrences of the same ETF across different stock exchanges, ensuring that only

4.4 Data Limitations
Our analysis relies on data extracted from Refinitiv Eikon with Datastream, which
introduces certain limitations. Unlike the model used in Brown et al. (2020), which
combines Bloomberg and CRSP data to complement each other, we use a single
data source. Their dual-source approach allows for substituting missing values be-
tween datasets, and benefits from Bloomberg’s more frequent updates on shares
outstanding.

The European market
For Europe, price returns are detailed in Table 7, while NAV returns are presented

A potential explanation lies in the correctness of model assumptions in the U.S.
market versus the European market. Brown’s model assumes zero arbitrage costs
and no arbitrage barriers, which aligns with their results for U.S. static portfolios in
Tables 6 and 5. However, many studies suggest that ETF arbitrage barriers would
generally be higher for European markets compared to U.S. markets, especially if
the ETFs are traded in USD. For instance, Lee et al. (2014) argues that ETFs for
European countries are illiquid compared to countries of other regions. Among other
things, the European ETF market is characterized by fragmentation across multiple
exchanges and jurisdictions compared to the U.S. market (Fioravanti and Gentile,
2011). This dispersion leads to liquidity being spread thinly across various trading
venues, resulting in wider bid-ask spreads and less efficient pricing mechanisms. In
contrast, the U.S. market benefits from more centralized trading, which enhances
liquidity and pricing efficiency. These contrasts in efficiency between Europe and
the U.S. may explain our results.

Additionally, the European ETF market features a lower proportion of retail in-

the ETFs can be traded across multiple exchanges regardless of their domicile. While
market liquidity in countries without bans may not have directly changed, the pric-

hindered by deteriorated liquidity on other exchanges.

negatively impacts trading efficiency compared to the more consolidated U.S. mar-

challenge due to venue specialization and the distribution of trades across multiple
infrastructures (European Securities and Markets Authority, 2023).

The motivation behind this portfolio method is that results in this dynamic study
have less necessity for the fourth and most extreme assumption in Section 3.3 –where
the ETF is assumed to not be subject to any future non-fundamental demand shock
in the long-term after t = 2, as seen in Figure 3 (Brown et al., 2020). Imagine a
highly volatile and liquid ETF that experiences high fund flows many months in a
row. That ETF will not experience a price reversion to fundamental values during
those months, and hence the static portfolio method will not capture these effects.

With a dynamic portfolio strategy, we aim to capture the more short-term effects
of fund flows. In Figure 3, that would be around t = 2. This portfolio strategy will
hence allow us to capture the effects that Ben-David et al. (2018) studies –where

                                   19
non-fundamental demand and price noise is transmitted through the arbitrage mech-
anism from the ETF to its underlying assets. Thus the expectation is that ETF price
returns and NAV returns move in opposite directions, just like in t = 2 in Figure 3
(Brown et al., 2020). Moreover, we will explore potential hedging opportunities im-
plied by the above-mentioned contrast between NAV, and ETF share price returns
in the short term, which has been noted earlier by Huang et al. (2020).

5.3 Robustness Checks with Panel Regressions

markets to determine if prior results still hold through panel regressions inspired by
Brown et al. (2020). In this case, we perform regressions where the dependent

difference between an ETF and its NAV. We also incorporate time-fixed effects and

6 Limitations and Future Work

This study has limitations that should be further addressed and explored in future
work to deepen the understanding of the findings and extend their applicability.
Here, we outline several key areas for future research.

First, the stark contrast in results between the European and U.S. ETF markets war-
rants further investigation. This study identifies the disparity and provides plausible
explanations grounded in existing literature and statistical analysis of the frequency
of ETFs in extreme deciles. However, deeper exploration remains necessary. Future
research should undertake more comprehensive statistical and econometric analy-
ses, focusing on structural differences in ETF ecosystems, regulatory frameworks,
liquidity conditions, and AP behaviors.

Second, this study focuses on the two largest Asia-Pacific markets with USD-traded
ETFs. However, incorporating additional emerging markets offers the potential to
uncover valuable insights into how regional variations, market maturity, and liquid-
ity conditions affect return predictability. Including countries with diverse economic
structures and ETF trading volumes, may reveal patterns that remain obscured
within the current scope of the analysis.

Third, similar to the approach taken by Brown et al. (2020), future research should
explore distinct subsamples of ETFs, such as leveraged versus unleveraged funds or
actively managed ETFs. This could help uncover whether certain types of ETFs
exhibit stronger or weaker return predictability, and how these patterns differ across
regions. Moreover, ETFs focusing on specific industries, such as technology, health-
care, or energy, could exhibit unique characteristics that influence their pricing dy-
namics and susceptibility to demand shocks. Understanding how these industry-
specific funds interact with broader market trends may provide critical insights into
the observed differences between regions.

Fourth, future research could explore how investor behavior and types of investors
vary across markets and influence return predictability. Understanding the demo-
graphic, institutional, and retail investor mix in each region may clarify the observed
market inefficiencies and liquidity differences.

Finally, a promising avenue for future work lies in investigating the hedging oppor-
tunities presented by ETFs, particularly in the U.S. market. As ETFs are often
used as tools for hedging against broader market movements or specific sector risks,
understanding how their return predictability can inform hedging strategies could be
invaluable for both institutional and retail investors. By examining the relationship

                                   29
between ETF flows and the effectiveness of hedging, future studies could uncover
mechanisms that leverage the predictability of returns to mitigate risk exposure.
This analysis would not only extend the practical implications of return predictabil-
ity but also contribute to understanding the broader utility of ETFs in portfolio
management.

7 Conclusions

This study investigates the return predictability of ETF flows across three ma-
jor markets—U.S., Europe, and Asia-Pacific, through static and dynamic portfolio
strategies, supplemented by robustness checks using panel regressions. By analyz-
ing these distinct methodologies, we contribute to the growing body of literature on
the predictive power of non-fundamental demand shocks, signaled by ETF flows, on
subsequent ETF price and NAV returns.

To address our research question, our static portfolio analysis reveals strong evi-
dence of return predictability in the U.S. market, aligning with Brown et al.’s (2020)
model. Significant long-short portfolio returns demonstrate that Decile 1, ETFs with
high redemptions, outperform Decile 10, ETFs with high creations. This pattern
supports the hypothesis that ETF flows capture non-fundamental demand shocks.
However, this predictability is more pronounced for longer holding periods, suggest-
ing a delayed reversion to fundamental values due to persistent market volatility.
Our study hence confirms that Brown’s Model remains valid and persists despite sig-
nificant global shocks, including the COVID-19 pandemic and the Russian invasion
of Ukraine. However, the latter is likely to have had a greater impact on European
markets.

However, in Europe, the static portfolio results are less pronounced, reflecting the
fragmented and less liquid nature of the market. The results show limited statistical
significance, highlighting inefficiencies caused by higher arbitrage barriers and reg-
ulatory constraints. Similarly, the Asia-Pacific market displays weaker significant
results in the short-term, while showing more significant results for both ETF price
and NAV returns in long-term horizons. This indicates greater trading efficiency
compared to Europe but less so than the U.S. The higher liquidity in Asia-Pacific
ETFs likely facilitates better price correction mechanisms.

The dynamic portfolio study provides additional insights, showcasing how recali-
brated portfolios capture short-term effects of ETF flows. In the U.S., significant
positive and negative returns for ETF prices and NAVs, respectively, underline the
role of APs in transmitting non-fundamental shocks between ETFs and their un-
derlying assets. The European results lack the inverse relationship between price
and NAV, highlighting market inefficiencies. In Asia-Pacific, dynamic portfolio re-
sults suggest that large ETFs dominate return predictability, while smaller ETFs
contribute to volatility, reflecting a market structure between that of the U.S. and
Europe.

The insights derived from this study have significant implications for understanding

                                   30
ETF market dynamics and arbitrage mechanisms. By highlighting the disparities in
arbitrage efficiency and return predictability across regions, this research provides
a foundation for future studies. Specifically, our findings emphasize the need for
deeper investigations into the root causes of these disparities, the impact of investor
behavior, and the role of industry-specific ETFs in shaping market dynamics. Ex-
panding the scope to include additional emerging markets and subsamples of ETFs
will further enrich the understanding of global ETF ecosystems.

In conclusion, our study not only validates existing theoretical models but also
extends their applicability across diverse markets. The results pave the way for
more nuanced research on ETF flows, market inefficiencies, and their implications
for global investment strategies.

We examine whether a dynamic trading strategy can yield abnormal returns. Ta-
ble 27 shows that this strategy produces statistically significant abnormal returns
across all regions, with increasing magnitude and significance for longer holding pe-
riods. For value-weighted portfolios, the U.S. exhibits an expected alpha of 3.39%,
Asia-Pacific 14.2%, and Europe -7.62% over 12 months. The results suggest that in
the U.S. and Asia-Pacific, holding the portfolio for more than six months leads to
cumulative and increasingly significant abnormal returns, indicating inefficiency in
pricing fund flows.

In Europe, the strategy generates progressively negative returns over time, poten-
tially explained by factors outlined in Brown’s model (Remark 4). Additionally, the
data period coinciding with the Ukrainian invasion may have contributed to these
regional anomalies.

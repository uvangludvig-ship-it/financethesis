Truckloads of Power: Unpacking Concentration in U.S. Trucking

Abstract:

This thesis investigates the implications of increasing market concentration in the U.S. trucking
industry, focusing on the profitability drivers asset utilization and market power during the
2001–2014 period. Drawing on comprehensive regression analyses and a deep-dive industry
approach, the study examines whether profitability gains stem from efficiency improvements
or firms leveraging market power. Findings reveal a significant positive relationship between
concentration and profitability, driven primarily by increased profit margins as captured by the
Lerner Index, rather than operational efficiencies measured by asset utilization. Contrary to
initial hypotheses, the trucking industry's profitability aligns with aggregate market trends,
where market power appears to dominate over efficiency gains as the source of abnormal
profits. However, the results show a decreased magnitude of the profit margin effect. This is
speculated to be attributed to the minimization of efficiency gains captured by the Lerner index
prominent in other industries. This study contributes to the broader debate on market
consolidation's economic impact and provides insights for policymakers and corporate
managers on navigating the balance between efficiency and market power in a concentrated
market landscape.

Market competition, Mergers and acquisitions, Market power, Concentration levels, U.S.
Trucking Industry, Profitability drivers

1. Introduction
Over the past few decades, the US economy has experienced a notable rise in market
concentration across various industries, a trend that has become particularly pronounced in
recent years and garnered significant attention from policymakers, economists, and academics.
While some view this trend as a natural evolution of competitive markets, others see it as a
potential threat to economic equality and consumer welfare. Proponents argue that larger firms
benefit from economies of scale, reduced production costs, and improved efficiency, potentially
delivering better services and lower prices to consumers. Conversely, critics assert that
consolidation diminishes competition, increases prices, and stifles innovation, ultimately
harming consumer welfare. Against this backdrop, this thesis explores the complexities of
rising concentration, examining its consequences, and industry-specific nuances to assess its
broader implications for economic and social welfare.
A rich body of literature has sought to address these questions at an aggregate level. One of the
most notable contributions is by Grullon, Larkin, and Michaely (2019), who provide a
comprehensive analysis of the effects of market concentration across the U.S. economy. Their
findings reveal that, contrary to optimistic views, increased concentration has predominantly
negative consequences. As market power becomes concentrated in fewer hands, consumer
welfare tends to erode. Instead of realizing efficiency gains, firms with greater market power
often engage in rent-seeking behaviors, prioritizing profit maximization over productivity
improvements. Other scholars have also explored this area. Autor, Dorn, Katz, Patterson, and
Van Reenen (2020) present an alternative perspective, attributing the phenomenon to the
emergence of “superstar firms”. These are companies that, through superior technology,
innovation, or operational strategies, dominate their industries and accumulate significant
market power.

The two views differ significantly in both the root causes and the following implications of
increasing concentration. The market power view attributes the trend to intended actions, whilst
the other explains a more organic development. In this thesis, we will examine whether rising
concentration across industries drives profitability solely by market power or if alternative
mechanisms are at play. The findings will contribute to the finance literature by challenging
the notion that increased concentration through actions such as aggressive mergers and
acquisitions (M&A) inherently lead to increased market power and ability to leverage pricing.
Instead, this study will explore the possibility that increased concentration has material
efficiency benefits. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for both policymakers and
corporate managers when deciding what actions to implement.

The statistical analyses in this thesis focus on the period 2001-2014. This timeframe was chosen
because Grullon et al. (2019) identified a statistically significant and positive correlation
between concentration and profitability during these years. By aligning with this period, the
analysis ensures comparability with existing literature while allowing for a focused
investigation of trends within a specific context. Furthermore, we narrow the scope of the
analysis to the trucking industry. We argue that the trucking industry provides a suitable
industry for a deep-dive analysis by offering a more accurate separation of efficiency and
market power into observable proxies. Unlike other industries, we believe several distinct
reasons make it attractive to investigate: 1) Efficiency gains from technological developments
have affected the industry without inflating profit margins; 2) The industry is characterized by

1
dependency on tangible assets; and 3) The industry has limited opportunities for outsourcing
of operations and is regionally bound. Thus, the U.S. trucking industry offers an alternative
lens to examine concentration trends.

We hypothesized that market concentration would be a driver of profitability within the
trucking industry similarly to the aggregate market. However, given the distinct structure of
the trucking industry, asset utilization is proposed to offer a clearer indicator of efficiency. As
such, we further hypothesized that asset utilization would be the primary driver of profitability
in the trucking industry unlike many other industries where efficiency gains are often absorbed
into profit margins.

The initial analysis suggests that profitability in the trucking industry does not exhibit a positive
correlation with increased market concentration, diverging from the conclusions found in
existing literature. This challenges the prevailing notion that consolidation boosts firm
performance through market power. However, a closer examination reveals that the analyzed
period coincides with extraordinary economic conditions, including the recovery from the dot-
com bubble and the 2008 financial crisis. During this time, the trucking industry faced
substantial external pressures, such as volatile fuel prices and fluctuating demand, which may
have influenced the results.

Controlling for external factors reveals a statistically significant positive correlation between
concentration and profitability. The analysis further shows that this profitability gain is
primarily driven by increased profit margins rather than improved asset utilization.
Specifically, a shift in concentration from the 25th to the 75th percentile results in a 20.4%
increase in profit margins, compared to only a 5.1% increase for asset utilization. Additionally,
the correlation between the Lerner index, a proxy for profit margins, and concentration is highly
significant, whereas the correlation for asset utilization is significant only at the 90%
confidence level. The results contradicted our initial beliefs, as the efficiency effect was not the
prominent profitability driver. Further, while these findings align with Grullon et al. (2019) in
some respects, the effect on the Lerner index is substantially lower than what is observed at the
aggregate level. We suggest that this outcome stems from the unique characteristics of the
trucking industry, where efficiency gains are less likely to be reflected in the Lerner index.

We further considered the possibility of composition changes of actors materially affecting the
relationships within the industry. The analysis revealed significant events in the industry that
affected both concentration and profitability. The most prominent event concerned the largest
actor, Yellow, who suffered financial distress. As the development potentially distorted results,
a robustness test excluding Yellow was conducted. The exclusion did not have material effect
on the results, strengthening our findings’ validity.

Lastly, we examined the composition of goods and services provided, revealing an increasingly
diverse mix of transported commodities and a rise in dispersed shipments driven by e-
commerce. To assess whether this trend materially impacted our results, we included a
concentration index of commodity types. While the results showed significant differences to
previous tests, data limitations prevented further investigation into the specific effects.
Furthermore, the model experienced high levels of multicollinearity raising issues with its
explanatory power.

In summary, this thesis contributes to the ongoing debate on market concentrations effect on
profitability by examining its implications in the trucking industry. By exploring the interplay

the aggregate trends identified in existing literature. The findings indicate that market power is

2. Hypothesis Development
   2.1. Profitability and Market Concentration
Examining factors influencing profitability is a highly relevant field for finance literature,
driven by its critical implications for corporate strategy and policymaking. Research has
consistently sought to understand the various determinants that affect a firm's ability to generate
profits, such as market structure, competitive behavior, and strategic management decisions.
However, the relationship between market concentration and profitability has over the decades
remained an area with mixed conclusions.

Historically, the relationship between market concentration and price-cost margins has been
examined by Domowitz, Hubbard, and Petersen (1986a, 1986b, 1987). The authors focused on
how business cycles affect price-cost margins in industries of varying concentration, finding
that highly concentrated industries often leverage pricing power to maintain profitability during
economic fluctuations. A separate study by Schmalensee (1989) analyzed U.S. manufacturing
from 1953 to 1983, showing that firm-specific factors like efficiency and market position drive
profitability differences more than industry-wide concentration. His findings also reveal that
larger firms' profitability advantages have declined over time. Together, these studies highlight
the complex interplay of industry conditions and firm-level traits in shaping the relationship
between market concentration and profitability.

In a more recent study, Grullon et al. (2019) reveal a significant rise in U.S. industry
concentration since the late 1990s, linked to higher profitability. The profitability gains were
driven by profit margins rather than improved asset efficiency. Unlike earlier studies by
Domowitz et al. and Schmalensee, which showed weak correlations between concentration and
profitability, they find a strong positive link in 2001 to 2014, with earlier periods (1972-1986
and 1987-2000) showing little or no correlation. This finding suggests that recent increased
concentration has been accompanied by the tendency to leverage market power to achieve
greater profitability through markups. Such action entails raising prices due to decreased
competitive pressure, without improving efficiency. The authors further explain that high
barriers to entry amplify this effect, allowing incumbents to secure profits through structural
advantages rather than enhancements in operations or product quality. However, they
acknowledge that while market power is a plausible explanation, it is not definitively proven
that this is the sole cause of higher profitability.

That the U.S. market has undergone a noticeable shift toward fewer publicly listed companies
and increased market concentration has been extensively documented in both academic and
policy literature. The number of public firms has declined substantially since the late 1990s,
dropping from over 8,000 in 1996 to approximately 4,300 by 2019 (EY & ACCF, 2022). The

3
high degree of consolidation has raised concerns about its effects on competition, consumers,
and the broader economy. In line with the findings of Grullon et al., an article by Philippon
(2019) finds that business concentration and profit margins have notably increased across most
U.S. industries over the past two decades. This trend has been associated with weaker
productivity growth, a decline in investment rates, and a shift in market dynamics that favor
incumbent firms.

Another modern perspective is provided by Autor et al. (2020) highlighting how the rise of
"superstar firms" – highly productive companies with large market shares and substantial
intangible assets – has driven increased market concentration. These firms dominate by
offering superior products or services that meet higher consumer expectations, leveraging
technological advantages to capture a greater share of industry sales. This trend is linked to
rising markups, profitability, and a significant decline in the labor share of income in industries
with the most concentration growth, driven by the efficiency and scale of these firms requiring
less labor relative to output.

Supporting the view of superstars, Syverson (2011) highlights that in industries with strong
competitive pressures, higher market concentration often results from firms enhancing
productivity and differentiating their products to maintain or expand their market positions.
Firms facing these pressures invest heavily in improving productivity and quality, which helps
them build consumer trust and loyalty. This focus on productivity and quality allows firms to
sustain or increase margins without relying on anti-competitive practices. Larger firms, in
particular, benefit from economies of scale that enable substantial investments in technology
and advanced production processes, allowing them to produce superior products that can
command premium pricing.

    2.2. Our Contribution
Existing literature extensively examines the relationship between market concentration and
profitability, with notable findings from Grullon et al. (2019), who identified a significant
positive correlation during the 2001–2014 period. They attributed this correlation largely to
market power, highlighting that increased pricing power, measured through the Lerner index,
had a more substantial impact on profitability (ROA) than asset efficiency. Their conclusion,
however, leaves room for further examination, as factors such as the rise of superstar firms and
the influence of economies of scale and R&D investments (Autor et al. 2020; Syverson, 2011)
complicate the narrative. These nuances call into question whether profitability increases are
truly driven by market power alone.

We identify a critical gap in the literature regarding the suitability of the Lerner index as a
proxy for market power and asset utilization as a measure of efficiency. At the aggregate level,
efficiency gains can lower operational costs and inflate the Lerner index without significantly
affecting asset utilization. To address this limitation, we propose focusing on an industry where
these metrics more accurately represent market power and efficiency to allow for a clearer
distinction between the effects of the on profitability.

Our study aims to determine whether the relationship between concentration and profitability
holds in such a context. Specifically, we investigate whether profitability gains caused by
increasing concentration is driven by market power or by efficiency gains. By going deeper
into these dynamics, we hope to clarify the underlying mechanisms that contribute to rising
profit margins. Furthermore, we adopt a granular approach to examine how industry-specific

4
factors such as cost shocks, demand, market structure, and the characteristics of products and
services in an industry, may influence financial metrics and concentration effects.

This research contributes to existing literature in several ways. First, it provides a clearer
framework for disentangling the contributions of market power and efficiency to profitability,
offering more precise insights into their respective roles. Second, it gives insight into the
strategic implications for M&A. If market power proves to be the dominant factor, our findings
would support the increasing importance of market consolidation as a driver of sustainable
profitability, reinforcing the rationale for M&A as a tool for reducing competition. However, if
efficiency gains emerge as the primary driver, the results would highlight the value of scale,
synergies, and operational improvements post-merger. Finally, our findings offer broader
insights into how industry-specific characteristics influence the profitability dynamics of
market concentration. Ultimately, this study aims to contribute to the understanding of the
relationship between concentration and profitability by providing a more robust analysis of the
roles played by market power and efficiency.

    2.3. U.S. Trucking Industry
Technological advancements in recent decades have significantly reshaped market dynamics.
Acemoglu and Restrepo (2017) highlight how automatization has boosted productivity and
increased demand for technical roles, while Brynjolfsson and McAfee (2014) emphasize that
these innovations have enabled firms to reduce costs and adapt more effectively to competitive
environments. However, such efficiency gains are often captured through cost-effective
operations rather than traditional metrics such as asset utilization. To address this, we focus on
an industry where efficiency gains are more accurately reflected in asset utilization, allowing
for a clearer distinction between efficiency and market power. The approach is discussed
further in Section 3.1.

Existing literature further suggests that technological developments affect asset-heavy and
asset-light industries in contrasting ways. Asset-light industries mainly leverage new
technology to enhance flexibility and prioritize knowledge-driven capabilities such as R&D
and branding, whilst asset-heavy industries emphasize improving maintenance, tool cost
management, and machine hour-based cost allocations to maximize asset utilization (Yu, 2018;
Bruggeman & Slagmulder, 1995). The technological developments will therefore have
efficiency gains better captured by asset utilization than asset-light industries. Furthermore,
asset-light industries are more susceptible to the mentioned superstar firm effect – where highly
productive and profitable firms capture market share, causing distortions between the relation
of concentration and profitability. This reinforces the idea that asset utilization serves as a more
reliable proxy for efficiency gains in asset-heavy industries. These dynamics are explored
further in Section 3.2.

In parallel with technological advancements, the structure and dynamics of U.S. and global
markets have been significantly reshaped by increasing globalization in recent decades
(Congressional Research Service, 2013). A key aspect of this transformation has been increased
outsourcing and offshoring of core processes such as manufacturing and logistics to low-wage
countries, enabling firms to reduce costs and adopt new production strategies. These efficiency
gains of firms’ cost structures will inflate the Lerner index, affecting its accuracy as a proxy for
market power. Analyzing industries with limited outsourcing and offshoring could therefore

5
provide a clearer lens for distinguishing between efficiency-driven and market power-driven
profitability. This distinction is further explored in Section 3.3.

By focusing on an industry with minimal automatization, with asset-heavy characteristics, and
limited outsourcing/offshoring, we aim to extend the framework proposed by Grullon et al.
(2019). This approach seeks to isolate the drivers of profitability in concentrated markets under
specific constraints, offering nuanced insights into the interplay between market power and
efficiency. If such an industry exhibits increased profitability driven by market power, properly
proxied by the Lerner index, it would reinforce the argument that concentration has become a
key determinant of profitability without corresponding efficiency gains. This finding would
contribute to the broader discourse on the economic implications of rising concentration. Our
study provides a framework for disentangling market power and efficiency, advancing the
understanding of how concentration affects profitability across varying industry contexts.
To examine how the trend of increased concentration impacts such an industry, we focus on
the U.S. trucking industry. This industry provides a compelling context for exploring Grullon
et al.'s findings for several reasons: 1) the efficiency gains from technological developments
are primarily captured in asset utilization, 2) the industry is characterized by tangible assets,
and 3) it has limited possibilities for outsourcing and offshoring. For a more detailed
explanation of these points and support for our arguments, see Section 3. We believe that unlike
many industries in the U.S. that do not have these specific features, the potential efficiency
gain will be properly captured in the asset utilization metric in the trucking industry, thereby
preventing skewed interpretations of market power versus efficiency. A detailed definition of
what constitutes the trucking industry is provided in Section 4.1.

To further determine if trucking is a suitable industry to analyze, recent trends of concentration
in the aggregate market and the trucking industry have been compared. Similarity in
concentration trends is essential to compare its effects. The results in Figure 1 are based on the
Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI). Clarification of this concentration metric and its
construction is available in Section 4.2. Panel A shows a steady rise in sales weighted HHI
since 1985, with a sharp increase in the period 1997-2002, mirroring the trend identified by
Grullon et al. (2019). Notably, the figure shows that this upward trajectory in the HHI has
persisted beyond 2014. Panel B displays the HHI for the trucking industry, reflecting trends in
industry concentration without the sales-weighted adjustments applied in Panel A. The
concentration does not follow the same clear upward trend throughout the period. However,
some notable similarities exist. Panel C highlights development in the period 2001-2014, which
is in focus. During this period, the trucking industry shows an upward trend in HHI, barring
decreases in the period 2008-2010. Although the absolute values differ in magnitude, both
industries share an increase in concentration. Thus, our focus on the trucking industry remains
valid.

    2.1. Hypotheses Formulation
This study proposes three hypotheses to explore the relationship between market concentration
and financial performance in the trucking industry. We believe that increased concentration
does entail major efficiency gains, but that these efficiency gains have been better captured by
the Lerner index in the findings of Grullon et al. (2019). As such, we expect concentration to
influence financial performance differently in the trucking industry, and believe that asset
utilization will be a stronger driver than proposed in previous literature.

3. Structural Dynamics in Trucking
This section intends to support the notion that asset utilization and the Lerner index serve as
more precise proxies for measuring efficiency and market power within the trucking industry
compared to aggregate-level analyses of the metrics. The section starts with discussing
automatization and technological development, followed by a discussion on tangible and
intangible asset dependencies. Lastly, the roles of outsourcing and offshoring are discussed. As
such, we aim to expand on why our analysis contributes to the subject on efficiency and market
power.

    3.1. Automatization and Technological Development
In early 2000’s, the United States experienced a surge in automation, particularly with
industrial robot usage quadrupling, driven by technological advancements and the need to stay
competitive in a global market (Acemoglu & Restrepo, 2017). One significant consequence
has been the escalation in asset investments, as firms increasingly acquire high-cost equipment
and technology to support automated processes. This shift has led to significant efficiency
gains, with reduced cost structures and minimal errors in production. It has also required
substantial capital investments, creating a more capital-intensive economic structure with
lower asset utilization (Decker, Haltiwanger, Jarmin & Miranda, 2017). However, the
efficiency benefits of the structural change are not accurately captured by asset utilization but
rather by increased margins. This creates challenges in accurately capturing the improvements
on an aggregate level using asset utilization and the Lerner index as the analyzed metrics.
In contrast, the trucking industry prioritized automatization technologies that enhanced safety,
fuel efficiency, and logistical coordination rather than achieving full automation or displacing
drivers. Further, the development of Internet of Things (IoT), enabling real-time data exchange,
has been particularly transformative. IoT technology revolutionized fleet management by
optimizing scheduling, addressing maintenance proactively, and minimizing downtime (Chen,
Yu, & Khan, 2021). It also improved route optimization by integrating real-time data on traffic,
road conditions, and weather, allowing trucking companies to improve fleet productivity, and
reduce the number of vehicles needed to complete tasks (Gittleman & Monaco, 2018).

8
Unlike many industries, the trucking sector’s technological advancements did not eliminate
jobs or significantly expand the asset base when improving operational efficiency. While the
trucking industry is not entirely exempt from the Lerner index being influenced by operational
improvements, we argue that it is less distorted compared to other sectors. This structural
distinction suggests that efficiency gains in trucking are better reflected in asset utilization than
in aggregate market measures.

    3.2. Intangible vs Tangible Asset Dependency
During the study period, the relative importance of tangible assets declined across the U.S.
market, giving way to intangible capital. By 2014, intangible assets accounted for a
significantly larger share of firms' market value than in prior decades, signaling a broader
structural transformation in the economy (Corrado, Hulten & Sichel, 2005; Haskel & Westlake,
2017). Parallel to this trend, superstar firms grew in prominence, capitalizing on economies of
scale, productivity and intellectual property to achieve higher margins. Their high profit
margins driven by quality potentially inflate the aggregate Lerner index without exercising
market power (Autor et al. 2020).

For instance, Apple significantly increased their presence on the S&P 500 index and became
one of the largest constituents by launching high quality products such as the iPhone. The
iPhone's success can be attributed to several factors, including its innovative design, user-
friendly interface, and the robust ecosystem of applications and services that Apple developed
around it (Laugesen & Yuan, 2010). Thus, with the intention to separate market power from
productivity, traditional metrics such as asset utilization and the Lerner index are not suitable.
The Lerner index, which measures a firm's pricing power, may reflect gains attributable to
enhanced operations, such as improved R&D due to economies of scale and higher
productivity, rather than pure market power.

As earlier referenced, asset-light and asset-heavy industries often leverage new technological
developments in differing ways. (Yu, 2018; Bruggeman & Slagmulder, 1995). This suggests
that asset utilization will be a better proxy for efficiency gains in an asset-heavy industry. The
trucking industry, while incorporating intangible assets through the use of IoT as discussed in
section 3.1, remains fundamentally reliant on tangible assets for its core service of transporting
goods. Therefore, asset utilization effectively captures the efficiency of service delivery.

In summary, analyzing the trucking industry provides a more accurate framework for
distinguishing between efficiency gains and market power. In this asset-heavy sector, increases
in the Lerner index are more likely to reflect genuine market power, while efficiency gains are
effectively captured by asset utilization metrics. This contrasts with asset-light industries,
where intangible assets and the presence of superstar firms can distort these measurements.
    3.3. Outsourcing and Globalization
Another major structural change in the U.S. business landscape has been the increasing reliance
on outsourcing across many industries. Outsourcing has become an integral component of
corporate strategy to improve efficiency by relying on external support for peripheral functions.
Empirical evidence underscores the growth of outsourcing in this period. A 2013 report from
the Congressional Research Service (CRS) details substantial increases in outsourcing among
US firms. The report, depicted in Appendix A, reveals a rapid rise in employment and

9
production within foreign affiliates of US companies. This shift allows businesses to streamline
their operations by allocating non-core activities to third parties, reducing overhead and
focusing resources on essential business functions. By outsourcing tasks such as
manufacturing, customer service, and logistics, firms can avoid the costs associated with
managing these functions internally, allowing them to enhance flexibility and improve their
financial efficiency. As such, these efficiency gains are captured in the Lerner index rather than
asset utilization.

While many industries outsource tasks such as manufacturing and logistics to third parties,
trucking companies occupy a distinct position as the recipients of outsourced work. The
trucking industry analyzed contains exclusively third-party logistics providers. Businesses
requiring transportation services delegate the responsibilities to trucking firms, positioning the
companies as outsourcing providers that meet the transportation demands (Min, 2013).
Consequently, trucking firms do not outsource in the traditional sense; instead, they operate as
the endpoint recipients of outsourced logistics tasks. While trucking firms specialized in long-
range freight may outsource localized end-route transportation to other trucking companies,
both types of operations are captured within the scope of our analysis.

Offshoring, a particular form of outsourcing, has also become prominent in industries that can
relocate production to countries with lower labor costs. This practice is widespread in
manufacturing, where production processes can be effectively transferred abroad to reduce
operational expenses, visible in Appendix A. By shifting labor-intensive processes to low-wage
countries, companies achieve substantial cost savings, which are then redirected toward
activities that generate competitive advantages domestically (Milberg & Winkler, 2010). This
is not possible for the trucking industry as the act of moving goods from one location to another
is inherently locally bound. Trucking companies cannot access the same cost-saving strategies
available to other industries that outsource tasks internationally, such as relocating production
to countries with lower wage rates.

These inherent differences highlight the unique position of the trucking industry provides
within the broader framework of structural economic change. While many sectors experienced
a shift toward outsourcing and offshoring, leading to reduced domestic labor demand, the
trucking industry did not. This meant that potential outsourcing related cost reductions inflating
the Lerner index are better controlled for by analyzing the trucking industry. This results in
more accurate separation of efficiency and market power into their two respective proxies.
4. Methodology
This study investigates the relationship between market concentration and profitability,
focusing on the trucking industry and contrasting it with the aggregate US market. The analysis
is structured around three hypotheses, examining how concentration influences the financial
aspects: 1) profitability proxied by ROA; 2) asset efficiency proxied by asset utilization; and
3) market power proxied by the Lerner index. The study employs a combination of graphical
and statistical analyses. Graphical analysis is used to identify long-term trends across the 1985–
2023 period, while regression analysis is applied to firm-level data from 2001–2014 to test the
hypotheses quantitatively during the period where a statistically significant relationship
between concentration and profitability has been found. This mixed-method approach allows
us to disentangle the pathways through which concentration affects profitability.

    4.1. Data
Our primary dataset includes all publicly listed firms in NYSE, AMEX, and NASDAQ from
the Compustat database spanning the period from 1985 to 2023. Industry classification is
determined using NAICSH codes from Compustat, using historical NAICS to account for
changing classifications over time. To address missing NAICSH values in the dataset, a multi-
step process was implemented. First, NAICS values were employed to fill in gaps. Second,
NAICS codes from a CRSP dataset were used to supplement missing data. Third, SIC codes
obtained from CRSP were matched to the Compustat dataset and converted to NAICS codes
using a conversion table sourced from the NAICS association (2024). More detailed
information on data sourcing and filtering can be found in Appendix B.

For the regression analyses, we filter for only firms in the trucking industry, as defined by firms
with the three-digit NAICS code 484. The trucking subsection of the data is further
supplemented by including firms classified as traded on the OTC market, to include one of the
largest firms in the US trucking industry, Yellow. We limit the statistical tests to the trucking
industry as existing literature already covers statistical findings on concentration effects on an
aggregate level, diminishing the value provided by such analyses. The regression tests were
conducted for the period from 2001 to 2014. The timeframe was selected based on prior
findings by Grullon et al. (2019), who identified a statistically significant correlation between
HHI and ROA during the period. To evaluate if this correlation holds within the trucking
industry, the same timeframe was adopted, ensuring comparability and allowing for a precise
analysis of trucking-specific data.

For graphical analysis, a broader timeframe from 1985 to 2023 is utilized. This extended period
allows for the observation of long-term trends that may be obscured in shorter intervals. By
considering data from nearly four decades, the graphs capture broader industry dynamics,
including shifts in market concentration, profitability, and external economic influences. This
approach provides a contextual backdrop against which the focused regression analysis can be
better understood, revealing both short-term correlations and long-term structural trends in the
trucking industry.

An important limitation of our data set concerns its size and scope. The analysis is restricted to
publicly listed firms on the US stock exchanges previously mentioned, supplemented by data
from the OTC market, collectively encompassing 40 firms. However, since a significant
portion of the trucking industry is comprised of private entities, the dataset available for
regression analysis remains inherently limited. Further implications of these limitations are
discussed in Section 9.1.

composition with sectorial trends, unlike the broader two-digit or more granular four-digit
levels. For each industry, the market share of individual firms was determined as a percentage
of total industry sales. Each firm's market share was then squared to calculate its individual
contribution to the HHI. Finally, these squared values were summed for all N firms i within an

Return on Assets (ROA) is a widely used metric for profitability because it provides insight
into firm performance without being affected by changes in capital structure, in contrast to
ROE. It has also been indicated to be more effective in identifying abnormal operating
performance in studies by Barber and Lyon (1996).

By examining ROA’s trends in conjunction with concentration levels, we can assess whether
increasing concentration correlates with higher profitability as firms potentially leverage their
market power or strong operational efficiency to improve financial performance. On an
aggregate level, we initially calculate ROA across all industries. To avoid distortion, we later
exclude financial firms (NAICS code 52) and focus solely on non-financial industries since
financial firms have considerably lower ROA levels than other sectors due to the nature of their
revenue and asset base. The impact of this adjustment on our results is shown in Appendix C.
All non-financial US firms are henceforth referred to as the aggregate market for simplicity.

ROA can be calculated using earnings before or after depreciation and amortization, EBITDA
or EBIT, in the numerator. By excluding depreciation and amortization, the analysis offers
insight into a firm’s cash-generating ability. However, EBIT provides a clearer view of
operating profitability by accounting for asset maintenance costs, capturing the true economic
expense of asset usage. Unlike EBITDA, EBIT includes non-cash costs and capital
requirements, reflecting the full cost of operations. Thus, EBIT is preferable for ROA as it
provides a more accurate and comparable measure of how effectively a firm’s assets generate
earnings.

The methodology to calculate ROA involved summing the earnings before income and taxes
(EBIT) for all companies within the respective datasets and dividing this value by the combined
total assets of all companies in the dataset. Total assets were taken as reported for each year to
maximize data availability, as using lagged or average values was deemed unlikely to
materially affect the results, despite being theoretically preferable.

    4.3. Regression Specification
To statistically test the first hypothesis, we estimate a regression model using ROA as the
dependent variable. The independent variables are the natural logarithm of total assets (log
assets) and the natural logarithm of the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (log HHI). ROA
represents the profitability of a firm in a given year, log assets controls for firm size by taking
the natural logarithm of total assets, and log HHI captures market concentration, which is
constant across firms within the same industry in a given year.

                       𝑅𝑂𝐴𝑖𝑡 = 𝛼𝑖 + 𝛽1log_𝑎𝑠𝑠𝑒𝑡𝑠𝑖𝑡 + 𝛽2log_hhi𝑡 + 𝜀𝑖𝑡

To account for unobserved, time-invariant firm-specific factors, such as management style or
firm-specific technology, the model includes firm fixed effects. However, time fixed effects are
omitted because log HHI varies only by year and not across firms. Including time fixed effects
would introduce collinearity with log HHI, making it ineffective in capturing annual variation
in market concentration. As a result, log HHI itself acts as a proxy for annual changes in market
concentration, allowing us to examine its correlation with firm profitability over time.

To address potential within-firm correlation in error terms across time, standard errors are
clustered at the firm level. This clustering ensures robust standard errors that account for serial
correlation and heteroskedasticity within firms, enhancing the reliability of the estimated
coefficients.

To test the second and third hypotheses, we use the same independent variables and fixed
effects structure, but with different dependent variables. For the second hypothesis, the
dependent variable is the Lerner Index, which measures pricing power. For the third hypothesis,
the dependent variable is Asset Utilization, which evaluates how effectively firms use their
assets to generate revenue.

The graphical analysis of aggregate data highlights the unique historical context of the 2001–
2014 period. This timeframe begins with a recovery phase, characterized by strong growth
across all key financial metrics, followed by the 2008 financial crisis, which had significant
effects. These dynamics may influence the underlying factors behind the positive relationship

Our statistical analyses reveal a strong positive correlation between asset utilization and market
concentration, indicating that increased concentration enhances efficiency in the trucking
industry. However, the lack of a positive relationship between ROA and market concentration
presents an unexpected result. Given that asset utilization, a key driver of profitability, shows
a significant positive correlation with concentration, this disconnect warrants further
investigation. It suggests the presence of mitigating factors, such as industry-specific dynamics
or omitted variables, that may counteract the anticipated gains in efficiency resulting from
higher concentration. Theoretically, for ROA to remain unaffected by changes in concentration
despite higher asset utilization, the Lerner index would need to decrease. Yet, the absence of a
significant relationship between concentration and the Lerner index highlights the need for
additional analysis into the interplay between market concentration, efficiency, and
profitability.

    6.2. Incorporation of Economic Conditions
To account for macroeconomic factors influencing profitability during the period, we add
several additional proxies to our regressions in Section 5. Firstly, we include freight volumes
to account for aggregate level of economic activity and to ensure that ROA is not affected by
omitted demand considerations rather than market power. While monopolistic theories suggest
that market power may influence industry volumes, we argue that volume should still be
included in the robustness test to control for demand variations. Secondly, to address cost
structure variations, crude oil prices are incorporated into the regression. As fuel is essential
for trucking operations, price shocks in the oil market significantly impact the industry's cost
structure and consequently profitability. By controlling for this variable, we aim to ensure that
changes in industry specific input costs are not omitted from the analysis. Finally, the
extraordinary conditions of the 2008 financial crisis, characterized by widespread bankruptcies
and severe economic disruptions, are explicitly controlled for to remove noise from the analysis
using a dummy variable during 2008-2009. Collectively, these allow us to capture both industry
trends and firm-specific characteristics, enhancing the model's explanatory power and ensuring
more reliable results.

The regression results, as presented in Table 5, reveal that market concentration exhibits a
significant positive correlation with both profitability and the Lerner Index, but no significant
relationship with asset utilization. These findings allow us to reject the null hypothesis
associated with Hypothesis 1 at the 95% confidence level, while the null hypothesis for
Hypothesis 2 cannot be rejected. Furthermore, the analysis suggests that the Lerner index is the
principal determinant of profitability, not asset utilization. To put the magnitudes of these
finding into perspective, a change in the concentration index from the 25th to the 75th
percentile leads to an increase in the Lerner index of 20.4%. Whilst asset utilization is not
significant at the 95% confidence level, analyzing magnitude of the coefficient reveal that a
similar change in concentration results in an increase of approximately 5.2% relative to its
median. This is substantially lower than the effect on the Lerner index.

The results for the Lerner index, though consistent with the findings of Grullon et al. (2019),
are significantly smaller in magnitude within the trucking industry. In the aggregate U.S.
market, an increase in concentration from the 25th to the 75th percentile corresponded to a
142% increase in the Lerner index relative to its median, compared to just 20.4% in our
findings. This suggests that the profit margin effects in the trucking industry may not
experience the same degree of “inflation” from efficiency gains as observed in other industries.
Another noteworthy difference is the lack of explanatory power of HHI on asset utilization in
our results, which was found to be significant on the aggregate level. However, given that the
p-value is 0.065 and the sample size is considerable smaller in our test, this could potentially
be attributed to data limitation.

    6.3. Addressing Risk of Multicollinearity
The inclusion of volume as a proxy for demand raises concerns about multicollinearity. A
Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) test, detailed in Appendix E (Panels A & B), reveals substantial
multicollinearity in the regression from Table 5, with volume and HHI showing the highest VIF
values. To address this, volume was excluded in a second regression (Table 6), with its VIF
values presented in Appendix E (Panels C & D). Despite reduced multicollinearity, HHI
remains significantly positively correlated with ROA, while its correlation with the Lerner
Index is significant only at the 90% confidence level, and its correlation with asset utilization
is insignificant.
The ROA regression in Table 6 shows lower explanatory power (adjusted R-squared of 0.078
and F-test statistic of 7.453) compared to Table 5. While multicollinearity decreases, VIF
values for HHI (150) and oil prices (128) remain high. As such, we argue that the initial model
is preferable and that the robustness test does not undermine the findings in Table 5 for three
reasons: 1) The variables do not have causal relationship. Crude oil prices are set on a global
spot market, and the trucking industry does not have a material impact on these prices; 2)
Multicollinearity complicates interpretation but does not invalidate results. Although HHI and
oil prices are strongly correlated (0.87), oil prices effectively proxy external costs, critical for
controlling the trucking industry's fuel dependency; and 3) The small dataset (14 annual

25
observations with 4–6 independent variables) inflates VIF values. Since the correlation
between concentration and profitability is found within a specific time period, this cannot be
addressed without diverting from methodology of previous literature. Consequently, even if
high VIF values are evident, the model is still applicable.

Further, when determining the most appropriate model, we argue that a proxy for demand is
crucial, and its exclusion would result in omitted effects on the relationship with profitability.
We therefore believe that Table 5 has the most theoretically sound model. Furthermore, the
tests indicate that volume is not manipulated as a means to inflate prices, having a significant
and positive relationship with high magnitude for both ROA (0.39) and the Lerner index (0.21).
Theoretically, monopolistic behavior would likely result in a negative or negligible relationship
between volume and profit margins, as monopolists typically restrict output to drive up prices.
It is therefore important to interpret these findings cautiously, as such tendencies might exist,
although unlikely in our opinion. Lastly, no coefficients change direction when excluding
volumes, indicating that the results are robust without the metric.

In summary, by controlling for fuel costs, demand, and the financial crisis, market
concentration in the trucking industry shows a positive, significant correlation with
profitability, consistent with existing literature. These findings align with Grullon et al. (2019),
highlighting market power, rather than asset efficiency, as the primary driver of profitability.

7. Changes in Industry Composition
As highlighted in the previous section, the trucking industry underwent substantial structural
changes during the observation period. Given that our analysis focuses on a single industry
with a limited number of actors, shifts in the industry's composition can have profound
implications for profitability metrics, competitive dynamics, and pricing strategies. To evaluate
whether the observed results might be influenced by changes in industry composition, we
examine the sales shares of individual firms within the trucking sector. Figure 7 illustrates the
market share of firms accounting for at least 4% of total industry sales, while the evolution of
the trucking industry’s concentration can be seen more granularly in Appendix F. Further, in
Table 4 we provide an overview of the events affecting the concentration in the trucking
industry.
Major developments during the period include the bankruptcy and subsequent restructuring of
SIRVA, which removed the company from the public market in 2008 (Transport Topics, 2008).
Prior to its removal, the company accounted for 12.3% of the market share in our dataset.
SIRVA’s collapse was primarily attributed to excessive debt that became unsustainable during
the financial crisis, coupled with shifting market demands that strained its traditional business
model. Similarly, the company Swift Transportation was taken off the public market in 2006
(Reuters, 2006). However, the development was shortly followed by a reinstatement to the
public market in 2010 (Bloomberg, 2010). At the time of removal, the company represented
8.44% of the market share in our dataset. While the privatization temporarily impacted
concentration levels within the industry, it did not affect the underlying competitive dynamics
or market power.

The most notable shift during the period was the acquisition of Roadway by Yellow in 2003.
Roadway Corp was the largest actor on the market and Yellow was the second largest at the
time (Deutsch, 2003). Yellow also acquired USF in 2005 (Machalaba & Berman, 2005). These
acquisitions resulted in Yellow’s market share skyrocketing to almost 18% in 2004 and almost
30% in 2007, shown in Appendix F. The acquisitions have notably increased levels of HHI in
the industry, visible in Figure 1, Panel B.

Given Yellow’s substantial presence in the industry, a firm-level analysis is warranted to
evaluate its operational performance. Appendix G illustrates the financial metrics of Yellow,
which exhibit trends closely aligned with the industry-wide metrics shown in Appendix D.
Notably, Yellow’s Lerner index remains consistently lower than the industry aggregate, peaking
at approximately 0.05, while the aggregate Lerner index for the trucking sector averages around
0.05. Additionally, Yellow’s profit margins were near or below zero for four years during the
examined period, compared to only one such instance for the industry aggregate. A closer
examination of Yellow’s financial trajectory reveals that the outcome was due to the potential
pitfalls of aggressive consolidation strategies. Yellow’s rapid expansion brought with it
significant integration challenges, resulting in inefficiencies and elevated costs during the
examined period (Maiden, 2023). The financial strain resulted in major restructuring efforts in
2011 and 2014, aimed at addressing debt and operational inefficiencies. Yellow’s increasing
market share may have distorting results on the observed relationship between market
concentration and profitability due to firm specific factors.

profitability in the trucking industry is the type of goods transported. Investigating the types of
goods transported can provide insights into changes in revenue streams and cost dynamics not
accounted for in previous sections. This examination will help uncover whether the observed
profitability trends are linked to internal shifts in the trucking industry.

Figure 8 presents the evolution of goods transported within the U.S trucking industry by
commodity type in 2002, 2007, and 2012, based on classification data from the Bureau of
Transportation Statistics (2024). The analysis reveals shifts toward a more evenly divided mix

products toward higher-value goods such as foodstuffs and gasoline. Such diversification may
introduce both opportunities and challenges for the industry (Trattner, Hyam, Forza & Herbert-
Hansen, 2019). On one hand, handling a broader portfolio of goods increases operational
complexity and costs, potentially placing downward pressure on margins. On the other hand,
diversification offers opportunities for higher margins through specialization and customized
services.

In addition, the rapid growth of e-commerce has also shifted transportation trends. While
previously centered around transporting goods from warehouses to retail stores, the rise of
online shopping has pivoted this model toward dispersed last-mile delivery systems. Silva,
Espíndola, Rotha, Camargo, Santos & Malafaia, (2014) explain that logistics has become the
backbone of e-commerce operations, requiring innovations in routing, tracking, and efficient
delivery to accommodate the complexity of smaller, more frequent shipments. The growth of
e-commerce sales is displayed in Appendix I, Panel A.

9. Identification of Omitted Variables and Limitations
    9.1. Omission of Private Firms
The trucking industry has undergone significant structural transformations in recent decades,
driven by technological advancements and evolving consumer demands. A notable
development not included in our analysis is the increasing prevalence of internal logistics
networks. For example, Amazon has fundamentally reshaped the logistics landscape through
the creation of its own infrastructure, reducing reliance on external carriers while
simultaneously intensifying competitive pressure. Through its subsidiaries, such as Amazon
Logistics, Amazon manages an extensive in-house delivery network to meet its e-commerce
demands, bypassing traditional transportation companies for a substantial share of its logistics
needs (Lieb & Lieb, 2016)

This phenomenon is not exclusive to e-commerce giants. For instance, Walmart has also
invested heavily in its private trucking fleet. By late 2005, Walmart operated a private fleet
with 8,000 drivers and 30,000 trailers, making it the second-largest private fleet in the United
States at the time (Hyatt, 2014). Subsidiaries of companies like Amazon and Walmart are not
included in our dataset, yet their presence exerts considerable competitive pressure on the
transportation industry, pointing to omitted competitive factors in our analysis. In 2012, private
carriers such as those operated by Walmart and Amazon accounted for 49% of all truck freight
tonnage, while for-hire carriers handled 51% (Costello, 2013).

In addition to these large in-house fleets, there is a substantial number of trucking firms that
are privately owned and operate independently. Data on the exact size and market share of
these private firms is limited, but they represent a significant portion of the industry. For
instance, there were 1.3 million trucking companies in the U.S. in 2014 (FMCSA, 2015). These
private firms fall outside the scope of our analysis. This limitation implies that the dataset,
which focuses on publicly traded firms, may overlook critical competitive dynamics introduced
by private operators.
    9.2. Role of Labor in the Trucking Industry
This paper has previously examined in detail the trends of declining labor share. An additional
dimension to consider is the weakening of unionization. Historically, unions have played a
pivotal role in securing higher wage shares for workers. Karabarbounis (2023) highlights that
sectors with low unionization rates, where collective bargaining power has diminished, have
experienced more pronounced wage stagnation, a trend directing a larger share of economic
gains toward capital owners.

The trucking industry, however, stands out in this regard due to its relatively high unionization
rates compared to the national average. In 2014, unionization in the transportation and
warehousing sector, which includes the trucking industry, was 19.6% of workers, significantly

31
higher than the 6.6% observed across the broader private sector (Bureau of Labor Statistics,
2015). This divergence may have notable implications for the trucking industry's capacity to
exert market power. As Chen, Kacperczyk and Ortiz-Molina (2009) argue, unionized firms
often face higher fixed labor costs, which can compress profit margins and limit their ability to
increase markups compared to non-unionized firms. In industries with strong union presence,
such as trucking, firms may find it difficult to pass on additional costs to customers through
price increases without jeopardizing their competitive position.

The role of labor and its implications on our results is not analyzed in depth. However, existing
literature indicates that it may reduce the ability to exercise market power. This potentially
imply that the correlation between concentration and market power may be more considerable
without such mitigating effect. Future research could explore how varying degrees of
unionization affect firms' strategic priorities, providing a broader understanding of labor's role
in shaping market outcomes across different sectors.

    9.3. Regulatory Considerations in the Trucking Industry
The US trucking industry has undergone substantial regulatory changes in recent decades. A
notable regulation is the introduction of the Hours of Service (HOS) rules in 2003 aimed at
enhancing road safety and mitigate driver fatigue (FMCSA, 2022). The regulation significantly
impacted logistics planning by requiring firms to hire additional drivers, adopt dual-driver
arrangements, and invest in electronic logging devices to ensure compliance. While these
measures succeeded in improving safety and standardizing working conditions, they also
escalated operational costs and necessitated investments in route optimization and fleet
management technologies. In addition to safety regulations, increasingly stringent emission
standards have imposed considerable technological and financial pressures on the industry.
Initiatives such as the 2007 Heavy-Duty Highway Diesel Program and the 2014 Greenhouse
Gas Emissions Standards mandated the adoption of advanced technologies to reduce emissions
and enhance fuel efficiency (EPA, 2023).

The industry-specific regulations highlight a key limitation on our analysis. Regulatory
changes unique to trucking, such as HOS compliance and emission standards, introduce
variables that may not apply or translate directly to other sectors. This limits the generalizability
of our findings, as the trucking industry’s cost structures, operational priorities, and responses
to regulation differ significantly from those of other industries. Recognizing these limitations
is crucial when applying our results to broader economic analyses or making cross-industry
comparisons. It highlights the need for careful contextualization when extending insights
beyond the trucking sector. With this in mind, we still believe that the value provided by our
successful separation of efficiency and market power does provide insight not limited to the
trucking industry.
10. Conclusion and Implications
This study investigates the trends and relationship between concentration and profitability in
the U.S. trucking industry with the aim to distinguish between market power and efficiency
more accurately. The trucking industry was selected due to several factors: 1) The industry’s
efficiency gains from technological developments have primarily been captured in asset
utilization; 2) The industry is characterized by tangible assets; and 3) The industry has limited
possibilities for outsourcing and offshoring. We find that concentration primarily drives
profitability through increased mark-ups and observe a less prominent, not significant, link

32
between concentration and asset utilization. Our results reinforce the findings of existing
literature on aggregate U.S. market and the notion of market power being the source of
abnormal profits in concentrated industries. However, our findings suggest that profit margin
gains sourced from increased concentration are lower in magnitude than implied by existing
literature. We contribute this result to a more accurate separation of efficiency from market
power. The analysis was conducted using data from publicly listed firms between 1985 and
2023, focusing on the period from 2001 to 2014 to align with existing findings.

Our initial regression analysis found no significant correlation between profitability and
concentration. When analyzing the drivers of profitability, a significant and positive
relationship emerged between concentration and asset efficiency, but not for profit margins and
concentration. This initial result suggested that increased concentration within the trucking
sector did not lead to higher profit margins or enhanced pricing power, but is instead closely
tied to improvements in how efficiently firms utilize their assets. However, the model had low
levels of explanatory power, and graphical analysis showcased that the period was affected by
extraordinary circumstances. For instance, the period initiates with a recovery after the
financial crisis of 2000 and coincides with the 2008 recession.

To account for the special circumstances, proxies for demand fluctuations, cost developments,
and the economic crisis of 2008 were accounted for. The results showed a positive and
significant relationship between concentration and profitability. This relationship was primarily
driven by increased mark-ups during higher levels of concentration. While a positive
correlation with asset utilization could be observed, it was only significant at the 90%
confidence level and weaker in magnitude. Specifically, a shift in concentration from the 25th
to the 75th percentile results in a 20.4% increase in profit margins, compared to only a 5.1%
increase for asset utilization. Such a relationship supports the finding that market power is the
source of abnormal profits. However, the result significantly differs from the aggregate U.S.
market, where an increase in concentration from the 25th to the 75th percentile corresponded
to a 142% increase in the Lerner index relative to its median. We believe that the findings are
attributable to the trucking industry not experiencing the same degree of inflation from
efficiency gains as observed in other industries, serving as a better indicator of the true
relationship.

To determine the validity of our findings, further robustness tests was conducted. A VIF
analysis suggested that the modified regression analysis had significant risk of multicollinearity
issues. Consequently, trucking tonnage volume, serving as the proxy for demand, was excluded
to establish the validity of the findings. The results showcase the same relations, while less
significant. However, we argue that the model’s reliability is higher when including a demand
proxy, to control for profitability improvements derived from other factors than market power.
This is also evident by comparing the models’ explanatory power.

Additionally, an analysis of industry actors was conducted to account for industry composition
changes. The analysis found that several prominent actors underwent events with major
changes with implications on concentration, potentially skewing results. Notably, the company
Yellow underwent a period of financial distress while being the largest actor within the industry.
To determine, if the firm had a material effect on the findings, an analysis excluding Yellow
was conducted. The results remained consistent, strengthening the validity and robustness of
the findings. However, the number of significant events within our industry showcases the

33
complexity of examining a period including effects from major crises. This may warrant further
investigation of the relationship between HHI and profitability in more recent time periods.

Lastly, changes in the industry’s composition of goods and services were analyzed. The period
was characterized by decreasing concentration of composition of goods, which was determined
by constructing a metric measuring the concentration of commodities transported. The metric
was included in a robustness test and was found to have significant impact on the results of our
regression, reducing the significance of HHI. However, the test was discredited due major data
limitations and high multicollinearity in the test. Albeit it warrants an interesting point for
further research and a limitation of our study.

Our results reveal that profitability gains from concentration in the trucking industry are driven
by market power rather than efficiency gains, contradicting our initial hypotheses that
efficiency would be the primary driver of profitability in this sector. While these findings align
with the conclusions of Grullon et al. (2019), they do not rule out the role of non-market power
effects, such as the influence of superstar firms proposed by Autor et al. (2020). Instead, they
underscore the importance of distinguishing between profitability gains stemming from market
power and those derived from efficiency, providing a more nuanced understanding of these
dynamics.

Importantly, our results suggest that the effect of market power in the trucking industry is less
pronounced than previous literature on the aggregate U.S. market has indicated. By introducing
a framework to disentangle the contributions of market power and efficiency to profitability,
this study offers a more accurate representation of the relationship. Furthermore, while our
findings confirm that M&A activity increasing concentration can boost profitability through
market power, the effects appear less significant than earlier studies have suggested. These
insights highlight the potential need for stronger antitrust policies to protect consumer welfare
from inefficient markups and inflated profits.

While our analysis provides valuable insights into the dynamics of concentration and
profitability, trucking industry specific dynamics and several limitations warrant consideration.
For instance, the exclusion of private firms and internal logistics networks, such as those
operated by Amazon and Walmart, omits critical competitive forces that shape market
outcomes from our analysis. Future research could incorporate data on these entities to provide
a more comprehensive view of industry competitiveness. Additionally, the role of labor,
particularly unionization, introduces complexities in the market power analysis. Given the
trucking sector's relatively high unionization rates, further investigation into how labor
dynamics influence profit margins and market behavior could yield significant insights to
properly ensure the applicability of our findings. Finally, the regulatory environment, including
safety and environmental compliance, uniquely impacts the trucking industry and may further
limit the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, while profitability was found to correlate with concentration, our initial
expectations about the underlying drivers were disproven. This supports existing literature but
raises questions about the extent to which concentration drives profitability gains solely
through market power. Consequently, further research in finance is needed to more accurately
distinguish profitability gains arising from efficiencies, such as product quality and operational
excellence, from those driven by market power. Such insights would help clarify the welfare
effects on consumers and the broader implications for market efficiency.

The data points collected from Compustat were the following: gvkey, financial year, sales, total
assets, earnings before interest and taxes, exchange code, NAICS codes, historical NAICS

The data points collected from CRSP were the following: permco, permno, NAICS codes, SIC

gvkey and permco. This was used to merge the data sets.

To ensure comparability with the Grullon et al. study, only firms listed of the NYSE, NASDAQ,
or American Exchange is included in the data. These are categorized by exchange codes 11, 12
or 14 in the Compustat data set. Yellow, the largest trucking industry company for a majority
of the 2001-2014 period was listed on over the counter (OTC) listing under share code 19.
Share code 19 companies were added into the dataset to include this company.

For the regression further data filtering was conducted. To manage extreme values distorting
results, all firms with sales or assets less than 5 million US dollars were excluded. The financial
metrics ROA, asset utilization, and Lerner index were also winsorized at 2% extremes. Firms
not present in a minimum of two consecutive years were also filtered to improve regression
performance. These filtering processes were also conducted by Grullon et al. (2019).

Due to data limitations, firm age was not included as a control variable in the regressions.
Furthermore, time fixed effects could not be included due to focusing on a single industry.

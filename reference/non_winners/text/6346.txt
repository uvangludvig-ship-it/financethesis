ESG In the Storm: Linking ESG ratings and stock performance during market distress

Abstract:

This paper investigates the relationship between ESG ratings and stock performance during
periods of market distress, focusing on the 2022 downturn driven by inflation, geopolitical
conflict, and monetary tightening. Drawing on the methodologies employed by Lins et al.
(2017) and Albuquerque et al. (2020), the analysis evaluates the effect of ESG ratings on
realized returns, abnormal returns, and volatility across European and U.S. equities. Using a
cross-sectional OLS regression model, we find that ESG ratings are significantly positively
associated with abnormal and realized returns, and negatively associated with total volatility.
When employing a pooled panel OLS regression framework to compare the 2022 crisis
period with the non-crisis year 2021, we find no evidence that these effects are unique to
crisis conditions. Furthermore, we study operational performance and stakeholder loyalty as
potential explanatory variables for our findings, but find no statistically significant evidence.
Lastly, we compare our primary results using Refinitiv Eikon and MSCI ESG ratings and do
not find consistent outcomes, highlighting the lack of consensus across ESG rating providers
and the methodological challenges associated with a ratings-based approach. Our findings
affirm the financial relevance of ESG in market downturns, while highlighting limitations in
current rating methodologies and the need for more objective ESG indicators in future
research.

1 Introduction

ESG, short for economic, social, and governance, is a framework used to assess environmental, social, and
governance risks for companies. Since the United Nations introduced the term in 2004, ESG has evolved
from a reputational bonus to a standard necessity for publicly traded companies. Successively, investor
preferences have shifted from exclusively prioritizing profits to increasingly incorporating broader stake-
holder perspectives and ESG considerations (Edmans and Kapercyk, 2022). As a result, the performance
of sustainable investing has garnered increasing attention, with investors aiming to discern whether such
strategies yield excess returns compared to ESG-neutral counterparts or whether they entail a financial
sacrifice in pursuit of broader societal objectives.
   While the broad literature has yielded inconclusive findings on the long-term performance of ESG-
focused stocks and funds (Alves, Krueger, and van Dijk, 2025), a more consistent body of evidence has
emerged regarding periods of economic distress and downturn, indicating a positive correlation between
ESG credentials and financial performance. Nofsinger and Varma (2014) were among the first to demon-
strate that funds with high ESG scores outperform neutral funds during market crises, while finding the
reverse relationship for normal economic times. Since then, two hallmark studies, by Lins, Servaes, and
Tamayo (2017), and Albuquerque, Koskinen, Yang, and Zhang (2020), have revealed that sustainability
focused firms tend to outperform their neutral counterparts during periods of market distress, from this
point referred to as the ESG effect or ESG resilience. The studies, investigating the 2008 Financial Cri-
sis and the 2020 COVID-19 crisis, respectively, attribute this effect to heightened stakeholder trust and
loyalty toward sustainable companies, which they argue becomes increasingly important during increased
market volatility.
   Aiming to strengthen the understanding and robustness of previous research findings, this study
empirically investigates the resilience of ESG stocks during the 2022 market downturn, triggered by
inflationary pressures and the Russo-Ukrainian conflict (Melgar, Lynch, Shapiro, 2022). Drawing on the
methodologies employed in previous literature, this paper examines whether the conclusions drawn by
Lins et al. (2017) and Albuquerque et al. (2020) still hold. While previously mentioned studies report
a positive relationship between ESG ratings and stock performance during crises, emerging evidence
suggests that ESG funds and stocks behaved differently during the 2022 market downturn, thus increasing
the need to re-evaluate ESG resilience. We therefore aim to examine whether the ESG effect persists
in the crisis period of 2022, to establish robustness and gain a deeper understanding of the underlying
dynamics driving ESG resilience.
   One indication that ESG investments might have underperformed the market during this recent crisis
is the increased demand for energy and military stocks following the Russo-Ukrainian war, even among
ESG-responsible investors. The MSCI World Aerospace & Defense Index, one of the leading defense
indices, earned a return of 12.9% compared to the parent index, MSCI World, which decreased in value
by 18.1% (MSCI, 2025). This development challenged conventional ESG categorizations and raised
questions about the inclusion of sin industries within sustainability ratings and frameworks (Harris and
Pfeifer, 2024). Morningstar, one of the largest index providers in the world, also noted that only 27%
of their sustainability indices outperformed ESG-neutral counterparts in 2022. This figure represents a
decline from 75% in 2020 (Lefkovitz, 2023) – the period studied in Albuquerque et al.’s paper (2020) –
suggesting a shift in the performance dynamics of ESG stocks. Following the crisis period, investors have
been increasingly redeeming capital from green funds as shown in Morningstar’s 2023 annual review of
U.S sustainable funds, which revealed the first calendar year net outflow since the start of their reviews
in 2013 (Stankiewicz, 2024).

                                             3
   Given the robust consensus on the ESG effect across existing literature and emerging contradictory
signals for the recent crisis in 2022, this study re-evaluates the relationship between ESG and stock
performance by addressing the following research question:

   Do higher ESG ratings contribute to better stock performance during periods of market downturn?

   This study builds upon the methodology, empirical models, and regression frameworks employed by
Lins et al. (2017) and Albuquerque et al. (2020) to address whether ESG scores had a significant impact
on stock performance during the market downturn of 2022, which we define as the first two quarters of
the year. With an event window of six months, we employ the regression methodology used by Lins et al.
(2017) with monthly returns to conclude whether ESG ratings affect stock performance, and expand on
Albuquerque et al.’s (2020) explanatory framework to decipher potential findings. Whereas Albuquerque
et al. (2020) focus on customer and investor loyalty as moderating factors of the ESG effect, our model
also incorporates average employee tenure as a proxy for employee loyalty, thereby contributing to a more
comprehensive and multifaceted framework for capturing stakeholder loyalty.
   To establish the robustness of our findings, we extend our analysis and methodology to the 2020
COVID-19 market crash. Additionally, ESG ratings from two distinct providers – Refinitiv and MSCI
– are used to examine the consistency of our results across alternative rating agencies. This approach
addresses the concerns raised by Berg, K¨olbel, and Rigobon (2022), who highlight the divergence in ESG
ratings across providers and the resulting variability in empirical outcomes.
   While previous literature has predominantly focused on the U.S. stock market, our study incorporates
U.S. and European equities to broaden its geographical scope. Similar to how Lins et al. (2017) utilize
cross-U.S. state differences in trust as explanatory variables, this study investigates whether the ESG
effect varies across the two regions, particularly in light of the European Union’s stricter ESG regulations
(G. Hatter, May, Barbone, Ngunjiri, and Thomas, 2025), and its comparatively higher level of institutional
trust (Edelman, 2021). Therefore, by extending the sample, we will both evaluate the universality of the
ESG effect and discern the geographical dynamics behind it.
   We find that ESG rating has a significant positive effect on both realized and abnormal returns during
the crisis period, and a significant negative effect on total volatility. The effect is, however, only prominent
in the European subsample, contrasting with previous literature. Furthermore, in the pooled panel OLS
regression, we find limited evidence that the effect is specifically driven by the crisis period. Similarly,
our explanatory variables exhibit no statistical significance, nor do we observe consistent results when
using MSCI’s ESG rating in the core regression models. Therefore, while our results provide support for
ESG resilience, they do not provide an explanation for the underlying mechanism.
   The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews the existing literature on
the performance of ESG stocks during market downturns, outlines the contributions of this paper, and
concludes with the development of testable hypotheses. Section 3 covers the data collection process and
addresses potential limitations in the dataset. Section 4 details the methodology employed to obtain
the empirical results, which are presented in Section 5. Sections 6 and 7 discuss our empirical findings
and acknowledge limitations with the study. Finally, Section 8 concludes with a summary of the main
insights.

2 Literary Review

2.1 Related Literature

The link between ESG and stock performance has become a central topic in modern finance literature. In
one of the most recent and comprehensive studies, Alves et al. (2025) show that the relationship between

                                             4
ESG ratings and global stock returns is statistically and economically insignificant. Using data from over
16,000 stocks across 48 countries and seven ESG rating providers from 2001–2020, the authors find no
consistent evidence that ESG ratings predict future stock performance. While acknowledging these find-
ings, our study distinguishes itself by investigating whether ESG ratings become meaningful performance
indicators, specifically during economic downturns, when market conditions amplify differences in firm
quality and investor sentiment.
   Several studies have examined the specific relationship between sustainable firms and stock returns
during periods of market distress. A key paper for our study, conducted by Lins et al. (2017), examines
the Great Financial Crisis of 2008 and the 2000–2001 Enron Accounting Scandals. They find statistically
significant relationships between corporate social performance and higher stock returns, higher operating
returns, and lower return volatility. They argue that this relationship is attributable to heightened trust
in socially responsible companies, which was particularly pronounced during the trust shock of the 2008
financial crisis. Using crisis periods of similar length, our study adopts much of Lins et al.’s methodology –
including regression specification and control variables – to examine whether ESG ratings are significantly
associated with stock performance, and whether this relationship is specific to the crisis period.
   In a more recent study, Albuquerque et al. (2020) examine the relationship between environmental and
social (ES) performance and stock returns during the COVID-19 crisis of 2020. They find that stocks with
higher environmental and social scores earned higher returns, operating profit margins, and experienced
less stock price volatility compared to non-ES stocks during the first quarter of 2020. Following that, they
argue that firms with higher ES performance benefit from greater investor and customer loyalty, resulting
in stable ownership, lower price elasticity of demand, higher profit margins, and increased returns during
economic contractions. Their empirical design uses institutional investors’ ESG ratings from Refinitiv F13
as a proxy for investor loyalty toward sustainable firms. Advertising expenditure serves as an additional
proxy for customer loyalty. Although the proxy reflects the firm’s investment in brand-building, it does
not measure its success or account for advertising aimed at retention rather than customer acquisition.
Dependence on high advertising expenditure may, for example, signal that the firm is either struggling
with customer loyalty or expanding rather than retaining its customer base.
   To address this limitation, we expand the analysis by introducing an additional proxy for stakeholder
loyalty. Specifically, we use employee tenure as a measure of employee loyalty, capturing internal trust
and organizational stability; institutional ownership percentage as a proxy for investor loyalty, reflecting
the extent of long-term, committed capital; and advertising-to-sales ratio as a proxy for customer loyalty.
By incorporating average tenure, we aim to construct a more robust, multi-dimensional framework of
stakeholder trust to discern the relationship between sustainable firms and stock returns.
   Heinkel, Kraus and Zechner (2001) develop theoretical models suggesting polluting firms face chal-
lenges in retaining investor loyalty, as socially responsible investors systematically exclude them. Our
study empirically incorporates these theories by examining whether the sale of sin products and low ESG
ratings constrain stock performance during the crisis period, applying a methodology consistent with our
loyalty framework.
   While existing literature has established relatively strong consensus regarding the resilience of ESG-
rated stocks during periods of financial distress, recent research by Berg et al. (2022) highlights critical
challenges to these findings. The study presents the substantial divergence among ESG rating providers
regarding measurement, scope, and weighting, and raises concerns about the comparability and reliability
of ESG ratings in empirical research. In light of these findings, our study addresses the issue of rating
heterogeneity by comparing results across two of the most widely used ESG rating systems: MSCI and
Refinitiv Eikon. In doing so, we aim to assess whether the observed relationship between ESG performance
and financial outcomes is robust or sensitive to variations in ESG measurement frameworks.
    This study contributes to the ESG literature in four key ways. Firstly, it serves to reassess the validity

                                             5
of existing literature on ESG stock performance, particularly in light of recent indicators suggesting that
green investments may have underperformed during the 2022 crisis period. Secondly, extending the
sample to include European stocks allows for identifying geographical heterogeneity in the ESG effect.
Third, it expands on Albuquerque et al.’s (2020) stakeholder loyalty framework by incorporating more
comprehensive proxies for employee loyalty. Finally, given the discrepancies between ESG scores across
rating agencies, this study evaluates the sensitivity of our results under alternative rating systems and,
consequently, the validity of using a ratings-based framework to analyze ESG resilience.

2.2 Hypothesis Development

In light of the broad academic consensus regarding the performance of sustainable firms during market
distress, we formulate the following hypotheses:

  i. Firms with higher ESG ratings experienced higher realized and abnormal returns during the crisis
                                             period of 2022.

This hypothesis is based on the robust findings of Lins et al. (2017) and Albuquerque et al. (2020).
While fund providers such as Morningstar report recent underperformance of ESG funds and stocks, our
analysis is grounded in established academic research. It aims to further evaluate the robustness of ESG
resilience during recent periods of financial distress.

  ii. The relationship between ESG ratings and stock performance is explicitly attributable to the crisis
                                                   period.

During non-crisis periods, Alves et al. (2025), Lins et al. (2017), and Albuquerque et al. (2020) all
present non-significant results for the relationship between sustainability rating and stock performance.
We therefore hypothesize that our pooled panel OLS regression models will yield non-significant or adverse
ESG effects for non-crisis periods, and positive coefficients during the crisis window.

      iii. Employing different ESG ratings yields diverging conclusions for Hypotheses (i) and (ii).

In light of the findings by Berg et al. (2022), we approach any conclusions with caution by incorporating
two contrasting ESG rating frameworks to highlight the variability in ESG assessments.

3 Data Collection

To examine the financial impact of ESG metrics, we collect data on ESG ratings, stock returns, and firm-
specific accounting variables for publicly listed firms from 2016–2022. ESG ratings, control variables, and
stock returns for studies of the 2022 crisis period are used as inclusion criteria for the dataset; only firms
with complete data across these dimensions are retained. Subsequent analyses – covering the COVID-19
crisis, operational performance, stakeholder loyalty, and related areas – are conducted using this restricted
sample, with adjustments made for any missing data observations.

3.1 Stock Sample

We construct our sample in S&P’s Capital IQ database by inserting limiting criteria, starting with the
country of incorporation. In addition to American stocks, the sample includes European stocks, thereby
expanding the geographical scope of previous literature and enabling cross-border comparisons. The
remaining criteria required complete data for the calendar years 2020 and 2021 to support our pooled
panel regressions discussed in Section 4.2. Our sample exhibits a minimum market capitalization of $250

                                             6
million for 2021 and 2020, thus excluding low-cap stocks that typically exhibit outlying behavior due to
lower liquidity, wider bid-ask spreads, and heightened price pressure during financial crises (Lins et al.,
2017). Our main set of firm-specific control variables follows the methodology of Lins et al. (2017) and
were collected for the two calendar years ending 2020 and 2021. Due to data limitations in Capital IQ,
the current ratio, defined as current assets divided by current liabilities, is used instead of the operating
liabilities-to-assets ratio, which Lins et al. (2017) employ. Descriptions of the control variables are
provided in Table 1.
   Monthly dividend-adjusted net stock returns for 2016–2022 are obtained from the LSEG’s Refinitiv
Eikon Database. Daily dividend-adjusted net stock returns are collected over the same period, used to
calculate daily stock price volatility and regressions for the COVID-19 crisis.

3.2 ESG Rating

Our primary measure of ESG performance is sourced from LSEG’s Refinitiv Eikon ESG Database, a
widely used (Ferrell, Liang, and Renneboog, 2016) (Albuquerque et al., 2022) and the most comprehensive
ESG dataset in academic literature (Corporate Finance Institute, 2025). Refinitiv derives ESG scores
based on data from annual reports, sustainability reports, non-governmental organizations (NGOs), and
other resources for publicly traded firms. The score is calculated as a weighted average for the three
ESG pillars: Environmental, Social, and Governance. Each pillar consists of several categories, which
are further broken down into granular dimensions. The overall pillar score is calculated by the relative
performance and materiality of these dimensions within the firm’s sector (for E and S) and country (for
G). (Albuquerque et al., 2020) (LSEG Data & Analytics, 2024)
   Our study incorporates MSCI’s ESG Rating, obtained from the Swedish House of Finance MSCI
ESG Ratings Time Series Database for cross-rating comparisons. MSCI evaluates firms based on how
effectively they manage financially material ESG risks relative to industry peers. Key ESG issues are
identified by sector, and firms are scored on their exposure and risk management. These scores are
weighted by financial materiality and aggregated into an overall score, with significant controversies
potentially triggering a downgrade. Final ratings range from CCC (worst) to AAA (best). (Lins et al.,
2017) (MSCI, 2024)
   While Refinitiv and MSCI aim to quantify ESG performance, they differ substantially in method-
ology. Refinitiv emphasises transparency and objectivity, scoring each firm based on the same criteria.
Conversely, MSCI’s approach relies more on its own assumptions on materiality, such that the final score
becomes more tailored but also more subjective. Unlike accounting data, ESG scores from Eikon and
MSCI’s publication dates vary throughout the year. As a result, ESG ratings are not collected strictly
as of the calendar year-end, but rather at various points throughout the preceding year.

3.3 Data Cleaning

In Capital IQ, we apply geographical criteria, a market capitalization threshold, and an inclusion filter
that omits firms with missing data points for the control variables, ending with a sample of approximately
2,900 stocks. Eikon’s screener platform obtains ESG ratings and returns data for the sample. Stocks with
missing ESG data for 2020 and 2021 or return data between 2017 and 2022 are consequently removed from
the sample. Finally, as Refinitiv displays data based on each firm’s fiscal year, the dataset is manually
validated to ensure that each ESG rating corresponds to the calendar year preceding the crisis period –
namely, 2019 for the COVID-19 period and 2021 for the 2022 period. The final sample comprises 2,112
firms with complete ESG and firm-level data for 2020 and 2021, as well as return data spanning from
January 2017 to June 2022. 632 of these are European, and the rest, 1480 stocks, have the U.S. as country
of incorporation.

                                             7
   This rigorous data cleaning process introduces the risk of survivorship bias, since only firms with
many specific data observations are included in the final sample. There is a risk that the sample is biased
towards more qualitative, media-covered, and transparent firms. Whether this bias systematically affects
our observed relationship between ESG rating and stock performance remains unclear, as we do not have
the resources to evaluate why the databases are missing specific data points. We cannot mitigate the risk
of survivorship bias beyond acknowledging it as a limitation to our study.

3.4 Control Variable Winsorization

Our primary analysis winsorizes the accounting control variables to mitigate the disproportionate influ-
ence of outliers. More specifically, unbounded control variables (EBIT) are winsorized symmetrically at
the top and bottom 0.5 percentiles, while non-negative variables (Debt, Cash, CR, and P/B) are win-
sorized only at the top 0.5 percentile. Furthermore, log(Market Capitalization) is not winsorized, as the
minimum threshold of $250 million already limits smaller outliers, and larger firms generally exhibit more
typical, rather than outlying, behavior (Resnick, Sheikh, and Song, 2009). After winsorization, the final
stock sample consists of 2,012 stocks. Table 16 (Appendix C) shows that our main findings are robust
whether we use the winsorized sample or not.

3.5 Fama-French Four-Factor Data

Fama-French Four-Factors are gathered from the F. Fama and R. French Data Library. The developed
markets dataset is used to ensure compatibility for our U.S. and European samples. The developed
markets index covers 23 developed countries, the vast majority of which (18 out of 23) are either the U.S.
or European. There is no explicit dataset for the U.S. and Europe. The FF3 and momentum factors are
gathered separately as the database lacks a combined FF4 sample. (F. Fama R. French Data Library,
2025)

3.6 Operating Performance Measures

To empirically assess the operational performance dynamics of ESG-invested firms, a set of firm-level
key financial indicators is collected for both the pre-crisis and crisis periods. Specifically, return on
assets, asset turnover, and operating margin are selected as key performance measures. These metrics
are retrieved from the Capital IQ database for the calendar quarter ending December 31, 2021 (pre-crisis),
and the calendar quarter ending June 30, 2022 (final quarter of the crisis).

3.7 Stakeholder Loyalty Data

We expand on the loyalty framework introduced by Albuquerque et al. (2020) by incorporating additional
proxies for stakeholder loyalty. Specifically, we utilize the advertising expenditures-to-sales ratio, percent-
age of institutional ownership, and average employee tenure – sourced from the Capital IQ database – as
proxies for customer, investor, and employee loyalty, respectively. Furthermore, to empirically examine
the theories proposed by Heinkel et al. (2001), we collect data on “sin” product sales ratios as a proxy
for identifying polluting firms.
   Due to data limitations, Capital IQ does not provide historical time-stamped records for the selected
metrics. Instead, only the most recently recorded values are available, without specification of the exact
measurement date. Therefore, these values were likely recorded after the defined event window in 2022,
introducing potential concerns regarding temporal alignment. Moreover, the dataset is particularly con-
strained with respect to advertising expenditure, which is available for only 386 firms within the sample.

must be interpreted with caution, acknowledging the limitations in data completeness and timing.

4 Methodology

This section outlines the methodology used to derive our empirical results. In summary, our methodology
employs a series of cross-sectional OLS regressions and pooled panel OLS regressions with interaction
effects to isolate the effect of ESG ratings on stock performance. Across all regression models, standard
errors are clustered at the firm level to account for intra-firm correlation and to address potential het-

                                             9
eroscedasticity. Sections 4.1–4.3 present our baseline regression models. In Sections 4.4–4.6, we introduce
additional extensions to our methodology. Finally, Section 4.7 defines the event periods analyzed in our
study.

4.1 Measuring Stock Performance

Stock return performance was evaluated using cumulative realized returns, average monthly returns,
average monthly abnormal returns, daily stock return volatility, and idiosyncratic daily stock return
volatility across the study periods. Positively significant realized and abnormal returns indicate superior
stock returns, while negatively significant volatility metrics indicate greater stock price stability and lower
risk.

Cumulative Realized Returns
Cumulative realized returns, or buy-and-hold returns, are calculated to assess the direct, non-market-
adjusted, cumulative stock price returns during the periods of study. These are computed to evaluate the
realized return of each stock, without accounting for its market risk or exposure to systematic factors.

Volatility and Idiosyncratic Volatility
Total volatility, referred to as Volatility in table formats, is measured as the standard deviation of
daily stock returns during the period of study. Idiosyncratic volatility is calculated as the standard
deviation of residuals (εi) from the daily Fama-French Four-Factor model estimated over the same period.

Idiosyncratic Risk is excluded as a control variable to avoid multicollinearity, given its conceptual overlap
with the dependent variable.

4.2 Cross-Sectional OLS Regression Model

We employ a cross-sectional OLS regression model to evaluate the relationship between ESG ratings and
stock performance during the study period, using the five performance measures as dependent variables
and including firm-level control variables. The ESG rating and control variables are lagged one year from
the period of study, thereby mitigating any concerns of simultaneity.

In this model, Yi denotes the dependent variable for firm i, which may refer to cumulative realized returns,
realized returns, abnormal returns, total volatility, or adjusted volatility, depending on the specification.
ESGi,t−1 is the ESG rating measured one year prior to the performance period, and Xi,t−1 is a vector of
lagged firm-level control variables. The corresponding coefficients are represented by the vector γ. δj(i)

industry j to which firm i belongs. The error term εi captures all remaining unexplained variation.

4.3 Pooled Panel OLS Regression Model

To assess whether the potential effect of ESG ratings on stock performance varies between crisis and
non-crisis periods, we estimate a pooled panel regression model using data from the 2022 crisis period
and a pre-event window covering the full calendar year of 2021, a period characterized by a strong stock
market and relative economic stability (IMF, 2021). Whereas Lins et al. (2017) employ a longer non-crisis
period, we restrict our pre-period to 2021 for two primary reasons. First, extending the non-crisis period
into 2020 is not feasible due to the financial instability and market disruptions caused by the COVID-19
crisis. Second, for periods beyond 2022, Refinitiv Eikon lacks ESG data for a substantial portion of our
stock sample, limiting the reliability of more recent observations.
   For the pooled panel regressions, the control variables are updated with a one-year lag, and the ESG
rating is constant with a two-year lag from the crisis period (2020), similar to Lins et al. (2017). More
specifically, the panel observations for 2021 are matched with firm-level control variables measured in
2020, while those for 2022 are matched with controls from 2021. The panel observations for 2021 and
2022 are matched with ESG ratings captured in 2020, ensuring that all explanatory variables are lagged,
thereby mitigating any concerns of simultaneity.

   Abnormal returns are estimated using betas derived from an estimation period beginning five years
before the crisis period, following Lins et al. (2017), and ending before the pre-period. The four-year
beta estimation period thus spans from January 2017 to December 2020.

4.4 Operating Performance Measures

In our cross-sectional regressions on operational performance, control variables are limited to log(Market
Capitalization), Debt, and Cash, as well as continental and industry fixed effects to prevent overfitting
and preserve statistical power.

                    ∆Performancei,t = α + β1 · ESGi,t−1 + γ Xi,t−1 + δj + εi (VII)

In this cross-sectional regression, ∆Performancei,t represents the change in operational performance for
firm i between the fourth calendar quarter of 2021 and the second calendar quarter of 2022. The indepen-
dent variable of interest is the lagged ESG score, ESGi,t−1, which captures the firm’s sustainability profile
prior to the observed performance change. The vector Xi,t−1 includes control variables: log(Market Cap-
italization), Debt, Cash, and EU Dummy. Industry fixed effects, denoted δj , are included to account for
unobserved sector-specific influences on performance. The error term εi captures idiosyncratic variation
unexplained by the model.

4.5 Stakeholder Loyalty Framework

The effect of stakeholder loyalty on ESG resilience is examined by incorporating the loyalty proxies in our
pooled panel regression models. More specifically, our realized and abnormal return models are expanded
upon by using an ESG dummy variable indicating that a firm is in the top quartile of ESG ratings, and a
triple interaction term indicating that a firm is in the top quartile of both ESG rating and loyalty proxy
for our sample. This term is set to capture whether a potential ESG effect is proxied for or moderated
by increased stakeholder loyalty.

4.6 Robustness Checks

To address concerns of ESG rating heterogeneity raised by Berg et al. (2022), both Refinitiv Eikon and
MSCI ESG scores are applied across the core cross-sectional and pooled panel regressions. Furthermore,
we estimate our core regressions using the COVID-19 period to test our findings’ robustness across
different types of crises. In addition to structural robustness checks, we conduct sensitivity analyses
by varying winsorization criteria, dummy variables, event windows, control variables, and other key
specifications to assess our empirical results’ sensitivity to methodological changes (view Appendix B
and C).

4.7 Defined Event Window

Our core analysis window spans from January 1st to June 30th, 2022. This six-month period represents
one of the most turbulent market periods in recent years, evidenced by a 19.97% decrease in the MSCI All
Country World Index (MSCI, 2025). The STOXX Europe 600 fell by 16.52% (STOXX, 2025) and the S&P
500 by approximately 21%, representing the worst first two quarters of a year for the S&P 500 since 1970
(Melgar et al., 2022). Whereas Lins et al. (2017) characterized the 2008 financial crisis as a trust shock,
we interpret the first half of 2022 as a multi-dimensional market shock, driven by inflationary pressures,
geopolitical tensions, and tightening monetary policy. Annual inflation rates surged in December 2021
across Europe and the U.S., followed shortly by the Russo-Ukrainian war outbreak, further accelerating
oil and energy prices. European GDP growth remained subdued throughout the period, and the U.S.
recorded negative growth for the first two consecutive quarters, meeting the most common definition of
a recession (Claessens, Ayhan Kose, 2009). (Melgar et al. 2022)
   Our study also incorporates the stock market crash following COVID-19 to assert robustness for our
models across two distinct crises. Following Albuquerque et al. (2020), we define the event window as
February 24 to March 17, 2020, capturing the acute phase of the pandemic-driven downturn. February
24 marks the first trading day when global markets reacted sharply to the escalating spread of COVID-
19 outside of China, particularly in Europe, triggering investor panic. The window ends on March 17,
when President Trump introduced an aggressive monetary and fiscal policy, which globally dampened the
economic downturn (Albuquerque et al. 2020). With a shorter event window, daily returns are employed
instead of monthly.

6 Discussion of Results

Our empirical findings offer valuable insights when compared to our initial hypotheses and the existing
literature. Consistent with Lins et al. (2017) and Albuquerque et al. (2020), our cross-sectional regres-
sions indicate that ESG ratings are positively associated with both realized and abnormal returns during
the event window. Moreover, consistent with Albuquerque et al. (2020), we find that ESG ratings are
associated with lower total return volatility, supporting the view that ESG characteristics enhance stock
price stability. These findings support Hypothesis (i). Moreover, Table 4 indicates that this relationship
is strongly attributed to the social pillar score, which exhibits the highest correlation with the overall
ESG rating and is central to Albuquerque et al. (2020) and Lins et al. (2017).
   In contrast to much of the existing literature, this effect is, however, not significantly pronounced
in the U.S. subsample. Europe has stricter ESG regulations, with frameworks such as SFDR, higher
investor demand for sustainable investments, greater public and institutional trust in ESG frameworks,
and broader societal alignment with sustainability goals (Edelman, 2021) (Gatison et al., 2025). These
factors suggest that ESG ratings carry greater informational and financial relevance in Europe than in
the U.S., potentially explaining the more potent effects observed in the European sample. However, it
remains unclear why this information is not fully reflected in asset prices and why ESG ratings continue
to influence abnormal returns for European stocks significantly.
   Our pooled panel OLS regression models distinguish between crisis and non-crisis periods and yield

                                             25
ambiguous evidence in support of Hypothesis (ii). Regarding realized returns, the effect observed in
Table 2 is explicitly attributable to the crisis period. We observe the inverse pattern for abnormal
returns: a significant effect during non-crisis periods, but no explicit change in this relationship during
the first half of 2022. When examined by region, neither the U.S. nor the European sample reveals a
significant relationship between abnormal returns and ESG ratings. In contrast, the ESG effect on realized
returns differs notably between the two regions. Additionally, there is no evidence that ESG ratings are
negatively correlated with total or idiosyncratic volatility, specifically during the crisis period. Addressing
Hypothesis (ii), our study finds that firms with higher ESG ratings exhibited significantly higher realized
returns during crisis compared to non-crisis periods. However, this relationship is not prominent when
controlling for market expectations, nor is the effect consistent when dividing the sample into regional
subsamples. Furthermore, we find no support for the idea that the crisis period significantly altered the
ESG-volatility relationship, thus yielding inconclusive implications regarding Hypothesis (ii).
   Whereas Lins et al. (2017) and Albuquerque et al. (2020) partially attribute their support for
Hypotheses (i) and (ii) to mechanisms such as enhanced operational performance, stakeholder loyalty,
and trust in socially responsible firms, we find no such evidence in our analysis. The observations from
Tables 7 and 8 indicate that neither stakeholder loyalty nor operational performance was significantly
pronounced among high ESG firms during the crisis. Consequently, our findings offer no explanatory
support for the positive effects of ESG ratings on stock performance observed during the crisis, leaving
Hypothesis (i) empirically supported but theoretically unexplained. The absence of explanatory findings
and ESG effects outside the crisis window implies a broader ESG-return relationship, potentially driven by
unobserved factors beyond the loyalty or performance-based explanations emphasized in prior literature.
However, due to substantial data limitations, especially concerning stakeholder loyalty, we cannot draw
conclusive inferences about the conclusions of our explanatory models.
   Table 10 extends our regression analysis to the COVID-19 crisis period, using daily return data to
evaluate the consistency of our findings across crises. While the results closely resemble those observed for
the 2022 downturn, they again contrast the findings of Albuquerque et al. (2020), who report significant
effects for abnormal returns during the crisis period, but not during the non-crisis intervals. However,
with contrasting methodologies, it becomes difficult to establish direct comparability between our results
and those of Albuquerque et al. (2020).
   Finally, when using MSCI’s ESG ratings rather than Refinitiv’s, our cross-sectional and pooled panel
regressions yield no significant conclusions regarding ESG resilience, as virtually no factor across Table 9 is
significant. Hypothesis (iii) is therefore supported, as both the estimated coefficients and the conclusions
for Hypothesis (i) and (ii), vary depending on the employed ESG rating provider. The correlation
estimate of 0.4398 between the databases’ 2021 sustainability ratings highlights the concern raised by
Berg et al. (2022) regarding the lack of consensus across rating providers. Given Refinitiv’s focus on
objective indicators and MSCI’s emphasis on materiality, the two ratings capture different dimensions
of ESG performance, which likely contributes to the variation in estimated coefficients. We therefore
caution against interpreting our findings as a definitive conclusion on sustainable investing, and instead
frame them as insights specific to Refinitiv’s ESG ratings.

7 Limitations

This section outlines potential limitations related to our dataset, model specifications, and empirical
results. The issue of survivorship bias is addressed separately in Section 3.3.
    In contrast to Lins et al. (2017) and Alves et al. (2025), our pooled panel OLS regression models reveal
a significant relationship between ESG ratings and abnormal returns during the non-crisis period, with no
significant change observed during the 2022 crisis period (see Table 7). This contradicts Hypothesis (ii ).

                                             26
Unlike the mentioned studies, our non-crisis window is limited to a single year, 2021, rather than a multi-
year span, as outlined and justified in Section 4.2. This methodological distinction may partly explain
the divergence in our findings. Notably, 2021 may represent an atypical year for ESG performance, given
its proximity to the COVID-19 crisis, limiting the generalizability of our results. As more post-crisis data
becomes available, future research could revisit this analysis using a longer, forward-looking window to
examine how the 2022 crisis period shaped ESG performance and asset pricing in relation to subsequent
years.
    Furthermore, as outlined in Section 3.7, our dataset is especially limited concerning our loyalty model,
displayed in Tables 7 and 12, and sin product model, displayed in Table 13. Capital IQ does not provide
transparency on the timing of data collection and only reports advertising expenditure for 386 firms
in our sample. Additionally, ESG ratings on joint institutional ownership could not be retrieved from
the Refinitiv F13 database, as in Albuquerque et al. (2020), so institutional ownership was used as
an imperfect proxy for investor loyalty towards sustainable firms. Future research should implement
more comprehensive databases, with annually updated observations, where possible. This is particularly
relevant for advertising expenditure, which is a relatively weak proxy for customer loyalty, and may be
more effectively captured through more direct indicators such as customer retention or satisfaction data.
   The geographic segmentation of our analysis provided valuable insight into regional heterogeneity in
the ESG effect, though it also introduced complexity in interpreting and generalizing the results. More-
over, the relatively small size of the European subsample, limited to 632 stocks (610 when winsorized),
may limit statistical power and reduce the reliability of inferences drawn for that region. Additionally, we
model Fama-French Four-Factor regressions using the developed market index as a benchmark to ensure
comparability between our U.S. and European samples. Using non-region-specific Fama-French factors,
such as applying developed market factors to European stocks, may introduce bias by misrepresenting
local risk premiums and distorting abnormal return estimates. Considering our divergent findings of ESG
effects across geographic contexts, we encourage future research to cover U.S. and European markets in
isolation to improve the results’ focus, accuracy, and generalizability.
   Furthermore, Appendix D outlines diagnostic tests for the cross-sectional and pooled panel OLS regres-
sions, using abnormal returns as the dependent variable. The pooled panel model exhibits right-skewness
and strong leptokurtosis (Table 18), primarily driven by two extreme outliers (Figure 6), suggesting that
more aggressive winsorization could improve model consistency. In both models, the Ramsey RESET
test is significant, indicating potential misspecification and that key nonlinearities or omitted variables
may be present. Our linear models may therefore not fully capture the true nature of the underlying
relationships or the effect of certain explanatory variables.
   The divergence and lack of consensus among ESG rating providers complicate the analysis and inter-
pretation of ESG-related findings. As demonstrated by the contrasting results obtained using Refinitiv
Eikon and MSCI ratings, it is challenging to draw universally generalizable conclusions when relying on a
ratings-based approach. Given the partially subjective and non-standardized nature of these scores, future
research may benefit from analyzing quantifiable sustainability indicators in isolation – such as carbon
emissions, gender diversity, employee satisfaction, and corruption controversies – to enhance objectivity
and comparability across studies.

8 Conclusion

This paper examines the relationship between ESG ratings and stock performance during economic
downturns, specifically focusing on the first half of 2022. Consistent with existing literature, our empirical
results indicate that during the crisis period, ESG ratings are significantly and positively associated with
realized and abnormal returns, and negatively correlated with total volatility. However, in contrast to

                                             27
Lins et al. (2017) and Albuquerque et al. (2020), this relationship was not statistically significant for the
American stock sample. Our results suggest strong heterogeneities between the ESG effect for U.S. and
European companies, highlighting the importance of further research across geographical differences in
regulations, investor demands, and ESG awareness.
   While our baseline cross-sectional regressions reveal a positive relationship between ESG rating and
stock performance, our panel specifications demonstrate that this effect is not attributable to the crisis
period alone. In particular, we observe significant volatility and abnormal return effects outside the crisis
window, suggesting that the ESG effect may reflect a broader, more persistent relationship rather than one
unique to periods of market distress. Moreover, our models cannot attribute ESG performance to either
operational performance or loyalty-driven mechanisms, suggesting that ESG resilience, where present,
may operate through unobserved factors. These factors may involve levels of sustainability orientation
and regulatory frameworks, which are stronger in Europe, where the effects were more pronounced.
However, substantial data limitations prevent definitive conclusions, particularly for the loyalty models.
    In summary, our study contributes to the existing body of literature by revealing a positive relationship
between Refinitiv ESG ratings and stock performance during the first half of 2022, thereby confirming
the financial relevancy of ESG factors during a recent market downturn. Our findings also diverge from
much of the existing literature, as the observed effect does not appear to be uniquely attributable to
the crisis period. Despite certain data limitations, the inability of traditional loyalty and operational
performance models to account for our findings points to alternative resilience mechanisms. It opens
up important avenues for future research. Moreover, our results reveal a more substantial ESG effect
in Europe, encouraging future research to discern what specific geographical and regulatory differences
influence this relationship. Recent regulatory developments within the European Union, such as the
Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), demonstrate a broader trend toward standardized,
transparent sustainability reporting (G. Hatter et al. 2025). With the potential for similar directives to
be adopted globally, it is critical to research how such developments shape sustainable investing.
   Finally, our diverging results raise concerns about the reliability of a ratings-based approach for
assessing the impact of sustainable investing. Instead, we recommend that future research focus on
objective and quantifiable sustainability indicators, such as carbon emissions or gender distributions, to
identify the most material factors. Such an approach could serve as the foundation for developing a more
consistent and transparent ESG rating framework.

and internal consistency of the regression models.

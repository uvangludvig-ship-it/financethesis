Do congressional stock trades generate abnormal returns, and how does public disclosure
affect the magnitude and persistence of these results?

Abstract:

By studying the stock transactions made by U.S. lawmakers, we find that members of Congress
consistently earn abnormal returns on their stock trades, and that a value-weighted portfolio based
on timely disclosure information (under the STOCK Act, 2012), also consistently would have
generated statistically significant alpha during the  period  of  2012-2023. Disaggregating Congress
by group characteristics,  show that certain sub-groups of Congress have performed especially well
and that the timing of disclosures has  a close correlation with how valuable this disclosure
information is for outside investors: longer delays correlate with larger interim returns, and trades
reported on time generally outperform otherwise similar late-reported trades after disclosure.

1.  Introduction

Insider trading, the act of leveraging non-public,  market-moving information for private gain, has
long attracted  attention  from academics, policymakers, and market participants (Seyhun, H. N.
1986,  Leland, H. E. 1992). Regulatory bodies worldwide aim to deter such practices, recognizing
that insider trading undermines investor confidence and calls into question the integrity of financial
markets. Central to understanding insider trading is the concept of the Efficient Market Hypothesis
(EMH). In its strong form, the EMH posits that prices reflect all available information (public and
private), suggesting that achieving abnormal returns consistently is infeasible (Fama, 1970).
However, empirical evidence of insider trading challenges this assumption, highlighting instances
where individuals or groups appear to benefit personally from early access to price-sensitive data,
fueling concerns about unequal opportunities for market participants.  While insider trading often
is associated with corporate executives or major shareholders  (Lakonishok, J., & Lee, I. 2001),  the
issue becomes even more complex when it involves public officials, especially elected lawmakers.
In the early 2010s public unease about congressional stock trading accelerated, when media
investigations, most prominently a 2011 60 Minutes exposé, revealed a series of suspiciously well-
timed trades by lawmakers and their families (Kroft, 2011). These disclosures culminated in
widespread criticism and calls for reform. In response, the U.S. Congress enacted the Stop Trading
on Congressional Knowledge (STOCK) Act in 2012, which explicitly applied insider trading laws
to the members of Congress. It mandated disclosure of security transactions over $1000 within 45
days (112th U.S. Congress, 2012).

Our  research  aims to investigate whether members of the U.S. Congress benefit from confidential
or non-public  information, even after the STOCK act,  and whether  any observed abnormal
performance prevails after the transactions are disclosed. Specifically, this study seeks to answer
the following research question:  Do congressional stock trades generate abnormal returns, and
how does public disclosure affect the magnitude and persistence of these results?  Our hypothesis
is that members of Congress generate alpha, and that a portion of this advantage can be
systematically captured by external investors who act on the information once  filings are made
public.  A natural question  than  grows, how can such an alpha persist in an otherwise  seemingly
efficient  market?

Our study is inspired by and compliments a recent study by former Stockholm Schools of
Economics master students; Balsys and Dalyanci (2024). They found that stocks purchased by U.S.
senators tend to significantly outperform the market, especially during times of economic turmoil,
such as during the COVID pandemic. We extend their study by examining not only whether
Congress generates alpha, but also whether any possible abnormal performance remains after the
information becomes public, as well as a study on the interim returns.  In other words, we further
disaggregate  their results, and  ask: does alpha survive public disclosure? If so, what can we learn
from studying the timing between transaction and disclosure date?

                                    3
To examine this important  question, we  follow  Balsys and  Dalyanci’s  (2024)  methodology of
portfolio  construction.  We create  value-weighted portfolios  based on  61,873  individual  trades
executed by 409 unique U.S. senators and representatives between 3 January 2012 and 15
December 2023.  To calculate daily returns without incorporating fluctuations due to trading
volume (i.e. congress buying and selling parts of their  holdings  resulting in a value change of the
total value of the portfolio), we calculate a net asset value (NAV), set to $100 on the initial date
(January 2nd, 2012).  Tracking this NAV,  based  on each  day's  value-weighted portfolio returns,
gives  us a time series of the congressional portfolio  returns.  If  this portfolio,  based on the actual
transaction dates, generates  abnormal returns compared  to the  S&P 500, we conclude that the
congress indeed generates  alpha  by trading  in  public equities. We then move on to  construct a
NAV  time series based on the filing dates of the congress transactions  (i.e. we construct a value-
weighted portfolio based on the share prices of the  underlying equities, as of the day they were
disclosed).  This time series lets  us study how a trading strategy based on publicly  disclosed
congressional trades would have performed  since the enforcement of the STOCK act.

A drawback of  our  study is that although the STOCK  Act requires members of  Congress to
disclose transactions in stocks, bonds, commodity futures, and other securities, we have only been
able to reliably track their stock trades. The reason is that  the publicly released files  do  not clearly
state  the detailed security identifiers and narrative descriptions, such as CUSIPs, option symbols,
contract terms, and share‑type labels, needed to distinguish bonds, options, futures, and other
derivatives from ordinary equity transactions.  Therefore, we focus purely on  documenting
performance of equity  stock transactions,  which  may not generalize their overall portfolio
performance or risk exposure.  Meaning that our results may not  produce representative  estimates
of the  “actual”  Congress  portfolio.

Similar  to what Balsys and Dalyanci (2024) found, our results show  that  during the studied period
(2012-2023), the value-weighted congress portfolio appreciated 729.75%, compared with the S&P
500 which appreciated 275.25%.  This outperformance could be explained by  simple exposure to
market risk factors, therefore we regressed the daily excess returns of the congress portfolio on the
Carhart four-factor regression model, to find that the  estimated annualized alpha is 5.95%  and
statistically significant, suggesting that the  abnormal performance  is  unexplained by traditional
risk factors.  We  continue  by disaggregating the  performance,  giving insight into how  different
subsets of politicians perform. Interesting findings include senators outperforming representatives,
Democrats outperforming Republicans, and committee differences in both overall return and when
trading  stocks  relevant to their  own  sector.  Finding  statistically significant alpha  on  Congress
trades  based on  transaction  date  leads us to investigate the  less studied  question:  could this
outperformance be monetized by outside investors  (does alpha persist after public disclosure)?

Our results reveal that even portfolios  based on  disclosure date  outperform the market.  Our  delta
analysis revealed interesting information regarding lag and return correlation as well as politicians’
individual behavior. Including,  not  surprisingly,  that  longer delays between transaction and
disclosure are linked to higher  in-between  returns.  We also  found  that  trades reported in
                                    4
compliance with  the STOCK Act  performed  better over the following 12 months  than non-
compliant  filings.

Taken together, these findings  challenge  the  strong version of the Efficient Market Hypothesis.
What is novel in our study is that we find that (i) the Congress advantage survives public disclosure,
(ii) it grows in a predictable way depending on how  closely  the politicians are connected to the
information, and  (iii)  that some politicians may use disclosure timing strategically.

Prior  research  on  US  congressional  trading  performance  has  resulted  in  mixed  findings.
Ziobrowski, Cheng, Boyd, and Ziobrowski (2004) examined every  observable common-stock
trade reported by U.S. senators between 1993 and 1998. They found that a portfolio mimicking
senators’ purchases outperformed the market by more than 10% annually on a trade-weighted basis
after risk adjustments, with abnormal returns persisting for up to a year. The authors attribute this
to the senators’ access to non-public information and the substantial delays in transaction
disclosures, which ranged from five to seventeen months.  However, a subsequent paper published
by  Eggers  & Hainmueller (2013) indicated that, in the period between 2004 and 2008, members
of Congress underperformed relative to standard market indicators.  Belmont, W., Sacerdote, B.,
Sehgal, R. & Van Hoek, I. (2020), also challenged the notion of congressional trading advantages,
arguing  that “congressional investors are simply not that different from other investors”, and any
instances of extraordinary returns were likely driven by a few aggressive traders or just random
luck.  These conflicting findings highlight how difficult it is for the public to know whether
members of Congress actually earn abnormal returns. The  discrepancies stem mainly from the
varied time periods analyzed and the different  methodologies applied.

In the subsequent sections, we detail our data processing, including how we conducted the cleaning,
matching and transformation  of  turning  raw filings into the variables used in the empirical tests.
We also discuss  limitations and challenges we encountered,  largely dependent on the very large
data set used to track the value-weighted NAV.  We then  outline the  empirical  implementation and
how we constructed our analysis.  Based on this  empirical  analysis we move to the results section
where we interpret our results and  how this  helps us answer  our research question, as well as a
discussion of the  robustness of our results,  where applicable.

2.2 Data Cleaning and Transformation

Before  commenting on the cleaning and transformation  process, it is important to note that most
of this process is done through R Studio, due to the large  nature of the files.

Working dataset:  We began the process by cleaning  our working transaction dataset.  After
loading the dataset to R Studio, we parse the file, converting transaction dates to a proper date
format and remove any entries with missing transaction dates. Depending on the analysis, we retain
different columns  required for the portfolio construction, however, in general, the  ones used in all
cases are:  (i)  transaction date, (ii) politician identifier, (iii) stock identifier (PERMNO), (iv) ticker,
(v) transaction type (buy or sell), (vi) transaction amount  and (vii) company name.  We  proceed to
filter the dataset to exclude trades lacking either stock identifier (PERMNO) or ticker symbol. We
also  consider Capital IQ’s company identification (CIQID), which is  under the PERMNO column
(although not the same), for some of the companies missing PERMNO.  Less than two percent of
the  sample  (1,210  out of 61,873)  was filtered out  to avoid unidentifiable positions.

Quiver Quantitative dataset:  For the purpose of  integrating disclosure-date data in our analysis,
we had to incorporate Quiver Quantitative’s recorded transactions as a new sheet into our working
dataset.  Because the Quiver data lacked PERMNOs and sometimes differed in how tickers were
recorded, we first manually  standardized the ticker symbols in this dataset to align with the CRSP
ticker formatting.  We created an Excel formula  that compared the tickers in the Quiver
Quantitative dataset to those in our working dataset and consequently returned  those unmatched.
We reviewed over 600 unmatched tickers  and found the common error to be  the formatting of
tickers  with suffixes for share classes.  It is also important to note that the dataset contained non-
equity securities, which were filtered out.  Once this step was finalized, we proceeded  with  the
cleaning process by loading  both sheets into R Studio.  We standardized date fields by converting
them into a consistent date format.  We also further cleaned and standardized ticker symbols in
both datasets by trimming whitespace and converting them to uppercase for consistency. Next, we
convert the transaction  types  in the Quiver  Quant  data to match the  format in our working dataset
(“buy” and “sell”).  It is important to note that, in this process, we classify “exchange” transactions
in the Quiver Quantitative dataset as buys, due to the difficulty of accurately tracking and matching
each transaction and share allocation  (especially  those transactions linked to previous transactions
outside  our data sample)  within the  time  constraints of this study.

We then generate a match key for each transaction in both datasets, combining the stock ticker,
trade date and transaction type.  We  retain the  associated filing date and then perform a left join to
merge these filing dates into our working dataset. We successfully merged 32,788 out of the 48,925
transactions provided by Quiver Quantitative. Although this represents a smaller sample of the full

dataset, we consider it sufficiently large, since after careful review,  we find the sample  large and

Merging transactions with Price Data:  With the clean trade records and consistent stock

merge the portfolio return data with the factor dataset on the date variable. To ensure alignment,

2.3  Limitations

While we consider the data cleaning and transformation process that we  used  the  best  procedure
to  provide the most information within the time constraint we had, several limitations affect the
interpretation of the results.  Below, we detail the specific considerations affecting both the
transaction-date-based and disclosure-date-based analyses.

Our transaction-date-based analyses rely on the working dataset provided by Balsys & Dalyanci
(2024) and  are  subject to a few data constraints that appear when we process this dataset. First, we
exclude any trades that lacked a valid stock identifier (ticker or PERMNO), which removes a small
portion (under 2%) of the records. While this filter ensures all included positions can be tracked  in
the stock price dataset, it reduces the sample size.  In addition, the dollar amount of each transaction

                                    8
is only an estimate based on a midpoint, since the actual value is hidden and the transaction’s value
is  reported in a range.  This introduces the possibility of imprecision in trade valuations, and thus,
in the calculation of each politician’s real position.  When merging the trades with market prices,
we also had to account for timing mismatches. If a trade’s reported execution date fell on a non-
trading day or a date with no price data, we aligned it with the next available trading day’s price.
This  further impacts our attempt to create an “actual” representation, and the very nature of these
errors  implies the potential existence of a  small  number  of errors in the dataset.

The analyses based on public disclosure dates  are  essentially  constrained by the integration of our
second dataset  (the dataset provided by Quiver Quantitative)  into our working dataset.  Since the
dataset did not provide a unique stock identifier (PERMNO),  it  used  different ticker formats for a
large fraction of tickers,  and included non-equity stocks, not all reported trades could be matched.
We successfully linked roughly two-thirds of all the transactions in the dataset  by matching ticker,
date and transaction type, but the remaining trades without  a  match had to be left out of the
disclosure-date-based analyses.  We have verified that this  smaller sample  of  trades  remained
diverse (to avoid sample-selection bias), so it should be representative of the overall data. However,
readers should note that some trades (particularly those we could not reconcile between the
datasets)  are  not  reflected  in  the  disclosure-date  results.  Additionally,  when  categorizing
transaction types,  transactions  recorded as “exchange” in the  Quiver  dataset were standardized as
buys in our merged data.  This  means a few transactions are categorized in a simplified manner
rather than in their original form. Finally, as with the execution dates, if a public disclosure date
occurred on a non-trading day, we associated that disclosure with the next trading day’s market
data.  We also found 30 transactions with  a  disclosure date prior to  the  transaction date. Although
we fixed them, that could further signal potential errors in the dataset.

In conclusion, while our results aim to provide meaningful information, readers should interpret
the findings with caution and not consider them as definitive or absolute.
3.  Empirical Implementation

This section outlines the empirical implementation and methodology of our analysis, detailing how
we construct portfolios of congressional trades, specify regression models for abnormal returns,
and examine patterns in trading behavior. We focus on methodology: how the data  is  used to test
for insider advantages, the rationale behind each step, and the structure of the models employed.

Our empirical approach is to simulate the investment performance of  Congress trades under
various scenarios and groupings, in order to detect any systematic ability of Congress members to
beat the market. In essence, we ask:  If one were to mirror the stock trades made by members of
Congress, how would those investments perform? And further,  do  timing or trader characteristics
(party, chamber, committee, etc.) affect this performance?

                                    9
3.1   Standard   Methodology:   Portfolio   Construction   and   Return
Measurement

The primary analysis is built around calendar-time portfolios that mimic congressional trades. This
approach, commonly used in studies of insider trading and long-run event outcomes (e.g., Jensen,
1968; Fama, 1998),  involves  aggregating trades into a portfolio and evaluating its returns over
time, rather than examining each trade in isolation. By forming portfolios, we account for the fact
that Congress members make many overlapping trades, and we avoid the biases that can arise from
simply summing individual trade returns. This methodology aligns with prior research on insider
investment performance, specifically,  Balsys & Dalyanci  (2024),  since we are building on their
paper. They  construct  several  portfolios  that mirror U.S.  Congress’ trades and find  they  earn
significantly higher returns than the market, suggesting an informational advantage. We adopt  the
same  portfolio-based approach here, tailored to  the  dataset of congressional trades.

Implementation of the Portfolio:  We create a hypothetical investor who “follows” all stock trades
made by Congress members. Each trade is incorporated into the portfolio on the day it occurred
(the transaction date) at the reported adjusted closing price.  If a congress member buys shares of a
company, our portfolio buys an equivalent position; if a member sells, our portfolio sells or
unwinds that position. Because congressional disclosures report transaction amounts in ranges (e.g.
$15,000–$50,000), we use  the midpoint of the reported range as the trade size. We then estimate
the number of shares traded by dividing this dollar amount by the stock’s adjusted closing price
on the transaction date.  For buy transactions, this yields a positive number of shares; for sells, we
record a negative share quantity (indicating a reduction in that holding). This share estimation is
necessary to consistently track the portfolio’s holdings and value over time, given that exact share
counts are not disclosed in the data.

One practical assumption we make is to allow for initial  holdings in cases where  sales  cannot be
tracked to  prior  purchases  for a given politician. In other words, if  the  company  weight is below
zero in any member’s portfolio,  we assume they owned those shares before our sample began
(Balsys & Dalyanci,  2024).  Without this adjustment, the portfolio would attempt to sell a stock it
never “bought,” resulting in a short position. To avoid introducing short-selling (which is not our
focus, as we aim to replicate the legislators’ long-only investments), we add a hypothetical opening
position  to the portfolio equal to the sale quantity for that stock-politician pair. This effectively
treats the sale as closing an existing position rather than creating a short sale. After this adjustment,
no stock position in the portfolio goes negative, aligning the simulation with a realistic scenario
that members are selling shares they already owned.

Using this constructed time series of portfolio returns, we calculate several performance  metrices.
These include the total cumulative return over the sample period, the annualized average return,
the volatility (standard deviation of daily returns), and the Sharpe ratio as a summary of risk-
adjusted performance. The Sharpe ratio is computed as the portfolio’s average excess return (over
a risk-free rate) divided by its return standard deviation. These  metrices  provide a first look at

                                   11
whether congressional trades, in aggregate, tend to outperform broad benchmarks. For instance, if
the portfolio significantly beats a broad market index like the S&P 500 over the same period, it
would hint at stock-picking skill or inside information. We will later compare the portfolio’s
trajectory to that of the S&P 500 graphically and in summary statistics. Indeed, our preliminary
calculations show that a portfolio of all Congress members’ trades achieves a substantial total
return over the period (hundreds of percentage points), noticeably higher than the market’s return,
motivating a closer examination of risk-adjusted performance.

Risk-Adjusted Performance and Regression Model:  While raw returns are informative, they may
be driven by exposure to well-known risk factors (for example, if Congress members mostly buy
small-cap or value stocks that happened to do well). To isolate abnormal returns  (returns beyond
what can be explained by standard risk premia)  we employ the Carhart four-factor regression
model (Carhart, 1997). This is an extension of the Fama–French three-factor model (Fama &
French, 1993) commonly used in asset pricing to evaluate performance. The model includes four
factors: the market excess  return, a size factor, a value factor, and a momentum factor. By
regressing the portfolio’s returns on these factors, we can estimate the portfolio’s alpha (the
intercept), which represents the average daily abnormal return after accounting for factor
exposures. The use of this model is standard in evaluating investment strategies and has been used
in prior literature on insider trading performance (e.g., Jeng, Metrick & Zeckhauser, 2003) to
determine if insiders’ gains persist after controlling for market  trends.

words, evidence of stock-picking skill or insider advantage. Conversely, a non-significant or zero
alpha would suggest that any outperformance can be fully explained by just loading on certain risk
factors (for example, if Congress members simply tilt toward small-cap stocks which had high
returns, that would show up as a strong  βₛ).

We run this four-factor regression on the overall Congress portfolio to estimate whether, as a whole,

sub-portfolios (described below) to see if abnormal performance is concentrated in certain groups.
Each regression yields not only an estimated  α  (with a t-statistic to assess significance), but also

portfolios.

It is worth noting that we could also examine simpler benchmarks, such as the Capital Asset
Pricing Model (CAPM) with just the market factor, or the Fama–French three-factor model
without momentum. However, we choose the four-factor model since momentum has been shown
to be an important driver of stock returns (Carhart, 1997) and could potentially be related to
congressional trading behavior (for example, if lawmakers chase trending stocks or alternatively
exploit reversals). By using the four-factor model,  we reduce the risk of omitted-variable bias in
detecting abnormal performance.
3.2 Transaction-date-based  Analyses

The  starting point of our thesis consists of building an overall Congress portfolio  based on the
transaction date, meticulously  following  the methodology outlined  in  (3.1).  The aim of this step  is
contextual and twofold: (i) to test whether Congress, in aggregate, appears to outperform the
market benchmark, and (ii) to identify any  differences from the results reported by Balsys &
Dalyanci (2024).

A key part of our empirical implementation is to investigate heterogeneity in trading performance
across different groups of legislators. While the aggregate Congress portfolio is informative, it
may mask important nuances. Prior research provides reason to suspect that not all politicians have
the same informational advantages. For instance, Ziobrowski et al. (2004) found that U.S. Senators’
trades outperformed the market by about 85 basis points per month, whereas a similar study by
Ziobrowski et al. (2011) found House members beat the market by roughly 55 basis points per
month,  a sizable outperformance for both, but Senators’ returns were noticeably higher. This gap
could be due to Senators having access to more sensitive  information (given their often broader
powers and longer terms) or simply being fewer in number and possibly more sophisticated
investors. Similarly, evidence has hinted at the possibility that members of certain committees (e.g.,
those dealing with defense, healthcare, or finance) might trade advantageously in stocks related to
upcoming legislation in their domain. We aim to systematically examine these possibilities by
breaking down the analysis by group.

Political Party:  We  divide  the data by political affiliation, creating a Democrat portfolio and a
Republican portfolio. This segmentation addresses whether one party’s lawmakers trade more
successfully than the other’s.  Examining  party differences  is  useful for  a few reasons.  One is that
party affiliation might correlate with other factors (for example, majority vs. minority status in

                                   13
Congress at a given time, which could affect access to information, or ideological differences in
approach to stock ownership). Another is public perception,  media sometimes speculate whether
one party is more “in touch” with business or more prone to active trading. By computing
performance separately, we can see if both Democrats and Republicans  generate  abnormal returns
or if any gap exists. The portfolio construction for each is  similar: include all trades by Democrats
in one and by Republicans in the other, then compute daily returns and run performance regressions.
We will report each party’s portfolio  α  and factor loadings. For example, it could turn out  that one
party’s portfolio has a higher market beta or more small-cap exposure than the other,  insights that
might reflect differences in portfolio preferences.

We also segment portfolio performance  by the U.S. presidential regime in power, to see if
Congress members perform differently under different presidencies. The analysis defines distinct
time periods corresponding to the administrations of Barack Obama  (Democratic  President, Dec
2009-  Jan  2017), Donald Trump (Republican  President, Jan 2017  –  Jan 2021) and Joe Biden
(Democratic  President, Jan 2021  –  Dec 2023).  For our sample, we consider the portion of Obama
and Biden’s  terms covered by the data: starting from 2012 up to  2024.  We study  whether each
portfolio does better when the President is of the same party (what we classify as own party) versus
when the President is from the opposing party.  We would want to note that, although the result is
of interest,  it only comprises three terms (not a large sample),  making the result less significant.

Chamber (House vs. Senate):  We  similarly  split  the transactions by the legislator’s chamber and
construct two separate portfolios: one that includes only trades made by members of the House of
Representatives, and one with only trades by Senators. This allows us to compute performance
metrics and  α  for each chamber’s trades independently. The construction of these portfolios
mirrors the overall portfolio methodology described above, but filters the trade list by chamber.
By comparing the results, we can test whether Senate trades indeed outperform House trades (as
earlier studies suggested). In practice, we will look at the difference in annualized returns and the
difference in regression intercepts between the two. To formally test differences, one could interact
a “Senate” dummy in the factor regression (pooled regression with an interaction term affecting
the intercept) or simply compare the two  α  estimates with their standard errors. Our approach is
primarily to present them side by side; if one is significantly positive and the other is not, that in
itself is telling. If both are positive, we consider whether their magnitudes differ meaningfully
(though a statistical test of difference in alpha is an extension we note qualitatively). The
motivation here is straightforward: if one chamber shows stronger abnormal performance, it might
indicate that chamber’s members have greater or more exploitable information. It could also reflect
differences in the types of stocks they trade or how quickly their trades become public (Senators
and House members have the same legal requirements under the STOCK Act, but perhaps cultural
or procedural differences in compliance exist).  We further divide the  groups to include political
affiliation, create four subgroups:  (i)  House Democrats, (ii) House Republicans, (iii) Senate
Democrats and (iv) Senate Republicans.  This party-level analysis was partly inspired by findings

                                   14
in Ziobrowski et al. (2011), who noted that within their House sample, Democrats’ trades
outperformed Republicans’ by a margin (about 9% vs. 2% annually).

Committee Membership:  To delve into potential sources of informational advantage, we analyze
performance by congressional committee. We obtained data on each member’s committee
assignments (e.g., Finance, Energy, Health, Armed Services, etc.) and for each committee, we
construct a portfolio comprising trades made by members of that committee. A given legislator
can belong to multiple committees, so a single trade may appear in multiple committee-based
portfolios (for example, if a Senator on both the Finance and Health committees buys a
pharmaceutical stock, that trade is relevant to both committees). While this introduces overlap,
each committee portfolio is evaluated on its own,  effectively treating “committee member trading”
as a group activity.  The expectation is that if committee-specific information is important, we
might see higher abnormal returns in committees whose domain closely ties to market-moving
information (such as Finance or Defense). Alternatively, committees with less market-sensitive
jurisdiction (say, the Agriculture Committee, unless trading agricultural stocks) might not show
any special performance.  We have identified 22 distinct committees of interest in our dataset and
created portfolios for each, including two dummy variables created for research purposes, inspired
by Balsys and Dalyanci (2024).  These two variables are “Economic Insight” (named  Economic
Knowledge Access Group) and “Economic Impact” (named  Economic Policy Influence Group).
Each equals 1 if a member served on at least one committee assessed to provide access to economic
knowledge/data or direct influence over economic policy, respectively.

For each committee portfolio, we calculate the same performance metrics and run the four-factor
regression to estimate alpha. We then compare these alphas across committees. This yields a cross-
sectional distribution of performance by committee, which we can analyze for patterns. Do any
committees stand out as particularly successful (significantly positive alpha) or unsuccessful? We
also compute the  Sharpe ratio  for each committee’s portfolio to see the risk-adjusted return from
that committee’s trades. It’s important to note that some committees have far more trades than
others (e.g., a committee that many members of Congress belong to, or one where members are
very active traders). We will be cautious in interpreting results for small-sample committees.
Nonetheless, this exercise helps  us understand  whether informational advantages are broad-based
or concentrated. If, for example, the Intelligence Committee or Defense-related committees earn
the highest abnormal returns, that could indicate those members have access to sensitive, non-
public information (such as defense contract decisions or national security developments) that they
might be leveraging in their stock trades. On the other hand, if no committee shows a significantly
different performance, it would suggest that any insider advantage is generalized rather than linked
to committee-specific information flow.

Individual Politician Performance:  Beyond group averages, we also examine the  distribution of
performance at the individual level. Each member of Congress in the dataset has his or her own
sequence of trades, which we can evaluate as an individual “portfolio”. While any single member’s
trades might be too few to reliably estimate a multi-factor alpha, we can still calculate their
                                   15
personal portfolio’s total return and compare it to benchmarks. We thus compute, for each
lawmaker, the cumulative return of an investment strategy following only that person’s trades.
This involves the same methodology of buying when they buy and selling  when they sell (using
that member’s subset of transactions). We record each politician’s total and annualized return over
their active trading period, as well as the number of trades and days active. The result is a list of
individual performance outcomes.  We do not expect to find that  every  member beats the market,
in fact, it is possible that only a subset of members  accounts  for most of the excess gains, with
others   perhaps   performing   closer   to   average   or   even   lagging.   Identifying   whether   the
outperformance is driven by a few star traders (and who they are) or is a more pervasive
phenomenon is important for interpreting the results. For example, if we find that the top 5% of
politicians achieved extremely high gains while the rest did not, that might indicate that a small
group either had exceptional information or took unusual risks. Conversely, if most members have
a  modest  outperformance, it would signal a more widespread  (albeit smaller)  insider advantage.
3.3 Disclosure-date-based Analyses

A unique aspect of our study is  that we extend our performance evaluation to a disclosure-date-
based analysis. We  start by  replicating the same aggregate Congress portfolio  as we did in the first
section of the transaction-date-based analysis, following the  portfolio  methodology  defined in
Section  3.1.  All else equal, we  shift from  the  transaction date as the “entry” position to the date
that the transaction was filed (disclosure date).  We compare it with a “compliant” portfolio
(filtering trades  by  compliance  with the 45-day STOCK Act mandate).  This  base analysis  allows
us to test  (on a general level)  whether  potential  abnormal  returns  associated with congressional
transactions persist after the transactions become  publicly available.  It also establishes  grounds for
studying why alpha might persist  once the information becomes public, and  if an outside investor
can benefit from  following  disclosed  congressional  trades.  If significant excess returns continue to
be observed after disclosure, it would suggest  a potential violation of the strong form of the
Efficient Market Hypothesis (Fama, 1970), or, in other words,  a market imperfection.  While this
study is important and informative, the limitations outlined in Section 2.3 must be considered when
reading, and we insist on interpreting the results with caution, and not as an absolute result.  Upon
establishing  whether abnormal performance survives public disclosure, we  then ask: what can we
learn  from the  period between transaction and disclosure date?

Delta Analysis:  Following the portfolio return analysis, we turn to an examination of the timing of
trade disclosures:  the  “delta” between a transaction’s date and the disclosure date.  For context, the
STOCK Act (2012) mandates that members of Congress disclose their trades within 45 days of
execution. However, even a delay of a few weeks could allow an informed trader to profit before
others are aware of their actions.  This analysis covers two questions: (i) whether variations in
disclosure lag are systematically related to returns, and (ii) whether individual politicians exhibit
consistent patterns in their filing delays. In what follows, we outline the empirical implementation
for the delta analysis.

The second component of the delta analysis  is the study of persistence (and predictability) of
disclosure timing behavior.  In other words, we investigate if the variation in filing lags is largely
idiosyncratic (random from trade to trade)  or if each legislator has a predictable pattern.  Evidence
of persistent behavior  would  suggest  that disclosure timing  is  (potentially)  an intentional practice
rather than purely driven by random factors.  Our approach here is to construct a time-series of
filing delays for each politician and apply both autocorrelation analysis and panel regressions to
detect patterns.  We  only include politicians with at least 10 transactions, spanning a period of at
least 90 days. For each qualifying politician, we then derive additional variables characterizing
their trading disclosure behavior over time. One key variable is the previous  filing lag for each
trade: for each trade by a given individual, we record the filing lag of their immediately prior trade
(if existent). We also include rolling measures such as the moving average of the past 3 and 5 filing
lags, or an indicator for batch filings (multiple trades disclosed on the same date).  This procedure
allows us to describe each transaction  in the context of the  politician's  history.

We first assess the “serial” dependence in  filing delays at the individual level by calculating a lag-
1 autocorrelation  of their filing delay series  (measuring the correlation between the filing lag  of a
transaction  and  that of  the preceding one).  We also compute a similar statistic for compliance,
effectively capturing  the tendency to repeat the same compliance behavior on consecutive trades.
To evaluate the significance of the autocorrelations, we use  t-tests  for autocorrelation = 0.  Finding
that a large share of members have a positive and significant autocorrelation would imply that non-
random behavior is prevalent.

While the autocorrelation analysis treats each member separately, we also implement panel
regressions to analyze  persistence across the full sample.  These regressions  model each trade’s
disclosure lag as a function of the legislator’s previous lag  (similar process to an AR(1) process),
pooling all trades but controlling for individual-specific effects through fixed or random intercepts.

We then extend this model by progressively adding controls,  including: (i)  party affiliation and
chamber  (in case  group-level traits explain persistence), (ii)  time effects  (to capture  cyclical
reporting patterns), and  (iii)  transaction-level indicators  (for instance,  transaction type  and batch
sells). This allows us to isolate whether the observed persistence is due to structural rather than
individual  behavior. Across specifications, we cluster standard errors at the legislator level  (to
account for autocorrelation  in each politician’s sequence of trades)  and test robustness by
subsample (e.g. frequent vs. infrequent traders).

We complement the fixed‑effects panel with additional diagnostics.  We estimate a random-
intercept mixed model  to verify that the persistence coefficient (ϕ) remains significant once
legislator‑level heterogeneity is treated as a random component and to quantify how much of the
total variance in delays  comes  from stable  and  unobserved differences across members. We also
implement a two‑state Markov  table  of compliance, comparing the probability of a late (or
on‑time) filing conditional on the previous filing’s status with its unconditional probability.  If the
conditional probability of a filing being late  (given that the previous filing by the same politician
was also late) exceeds the  overall late-filing rate  (how often filings are late, in general), it signals
systematic non-compliance, offering a complementary measure of persistence. Together, the
mixed model and Markov diagnostics confirm whether observed persistence is robust to
unobserved heterogeneity.

To  ensure that the relationships between disclosure lag and returns is  not  just a result from  broad
economic conditions, we  add to  the extended regression business‑cycle controls  sourced  from
FRED  (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, 2024): (i) quarterly real GDP growth, (ii) the monthly
unemployment rate, and (iii) the NBER recession dummy (coded = 1 in recession months). Each
trade is matched to the corresponding calendar quarter; the three series are merged into a single

                                   18
quarterly macro dataset and added to the specification as additional covariates. These variables
help explain any  cyclical effects that could jointly influence disclosure timing and interim returns.

To conclude the delta analysis, we  calculate  the buy‑and‑hold return  for each of the transaction-
to-filing buckets (created in the first step of the delta analysis)  over the 252 trading days (one year)
following the transaction. We then compare these one‑year returns for trades disclosed on time
versus those disclosed late.

Taken together, the cross‑sectional regressions, persistence diagnostics, business‑cycle controls,
and long‑run return comparison provide a comprehensive  analysis  for assessing whether delayed
disclosure  informs of  repeatable informational advantage and whether such advantages extend
beyond the moment a trade becomes public.

4.  Empirical  Results

This section presents the empirical  results  on whether, and to what extent, members of
the  U.S.  Congress earn abnormal stock‑market returns. Leveraging the transaction data and
portfolio‑construction methods detailed earlier, we first evaluate aggregate performance using
politicians’ actual trade dates,  and  examine how that performance varies by party affiliation,
chamber, and committee membership.

We then test whether the advantage survives once trades become public by analysing performance
on filing (disclosure) dates and analysing how the length of the disclosure lag affects pre‑ and
post‑filing returns.

Throughout, we benchmark against the S&P  500 and adjust returns with the Carhart  Four‑Factor
model to isolate true alpha.  The emphasis is on interpreting the magnitude, statistical significance,
and economic implications of the findings, highlighting what the outcomes reveal about
information asymmetry, market efficiency, and differences across political groups.

Each figure  and table  is introduced where relevant, with  clear  reminders of portfolio construction
so that the graphs remain self‑contained for readers who turn directly to the  results  section.

4.4 Committee-Based Analysis

We now expand our analysis to study performance on a committee level. Congressional
committees handle specific policy areas and are often in hand with sensitive, sector-specific
information.

Analysing  portfolios at this level helps us understand whether membership on certain committees
correlates with investment advantages. Additionally, we also explore whether such advantages, if
present, manifest mainly in committee members trading their own-sector stocks versus other-
sector stocks. Following Balsys & Dalyanci (2024), we introduce “economic insights” and
“economic impact” variables, derived from memberships in committees where members possess
important economic data (or legislative insights), or who have direct impact, respectively. To
facilitate understanding, we have included in the Appendix a table that comprises the variables
studied with their respective definitions  (Appendix 4.1,  Appendix 4.2).

                                   27
Please refer to  Appendix  4.3  to view the table that summarizes the overall performance metrics
across committees. Our results reveal significant disparities in returns, alpha generation and risk
profiles.  We have found a  considerable heterogeneity in returns across committees.  The  House
Select Committee on Economic Disparity and Fairness in Growth,  achieved the highest total return
(1140.23%) as well as the highest alpha (10.04%).  It is  however  important  to mention that due to
the small number of politicians in our analysis assigned to this committee, the results might be
driven more by individual performance. The House Committee on Small Business delivered a total
return of  (979.91%) and Senate Committee on Health, Education, Labor, and Pensions  returned
(923.71%),  also  demonstrating  remarkable performance. We  deem these results more informative
due to the  larger  sample of politicians in these committees and the amount of trades studied (+20
politicians  and  +3000  transactions).  On  the  other  hand,  some  committees  demonstrated
unexpectedly weak performance, such as the House Committee on Science, Space and Technology,
which recorded the lowest total return (335.4%).

We conducted a  Carhart  Four-Factor regression analysis  (Appendix 4.5), and these regressions
confirm statistically significant alphas across most of the committees. Interestingly, we find that
members who have access to important economic insights, or possess a level of power in regards
to impact  (our two research variables), have displayed significant positive alphas (t-statistics of
10.17  and  9.76  respectively),  indicating  that  these  politicians  (potentially)  trade  on  the
economically relevant information they possess. As expected, we also identify through our results
differences in investment style and exposure. For instance, the House Committee on Energy and
Commerce exhibited a more positive tilt towards small-cap stocks (SMB  =  0.2176, t-stat = 27.28)
compared to other committees, or the House Committee on Science, Space, and Technology with
a more negative exposure to momentum (MOM =  -0.1071, t-stat =  -5.63).

However, to directly test the presence of informational advantages, we study how committee
members do when trading the stocks under the umbrella of their committee (classified as “own-
sector” in our analysis), versus the stocks unrelated to the committee´s  primary policy focus
(classified as “other-sector” in our analysis)  (Appendix 4.4). Committees such as the House
Committee on Energy and Commerce or the House Committee on Science, Space, and Technology
have the highest proportion of trades in stocks of their own sector (39.3% and 35.2%). The study
becomes  very  interesting  when we compare the performance of  these  trades.  The  House
Committee on Science, Space, and Technology achieves superior own sector returns of 976.95%
(when compared to the returns of transactions of stocks in other sectors, which yielded a 190.35%
return). So, despite being the committee with the lowest total return (335.40%),  this analysis has
shown that they  possibly trade on  privileged  information, supporting the hypothesis that to
accurately identify where informational advantages truly exist within  congressional trading we
must move beyond conclusions on an aggregate study.  (Reminder: the 35.2  % figure is a count of
trade tickets; when returns are dollar‑weighted, the  House Committee on Science, Space, and
Technology’s effective exposure to own‑sector stocks is  a bit  lower).

4.5 Individual-Level Returns

We conclude the transaction-date-based section by examining individual performance. We have
identified significant heterogeneity in returns among individual politicians, with outliers on both
sides of the return spectrum. We have compiled a ranked table (available in  Appendix  Section 6)
that summarizes the individual annualized returns of all politicians between 2012 and 2023.

Politicians at the top of our rankings demonstrate high annualized returns, led by Steve King with
an annualized return of 85.62%, followed by Marlin Stutzman (62.52%) and Christopher Coons
(62.12%). However, we advice caution against interpreting these extreme outcomes purely as
evidence of consistent trading on private information, as these results could be influenced by a few
exceptionally successful trades rather than systematic informational advantages (Steve King, for
instance, has only one reported trade according to our dataset). Manual verification for the top
performers indicated that their returns often come from a limited number of highly “profitable”
trades.

Other considerations for caution include non-informative returns due to some of the individual
trades belonging to the group of non-identifiable transactions. Examples of this are Barry Moore
(-98.97%) and Saxby Chambliss (data unavailable), the latter due  to incomplete transaction
amount records of all his transactions in our dataset, which we cannot manually compile due to
time constraints. However, since we matched 98% (around 60,800 of the 61,872 transactions),
although with the need to be interpreted with caution, our results still provide informative and
accurate insights into the returns of the majority of politicians. The overall distribution of
individual returns remains positive, with a majority of politicians (over 90%) achieving positive
annualized returns. The persistence and frequency of above-market returns by many politicians
support our hypothesis that politicians might trade on insider information.

Taken together, the results of our transaction-date-based analysis provide compelling evidence that
members of Congress achieve abnormal returns even after adjusting for standard risk factors.
Having now established a strong base of abnormal returns based on transaction dates, we now turn
to a key question in our thesis: what happens when the information becomes public?

In the following sections, we shift our focus to the disclosure date,  analysing  whether abnormal
returns persist after disclosure and whether an external investor could feasibly replicate such
performance using publicly available data.

both outperforming the S&P 500, which ends at $260.36. The total returns were 471.03% and

4.7  Disclosure Lag and Return Analysis (Delta Analysis)

In this  last  section  of our thesis, we examine how the length and regularity of the disclosure delay
are themselves a source of economic information. As a reminder, we consider delta (the disclosure
lag)  as  the time between the transaction date and the disclosure date.

We find that  the delta  distribution  is highly skewed, with most filings clustered within the first
weeks of execution, with a compliance rate of 86.9%. The median disclosure lag is  26  days, with
the interquartile range spanning roughly  16  to  36  days  (Appendix 5.1).  We  find  that  delays  might
not be random: our results show that “buy” disclosures arrive on average around 5 days later than
“sale” disclosures (49.6 vs 44.4 days)  (Appendix 5.3).  In terms of political characteristics, we see
that Democrats, in general, have  been  the least compliance  with an  average of 51.2 days lag  (43.2
days for Republicans),  whereas independent members comply the most (average of 31 days lag)
(Appendix 5.4). Interestingly, the Senate filings lag the House by about a week  (Appendix 5.5).

Regarding returns, we see that a higher delta correlates with more cumulative market-adjusted
returns. Trades revealed in the first week  return  close to  zero (0.1%), whereas the transactions
hidden beyond the 45 days mandated by the STOCK Act appreciate  on average  by  6% before
being revealed to the market  (Appendix 5.2). We find that each extra day of non-disclosure adds
around 3.4 basis points of return (t = 30.3) after controlling for chamber, party, calendar year and
a compliant-filing dummy variable. Notably, the compliance dummy variable is positive, showing
that trades filed within the 45-day window still beat otherwise identical late filings by roughly
1.5%, confirming that both the length of delta and whether meeting (or not) the mandate timeframe
convey separate but meaningful signals  (Appendix 5.14).

In terms of predictability, when we consider every politician´s transactions, the delay on the next
filing is predicted almost perfectly by the delay of the previous one. The slope of the simple
regression is about 0.85, and that single variable captures three-quarters of the variation in the data
(R² of  around  0.76)  (Appendix 5.9 and 5.10). Looking at the individual level, the first-order
autocorrelation in the filing lags averages around 0.52 (median 0.58), with around 92% of
politicians showing a positive correlation, and 74% significant at the 5% threshold. A two-state
Markov  table  (Figure 4.7)  shows that a member who met the 45-day rule on the last trade does so
again 98.5% of the time, while a previous non-compliant remains non-compliant 89% of the time
(χ²  =  27,243,  p  <  0.001).

To better understand the results on the disclosure lag, we consider the macro environment in our
analysis. There are several points that stand out from our results. Firstly, we see that recessions
shorten the lag but do not eliminate them. During the COVID-19 recession, the only classified
recession by the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER) in our study period, the average
delay dropped to 29 days and the compliance rate increased to 92.5%  (Appendix 5.15). Second,
each extra day of delay in disclosure still yields the same approximate extra return (around 3 basis
points) even after we control for GDP growth, unemployment, party and chamber fixed effects
(Appendix 5.18). Thirdly, we find that the per-day return is higher when the conditions are weak.
Specifically, the recession-interaction term adds another 0.096% per day  (Appendix  5.20)  . Party
affiliation changes the level of lags, not the link between lag and return. During a recession, both
parties cut their delays by roughly half, and their average pre-filing returns meet at around 1.3%
(Appendix 5.16).

As the last part of this section, we track each of the return buckets (Appendix 5.2) for a full year
after disclosure. As visualized in Figure 4.8, we find that trades filed according to the STOCK Act
45-day mandate earn a steady 14% to 16% over the next twelve months, whereas the non-
compliant category achieves an average of 9.5%. In other words, the late positions exhaust most
of their alpha while being hidden, while the compliant transactions still have plenty of upside. This

provides very important information, especially for retail investors: politicians who disclose late
do so when most of the “gain” has already been realized, therefore, it is, on average, more
profitable to follow the positions of compliant politicians.

5.  Conclusion

Our analysis shows that when congressional  equity  trades are evaluated at the actual transaction
date, the resulting portfolios deliver  statistically significant  positive abnormal returns relative to
the S&P 500 (our market benchmark).  This result shows that the Congress, during 2012-2024
accumulated substantial  portfolio growths and  that this outperformance potentially is rooted in
transactions based on  sensitive  nonpublic information.

When we reconstruct  the  value weighted  congress  portfolio using the reported disclosure date,  we
find that the outperformance survives disclosure, generating both nominal and risk adjusted returns
far above  the  S&P 500.  This  result, however, should be interpreted with more caution  since our
sample size decreases  significantly due to  the merging procedure across datasets.

The analysis of interim behavior provides further insights. We find that disclosure delays are large
and non-random. The median lag between a transaction and its public filing is about four weeks.
“Buy” trades tend to be disclosed later than sells, and delays vary by party and chamber (for
example, Democrats report trades on average roughly a week later than Republicans, and Senators
lag House members by about a week). Importantly, longer delays correlate with larger interim
gains, and trades reported on time generally outperform otherwise similar late-reported trades,  12
months  after  disclosure.

Our results extend a body of mixed  studies  on congressional trading performance. Early work by
Ziobrowski et al. (2004) documented significant abnormal returns on senators’  equity  purchases,
while Eggers and Hainmueller (2013) and Belmont et al. (2020) later reported little or no out-
performance in the mid-2000s. By examining the entire STOCK-Act  (up to 2024)  era,  and by
separating transaction-date and disclosure-date returns, we show that legislators’ alpha not only
persists but remains economically large even after filings become public:  an effect the earlier
studies  did  not  find.  Building on  the most recent contribution by Balsys and Dalyanci (2024), who
focus on transaction-date gains, our analysis demonstrates that the advantage survives the STOCK
Act  mandate  and  is  concentrated  in  identifiable  sub-groups  (e.g.,  Senate  Democrats  and
economically powerful committees).  Our analysis validates the informational edge  mentioned  in
early studies  and indicates  potential  usefulness for  possible  investment  application  by outside
investors.

In principle, outside investors replicating Congressional portfolios would enjoy meaningful profits
(our disclosure-based portfolio earned roughly 7% annual alpha). In practice, however,  factors
such as transaction costs can  eliminate  the  advantage.  Studying this leads us to the next  and
concluding  section: possible further research.

                                   35
6.  Further Research

Future work could extend this analysis by  fixing some of the  limitations. First,  including  assets
beyond equity  securities. The STOCK Act  requires disclosure of bond, commodity, and  option
trades,  which have not been  studied  in our report. Incorporating bonds, stock  options  or
cryptocurrencies might reveal more interesting information about the performance or  patterns  of
Congress  trading. Second, increasing transaction detail would be valuable.  Our sample  excludes
many transactions, especially when studying performance using disclosure dates. Performing the
analysis with a larger sample of matched transactions would increase the significance of the results
or even contradict them.  Third, by linking trades to firm-specific or legislative events (like
committee hearings), the analysis could be even more informative. This last suggestion would
more closely reveal whether members of Congress are potentially trading on private information.

Turning to the practical side, one can build on our results to see whether a viable investment
strategy can be  constructed,  accounting for real-life conditions (for instance, transaction fees).  For
example,  building  a long-short portfolio  based on  specific  characteristics  (like  going long on  the
own-sector  stocks  bought by  members of the House Committee on Science, Space, and
Technology and selling or shorting those sold) could yield an even higher return than just following
Congress. Future empirical research would need to test whether such a portfolio delivers persistent
risk-adjusted returns, and how it relates to known factors (size, value  and  momentum).

As  introduced  by Balsys & Dalyanci (2024) it could be interesting to study  whether a stable
“Congress Factor” might exist.  It would be important to assess the economic rationale: is any
outperformance compensation for risk  or  a  temporary mispricing due to information asymmetry?
Developing a “Congress factor” would require rigorous tests across different periods and markets
to ensure it is not just a result of data processing. For now, the idea remains an intriguing hypothesis
that deserves careful theoretical and  practical  examination.

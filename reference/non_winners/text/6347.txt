Does Size and Age Matter?

Abstract:

   This paper investigates how family control influences firm performance in Swedish
   publicly listed companies, with a specific focus on how firm size and age moderate
   this relationship. The study contributes to the existing literature by shifting attention
   from family ownership to a novel definition of family control, while analyzing it in a
   unique institutional setting, Sweden, where dual-class shares allow families to retain
   voting control with proportionally lower ownership.  Using a panel data set of 284
  Swedish firms from 2010 to 2023, the study employs pooled OLS regression models
   with year and industry fixed effects to examine both accounting-based (ROA, ROE)
   and market-based (Tobin’s Q, stock return) performance outcomes.  The results show
   that  family  control  has  a  positive  and  significant  effect  on  accounting-based
   performance, consistent with theories of stewardship and agency cost reduction.
   However, this positive effect is diminished in larger and older firms, supporting  the
   notion that organizational  complexity weakens family control’s benefits. In contrast,
   no consistently significant relationship is found between family control and market-
   based performance measures, suggesting that investors may discount the value of
   family control, possibly due to  perceived risks associated with ownership-control
   discrepancies emerging from the family control.

                      1. Introduction

Family firms represent a dominant force in the global economy, contributing significantly to
wealth creation and long-term stability. In 2023 alone, the top 500 family-owned businesses
generated over USD 8 trillion in revenues (EY, 2023). Despite their prevalence and economic
potency, the governance dynamics within these firms, particularly the distinction between
ownership and control, remain underexplored in country-specific institutional contexts. Previous
literature proves that family control and ownership can enhance firm performance through
mechanisms rooted in agency theory and stewardship theory (Anderson and Reeb, 2003).
Families often have a significant portion of their wealth invested in the firm, incentivizing them
to reduce agency costs, avoid excessive risk-taking, and adopt long-term strategies that support
value-maximizing decisions (Demsetz and Lehn, 1985; Anderson and Reeb, 2003). These effects
extend to reduced cost of debt and higher accounting-based performance, particularly when the
family is actively involved in management or the board (Anderson and Reeb, 2003; Lwango,
Coeurderoy and Roche, 2017). Reputational concerns further motivate responsible governance,
as the family name is closely tied to firm success. However, this influence is not without
drawbacks. Families may pursue personal or non-financial objectives, limit R&D due to risk
aversion and dilution fears, or undermine innovation through nepotism or rigid structures
(Burkart, Gromb and Panunzi, 1997; Li and Ryan Jr., 2022). Interestingly, the benefits of family
involvement exhibit an inverted U-shape, where moderate control outperforms both low and
high levels of involvement/control (Reddy and Wellalage, 2023). As firms grow larger and older,
the positive effects of family control tend to decline (Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche, 2017),
reflecting increased complexity, generational shifts, and reduced managerial flexibility (Chirico
and Bau’, 2014; Reddy and Wellalage, 2023). However, and most importantly, Villalonga and
Amit (2006) find that the positive effect of family ownership on performance diminishes as
control in excess of ownership arises. This occurrence, linked to the negative aspects discussed
above regarding risk of pursuing personal benefits and decreased R&D, is particularly prominent
in family holdings where the voting control to ownership ratio is high. This problem stems from
the inherent risk that families exercise a proportionately higher voting control than their
economic incentives represent. According to Faccio and Lang (2002), Sweden has the highest
dual-class ownership in the world, where 66.07% of Swedish firms exhibit dual-class structures,
which promotes disproportionate voting-to-ownership structures. This, in combination with
favorable issuance regulations allowing owners to issue inferior voting rights to foreign
investors, allows high ownership manipulation in Swedish companies. Thus, we aim to observe
family control’s effect on accounting-based and market-based performance in Sweden, where
disproportionate voting control is prevalent, while observing moderating effects of firm size and
age. These patterns motivate our hypotheses:

                   (H1) Family control positively impacts firm performance.
                                     And;
                  (H2) This effect is negatively moderated by firm size and age.

To address these questions, we construct a novel panel dataset covering 284 Swedish publicly
listed companies between 2010 and 2023. We classify firms as family-controlled if the same
family or related entity holds at least 20% of the voting rights continuously throughout the
designated time period, and actively participates in governance through the board or executive
management. This definition accounts for Sweden’s unique legal and market conditions, where
pyramidal ownership and dual-class share systems are widespread, while being based on factual
definitions used in previous literature (Faccio and Lang, 2002; Anderson and Reeb, 2003;
Villalonga and Amit, 2006). Ownership data is collected manually using Modular Finance’s
Holdings platform and cross-referenced with public filings, allowing us to determine active
family involvement. Accounting-based Performance is measured using return on assets and
return on equity (ROA, ROE), and market-based performance is measured by Tobin’s Q and
stock return, consistent with prior literature (Anderson and Reeb, 2003; Villalonga and Amit,
2006; Chung and Pruitt, 2007). All financial data is extracted from Refintiv Eikon.

Our results reveal a positive and statistically significant relationship between family control and
accounting-based firm performance. On average, family-controlled firms exhibit higher ROA
and ROE than their non-family counterparts. These findings align with the agency theory and
stewardship theory perspectives, which posit that family involvement can reduce agency costs,
enhance monitoring, and promote long-term strategic vision, particularly when the family is
actively engaged in governance.

We next find that this positive relationship is not uniform across all firms. Specifically,
interaction models show that the performance benefits of family control diminish with increasing
firm size and age. This suggests that as companies grow and become more complex, the
advantages of family involvement, such as cohesion, alignment, and informal control become
harder to maintain or scale. These results lend support to theories around organizational
complexity and generational drift in large family-controlled firms.

Lastly, our market-based performance analysis yields mixed results. We find no consistent
valuation premium for family-controlled firms in terms of Tobin’s Q or stock returns. A likely
explanation lies in the ownership-control misalignment inherent to Sweden’s dual-class
structures. Investors may perceive high control with limited economic stake as a governance risk,
thus discounting the firm’s value despite strong internal performance. This is consistent with
Villalonga and Amit’s (2006) view that excess control relative to ownership can erode investor
confidence and mute any market-based reflection of operational success.

The empirical strategy faces several challenges, notably endogeneity concerns due to potential
reverse causality, i.e., whether high-performing firms attract or retain family control rather than
the other way around. To mitigate this, we focus on long-standing ownership relationships and
implement fixed effects to control for time- and industry-specific heterogeneity. All models
employ robust firm-clustered standard errors to account for serial correlation and
heteroskedasticity. Additionally, we perform winsorization on key variables to address outliers,
and robustness checks using varying thresholds of control through voting rights (10–50%) to test
for potential non-linear effects such as the inverted U-shape proposed in earlier studies (Reddy
and Wellalage, 2023).

This paper primarily contributes to the literature on family ownership and control. Anderson and
Reeb (2003) find that founding-family ownership enhances both accounting-based and
market-based performance in U.S. firms, especially when family members are active in
management. Furthermore, we differentiate from the study by broadening the scope of family
firms by including non-founding families in the sample. This provides for a broader family firm
perspective, and includes companies where families who have bought into the company at an
early age. Villalonga and Amit (2006), however, highlight the potential downsides of control in
excess of ownership, noting that such discrepancies can dilute valuation effects. Our results
extend these findings to Sweden’s institutional context, where control mechanisms are more
pronounced, and our novel family control definition builds on and assesses the validity of
control’s risk of inferior performance in comparison to ownership. We build on the findings of
Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche (2017), who document that firm size and age negatively
moderate the relationship between family control and accounting-based performance (measured
by ROA) in U.S. SMEs. By extending this analysis to include both accounting-based and
market-based performance in a different institutional and geographical context—larger, publicly
listed Swedish firms—we provide additional evidence on the broader dynamics of family
control. In doing so, we not only reinforce their findings in a new setting but also contribute
novel insights by examining whether the same moderating patterns apply to market-based
performance.

               2. Theoretical Framework

2.1 Positive effect of Family control and ownership

2.1.1 Ownership and Control definition
Family ownership, in its simplest form, is defined as a firm which is minority, majority or wholly
owned by a person/individual or family of relatives (Faccio and Lang, 2002). Subsequently, by
solely basing the definition on this requirement, all companies could practically be defined as
family owned, and hence Faccio and Lang (2002), Anderson and Reeb (2003), and Villalonga

                                       4
                                Does Size and Age matter?

and Amit (2006) argue that in order to ensure sufficient managerial influence (which is the
purpose of examining family ownership), the ownership threshold must exceed 20% of economic
ownership rights. In contrast, heterogeneity in different country legislations serves the ownership
playing field differently; Sweden’s lenient regulations regarding dual-class ownership structures
often provides for disproportionately higher voting-control than economic ownership. Thus, we
distinguish between voting-ownership and economic ownership: (1) family control concerns
owners who are actively engaged in the board or management and own above 20% of
voting-rights, while (2) family ownership concerns owners who are actively engaged in board or
management but own above 20% of the economic rights, which is in line with (Anderson and
Reeb, 2003).

2.1.2 Agency cost resolution and stewardship theory
Demsetz and Lehn (1985) mention in their study the immense concentration of family welfare,
as a percentage of total financial wealth, that families’ investment often constitutes in the
invested firm. According to the authors, this leads to strong reasons to reduce agency conflicts in
order to preserve wealth, and serves as a way to reduce alignment related issues as well as
excessive risk-taking. Furthermore, the lower risk-taking allows mitigation of agency cost of
debt, and family firms are found to have a 32 basis-point lower cost of debt than non-family
firms. In contrast, this relationship exhibits an inverted U-shape as the lowest cost of debt is
shown for firms with <12% family ownership, and for >12%, the cost of debt is reduced by 22
basis-points instead of 32. The relationship is negatively impacted by a descendant CEO, where
the cost of debt actually increases by 20 basis points, and is not affected by the founder as CEO.
Also, other types of blockholding owners, mainly institutional, do not have an effect on cost of
debt, showcasing the strength of long-term strategic focus, ownership alignment and devotion
has on the cost of debt (Anderson, Mansi and Reeb, 2003). Apart from agency theory, scholars
often cite stewardship theory as an explanatory variable for family ownership’s positive effect on
firm performance (Anderson, Mansi and Reeb, 2003; Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche, 2017).
The idea ties into that of agency theory, but it assumes that owners will act in a
value-maximizing manner if they serve as stewards for the company. This observation is
especially prominent in founding-family firms, where the founder holds an active role in the
company, or utilizes activism to influence strategic direction, but it also concerns active
non-founding family owners as their ownership is affected by the same value-maximizing end
goal. It also helps reconcile the idea of agency cost of debt, and explains the reason why family
ownership and family control leadership lowers cost of debt (Anderson, Mansi and Reeb, 2003;
Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche, 2017).

2.1.3 Long-term strategic vision
Anderson and Reeb (2003) note that family owners adopt a long-term holding mindset which
helps them rationalize and prioritize long-term value creation rather than short-term profitability.
This strategic vision fosters prudent investment choices, strong commitment to corporate

reputation, and resilience during economic downturns. The thought of value-maximization is
backed by Casson (1999) and Chami (2001) who argue that family-ownership stakes are assets
intended to be passed down in generations, and thus investments and ownership/control should
promote long-term growth. James (1999) concurs with this conclusion, and notes that this notion
of succession provides families incentives to invest in accordance to the market rule, that is, in
NPV (Net Present Value).

2.1.4 Reputation
Anderson and Reeb (2003) argue that founding family and non-founding family firms are highly
linked to the reputation of the family, and vice versa, which is due to the publicity often
surrounding powerful families and the association of the company to family-name. This creates a
self-fulfilling prophecy as the family’s personal brand is contingent on firm success, which
reinforces incentives to create both economic and social value to uphold family reputation. The
result of reputational benefits are of course also a larger web of business contacts, investors, and
suppliers, and potentially advantageous terms in equity and debt funding (Anderson and Reeb,
2003).

2.2 Negative effect of Family Control and Ownership

2.2.1 Using company to extract private benefit
The first potential threat regarding having a strong family owner, is the risk of excessive
over-pursuance of self-interests rather than the interest of the company, its minority shareholders,
and stakeholders (Fama and Jensen, 1985; Anderson and Reeb, 2003). As discussed before,
financial success of the company and family are often highly intertwined, but if families are
motivated by other definitions of success, such as social or environmental success, they might
destroy firm value due to forgoing NPV positive projects, or investing in NPV-negative projects.
Additionally, newer family generations might not take interest in developing and nurturing
family legacy, and might consequently deprive the company of future value through excessive
dividend outlay. There are numerous instances where families have acted in their self-interest
through for example special dividends instead of investing in the future (Anderson and Reeb,
2003), which adversely affects companies’ profitability and market position. It could also
adversely affect their relationship to employees through lower cohesion and less willingness to
work hard (Burkart, Gromb and Panunzi, 1997). Results hence show that there is a need for
non-family presence on boards and in management teams in order to mitigate the negative family
ownership side effects of family complexity (González-Cruz and Cruz-Ros, 2016).

2.2.2 Lower R&D investments
Li and Ryan Jr. (2022) observe a significantly negative effect between total investment (R&D +
Capex) and family ownership, primarily derived from a negative relationship between family

ownership and R&D investments. More specifically, a 1% decrease in family ownership results
in a 23-basis point increase in R&D. The authors relate this to Anderson, Mansi and Reeb (2003)
as they suggest that excessive risk aversion leads to lower R&D investments, which in turn is
because of the asymmetric wealth concentration that the equity stake in the company represents
for the family’s private placements. In other words, families forgo undertaking investments in
order to limit the risk of their personal capital tied to the valuation and performance of the stock.
Additionally, dilution fears limit cheap financing because families are often reluctant to dilute
their shareholding to obtain capital, which consequently adversely affects R&D and investments.
In other words, family-owned firms reject share issuances and subsequently risk failing to invest
sufficiently in NPV positive projects. Thus, investment is more dependent on internal cash flow
generation, and debt issuance, which in many cases can be expensive for small firms (Li and
Ryan Jr., 2022). Obviously, this problematization of equity issuance is more nuanced, and creates
a conflicting problem: not issuing equity risks depressing R&D investments which will hurt
growth prospects and innovation, while issuing equity dilutes family control and invites other
large blockholders to take control, which risks creating collusion between the large holders
(Reddy and Wellalage, 2023).

However, there are overwhelmingly strong arguments supporting the notion that family
ownership and control affects performance positively, and hence we formulate the following
hypothesis:

       H1: There is a positive relationship between family control and firm performance.

2.3 Active Ownership, Size, and Age

Similarly to many of the aforementioned studies, Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche (2017) finds a
positive relationship between family ownership and accounting-based performance (ROA) while
specifically observing U.S Small and Medium sized Enterprises. Additionally, Lwango,
Coeurderoy and Roche (2017) find that accounting-based performance is strengthened by active
family ownership, i.e., active managerial influence, or active board involvement. The study
references Miller and Breton-Miller (2005) who found a significantly positive effect of family
CEO’s involvement on performance when firms are small and family-owned through exploring
the managerial involvement in day-to-day operations. In general, Lwango, Coeurderoy and
Roche (2017) reference Uhlaner (2006) in saying that as ownership percentage of workers
increase, i.e., there is a higher proportion of owners in employee, or board seats, the loyalty and
sense of belonging increases in the company. This loyalty is said to be able to bring additional
revenue while bypassing extra costs. Furthermore, the idea of involvement links to previously
mentioned arguments surrounding neutralization of agency costs, which theoretically improves
performance.

A rational conclusion is consequently that as the company becomes bigger and older, and
subsequently adds new family managers through generational shifts, the cohesion and
involvement increases performance. Regarding size there is, for obvious reasons, a positive
relationship between size and performance, however, there also exists an inverted U-shape
relationship between active family members in top management positions (Chirico and Bau’,
2014). Thus, there is a growingly positive effect on performance as the number of managers
increase from zero to a few, and a declining positive effect on performance as the amount of
family members in management go from a few to many. Furthermore, there also exists an
inverted U-shape relationship between performance and high and low ownership/control level,
which is positively moderated by a family CEO. This is significant, as it provides background to
the complexity problem by explaining that firms with medium family ownership outperform
those with low ownership, but as ownership stake rises to high (from moderate) the positive
performance decreases again due to complexity problems and the tendency and risk of families
using the company to extract private benefits (Reddy and Wellalage, 2023). This partly helps
reconcile the differences between ownership and control as it is comparatively easier to gain and
retain high control of voting rights by issuing dual-class shares in comparison to holding and
retaining a similarly high ownership stake of total shares. This also brings more nuance to the
previous discussion regarding external capital, dilution, and R&D investments (external
blockholding is fine up to a certain point, whereafter it becomes harmful), and the reasons for
this occurrence, among others, are also rationally explained by arguments made above
surrounding collusion and diversity (Reddy and Wellalage, 2023). Moreover and importantly,
Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche (2017) found that family ownership and control’s positive effect
is negatively moderated by size and age. The theoretical reasoning behind this shift could be
explained by the level of increased complexity with integrating personnel and owners with
contrasting viewpoints, and consequently managing eventual problems associated with this
(Davis and Harveston, 2001), which also explains the inverted U-shape regarding percentage of
top management that family members constitute. Additionally, firm size introduces a
bureaucratic rigidity and decreases entrepreneurial flexibility, which could diminish or dilute
family owners' positive effect on performance (Chirico and Bau’, 2014). To further examine the
point, Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche (2017) cite Birley (2001), Burkart, Gromb and Panunzi
(1997), and Arregle et al. (2007), as they confirm that generational shifts are increasingly
important as the firms become older, highlighting the problems of nepotism. This notion
disfavors employee diversification and the natural sense of hiring based on experience and
expertise, which could hurt the company’s innovative direction. It could also make the company
more rigid and promote ‘business as usual’ rather than innovative investments and change.

The complexity and collusion problems associated with greater firm size and age promote the
following hypothesis:

 H2: The relationship between family control and firm performance is negatively moderated by
                                    the size and age of the firm.

              3. Sample & Data Collection

3.1 Sample & Data Cleaning

The dataset comprises unbalanced panel data on firms listed on Nasdaq Stockholm between 2010
and 2023. The sample includes firms from the Small Cap (market capitalization below SEK 1.5
billion), Mid Cap (SEK 1.5–10 billion), and Large Cap (above SEK 10 billion) segments, in
accordance with Nasdaq Stockholm’s official classifications (Gustafsson, 2018). Firms listed on
Spotlight Stock Market, First North, Nordic Growth Market (NGM), and Nordic MTF are
excluded, as these markets tend to include firms that are smaller, less liquid, or lack sufficient
and reliable data coverage in Refinitiv Eikon, the data source used for extraction of financial
data.

Data cleaning involved the removal of firms with missing values in performance or control
variables. This commonly affected secondary-listed companies (i.e., firms primarily listed on
foreign exchanges), recent IPOs, and very young firms for which return or accounting metrics
could not be reliably calculated. In particular, firms with less than five years of available stock
return data were excluded, as the limited time window is considered insufficient for robust
performance analysis. Additionally, each company with dual-class share structures, represented
by A-class and B-class shares, appeared multiple times as separate entities based on the number
of separate share-listings, in the data from Refinitiv Eikon. In other words, the dataset contained
duplicates, with identical data except for the share price which reflected the specific share class.
To avoid duplication, we retained only one entry per company, favoring the share class with
higher liquidity.

Furthermore, ownership data was manually collected through Modular Finance’s Holdings
ownership platform which provides comprehensive historical data on the ownership structures of
all firms listed on Nasdaq Stockholm. It showcases ownership progression, change, and the
nature of the ownership, hence, if the owner is managerially involved, a passive owner, or
involved with the board. While Holdings updates its database regularly, the data used in this
study is from the years 2010 to 2023. In addition, industry classification for each company in the
sample, used to control for industry differences in the analysis, was also retrieved from Holdings.

Finally, to determine firm age, the founding year of each company was manually collected from
official company websites, as no available database provided consistently reliable data on
incorporation dates. The final sample, after collecting and cleaning the data, consists of 284
firms, of which 76 were classified as family-controlled.

3.2 Ownership Limitation & Clarification

We adopt a novel definition of family control, building on the framework of Faccio and Lang’s
(2002), by identifying family-controlled firms as those in which a family holds more than 20% of
the voting rights and maintains active involvement in the board or executive management.

Based on this definition, the following criteria must be fulfilled for a company to be classified as
family-controlled:

    1. A family company is defined as a holding company owned by an individual, group of
        individuals, trust, or corporation/firm who all constitute a family, or have close ties to a
        family.
    2. The family must control at least 20% of voting rights at the beginning of the time period
       or when the company was listed.
    3. The same family must control above 20% of the voting rights at the end of the time
       period, that is, end of 2023, and the time frame of which there is data must equal or
       exceed five years. Hence, the family must continuously hold 20% of the voting rights
        throughout the allotted time period.
    4. A Family member/representative must hold an insider role, meaning they must be
       actively engaged in the company’s governance, either through management, or through
       the board of directors. This parameter successfully captures and ensures that the family
        has direct managerial influence.

Several listed holding companies in Sweden, such as Investor AB (owned by Wallenberg
family), L E Lundbergföretagen AB (owned by Lundberg family), and Investment AB Latour
(owned by Douglas family), are partly family-owned and hold significant stakes in portfolio
companies where the family remains actively engaged. These holdings follow a pyramidal
structure, where family control is exerted indirectly through ownership layers. Following Faccio
and Lang (2002), we calculate effective family control by multiplying ownership percentages
across tiers. For example, if a family owns 20% of a holding company, which in turn owns 20%
of a portfolio firm, the effective control is 4%. However, since this indirect structure may imply
passive family involvement, we only classify a portfolio company as family-controlled if a
family member sits on its board. This excludes cases where only non-family executives represent
the holding company. The definition also extends to foundations irrevocably linked to a family,
such as the Knut and Alice Wallenberg Foundation, which controls 42.96% of Investor AB’s
voting rights. While Investor is considered family-controlled, its portfolio firm ABB Ltd, in
which Investor holds 14.10% of voting rights, results in just 6.02% indirect control, below our
20% threshold. By contrast, Holmen AB qualifies as family-controlled, as the Lundberg family
indirectly holds 57.36% of its voting rights through L E Lundbergföretagen (92.2% × 62.21%),
meeting both the control and engagement criteria (all percentages calculated based on 31st
December 2023, and taken from Holdings ( no date)).

The study specifically observes and notes companies based on the largest owning entity. In cases
where ownership is distributed among families, such as L E Lundbergföretagen, where one

                   4. Methodology

4.1 Overview

This study employs a quantitative research methodology, drawing from Anderson and Reeb
(2003) and partially by Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche (2017). In the analysis, Ordinary Least
Squares (OLS) regression models are used to investigate the relationship between family control
and firm performance, based on the panel dataset of Swedish listed firms. Firm performance
indicators include both accounting-based and market-based measures. All regression models
include industry and year fixed effects to mitigate potential omitted variable bias arising from
unobserved, time-invariant, industry-specific factors, and time-specific macroeconomic
influences.

4.2 Measures of Firm Performance

Return on assets (ROA) is our main measure of accounting-based performance, and as a
complement we also use return on equity (ROE). ROA is defined based on net income divided
by total assets, both in million SEK, and ROE is defined based on net income divided by
shareholders’ common equity, also in million SEK. We primarily use ROA due to its high
adaptability and accuracy in assessing firm profitability, and its prevalence in previous literature
(Anderson and Reeb, 2003; Villalonga and Amit, 2006; Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche, 2017;
Reddy and Wellalage, 2023).

Regarding market-based performance, our main measure is Tobin’s Q, and our complimentary
measure is annual Stock Return. Due to inherent difficulties in estimating the replacement value
of assets for the definition of Tobin’s Q as used by Anderson and Reeb (2003), we estimate
Tobin’s Q using an approximated definition in line with Chung and Pruitt (2007): market
capitalization plus total interest-bearing debt, both in million SEK, divided by total assets in
million SEK. Stock Return is defined as the percentage change in stock price from one year to
another adjusted for the dividend per share, that is, closing stock price plus dividend per share
divided by opening stock price on a yearly basis. We use stock return to measure and verify stock
performance and Tobin’s Q due to its accurate depiction of market valuation in relation to book
value, reflecting market expectations of the firm’s future performance, and due to its high usage
in previous literature (Anderson and Reeb, 2003; Villalonga and Amit, 2006; Reddy and
Wellalage, 2023).

4.3 Model Specifications

Two different types of OLS model specifications are employed to test our two hypotheses. The
Baseline Model specification is used to estimate the direct effect of family control on firm
performance, while controlling for firm size, firm age as well as leverage. The Baseline Model
specification is formulated as:

        Firm Performanceit = 𝛽0 + 𝛽1(Family Control)i + 𝛽2(Firm Size)it + 𝛽3(Firm Age)it +
                                     𝛽4(Debt/Equity)it + 𝛾t + 𝛿Industry + 𝜖it

Where Firm Performance is the performance measure of interest (ROA, ROE, Tobin’s Q or stock
return). Family Control is a binary indicator equal to 1 if (i) the same family entity (a family or
individual) holds at least 20% of the firm’s voting rights continuously throughout the years the
firm is listed during the observed time period (2010–2023), and (ii) has at least one
representative actively involved in the firm’s management or board. If either condition is not
met, Family Control is equal to 0. Firm Size is defined as the natural logarithm of market
capitalization in million SEK (ln(Market Cap)), and Firm Age is the natural logarithm of firm
age, calculated as the difference between the current year and the founding year. Debt/Equity is
the debt-to-equity ratio in percent, which is used to control for leverage. The remaining three
terms denote Year fixed effects (𝛾t), Industry fixed effects (𝛿Industry) and the error term (𝜖it).

To explore whether the relationship between Family Control and Firm Performance is moderated
by Firm Size and/or Firm Age, we estimate interaction models. These include interaction terms
between Family Control and the mean-centered versions of Firm Size and Firm Age. The
Interaction Model specification is formulated as:

       Firm Performanceit = 𝛽0 + 𝛽1(Family Control)i + 𝛽2(Firm Sizec)it + 𝛽3(Firm Agec)it +
𝛽4(Debt/Equity)it + 𝛽5((Family Control)i * (Firm Sizec)it) + 𝛽6((Family Control)i * (Firm Agec)it) +
                                                 𝛾t + 𝛿Industry + 𝜖it

Where Firm Sizec and Firm Agec denote mean-centered versions of the Firm Size and Firm Age
variables, and the remaining variables are defined just like in the baseline model specification
above.

Mean-centering is applied to the continuous moderator variables, Firm Size and Firm Age, in all
regressions that include interaction terms, in order to improve the interpretability of the
coefficients, particularly the main effect of the Family Control variable. Centering is performed
by subtracting the sample mean from each observation, which re-scales the variable to have a
mean of zero while preserving its variance and distribution. As a result, the estimated coefficient
on Family Control in the interaction models reflects its effect at the average level of Firm Size

                                      14
                                Does Size and Age matter?

and Firm Age, respectively. This allows for a more meaningful interpretation of the main effect
in the presence of interaction terms (Dawson, 2014).

To address the issue of serial correlation and heteroskedasticity in the panel data, all regressions
are estimated with robust standard errors clustered at the firm level. This adjustment accounts for
potential non-independence of error terms within firms over time and ensures that statistical
inference is not biased by within-firm correlation over time. This type of robust standard errors
are widely utilized in financial research particularly in panel data settings (Petersen, 2009).
Additionally, Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) tests were conducted for all main regressions to
check for potential multicollinearity issues. All VIF values, displayed in Table 13 (Appendix),
were well below conventional thresholds (<10), indicating that multicollinearity is not a concern.

4.4 Alternative Control Thresholds

In comparison to the 20% threshold, Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche (2017), uses a controlling
ownership stake exceeding 50%. This is due to their sample observing much smaller unlisted
companies have no more than 50 employees (only a portion of the sample have above 50
employees), meaning there will in many cases be multiple actors with above 20%. Consequently,
there will be an observable problem with analyzing the effect that potentially many different
families have on performance, creating the need to increase the threshold criterion to 50% to
ensure this is the largest UBO (ultimate beneficial owner). Our sample, ranging from one billion
SEK in market cap to around 800-900 billion SEK market cap has a wider disbursement of
ownership where multiple owners who all fulfill the four criteria mentioned above are unlikely.

With that being said, the discussion concerning Reddy and Wellalage (2023)’s arguments
surrounding Inverse U-shapes of different ownership-threshold- levels broadens the analysis and
scope of the entire discussion. Thus, in order to account for this concept, we adopt
complementary control thresholds of 10%, 30%, 40%, and 50%, which is used to run new
regressions on the dependent variables to test for the presence of an inverse U-shape (Reddy and
Wellalage, 2023), and thereby get a more nuanced understanding of the effect of family control.

4.5 Limitations

The first limitation of this study concerns problems surrounding the definition of
family-controlled firms, as it is in many cases difficult to determine the nature or origin of some
ownership actors (Villalonga and Amit, 2006). Using Modular Finance’s Holdings service we
extract the name of the owners of the company, either a person or organisation of any sort, and
thereafter, determine if the owner is a family or not. This classification is done based on official
company filings on Allabolag (Allabolag – Nyckeltal, Resultatredovisning och Befattningar för
svenska företag, no date), but also through Bolagsfakta (Bolagsfakta – Sveriges nya företagssök,
no date) who disclose beneficial ownership on a percentage basis. The latter makes family

                                      15
                                Does Size and Age matter?

classification for the majority of companies unproblematic, however some companies do not
have data regarding the beneficial owner on Bolagsfakta. In these cases, the company is excluded
from our sample as we can not verify the ownership structure. This problem extends to
foundations, whom per definition do not have an UBO, and instead more research must be
conducted to decide the origin of the managing parties. Additionally, as all family-data is
extracted manually it is susceptible to possible human mistakes that could distort the data. While
Refinitiv Eikon is a reputable source of financial data, certain limitations remain. Notably, gaps
in the data implied that a considerable number of observations had to be dropped during the data
cleaning and preparation process in order to ensure consistency and reliability in the analysis.
Furthermore, determining the age of each firm poses challenges due to differing incorporation
definitions. For example, Epiroc AB, the former mining division of Atlas Copco AB who was
subsequently spun off in 2018, was formally recorded as a separate economic entity right after its
spin-off, but has existed since the inception of Atlas (currently known as Atlas Copco AB) in
1873 (Epiroc, no date). When such discrepancies arose, the earlier date was typically selected as
the founding year, as it more accurately reflects the firm's origin.. Problems in defining the
founding year could create biases in the Firm Age variable which distorts regression results, and
consequently affects validity of the results in the interaction model.

Additionally, there is risk that our results suffer from omitted variable bias, where the dependent
variable is affected by other variables rather than the independent variable. Examples of this are
more extensive regressions on insider ownership for family CEOs, which connects to
stewardship theory and is heavily used in previous literature (Anderson and Reeb, 2003; Reddy
and Wellalage, 2023). We partly explore this notion in our study, but not as thoroughly as
previously mentioned studies. Another limitation is the potential influence of other blockholder
on results and management decisions, however, this relationship is not the focal point of this
study and is hence not emphasized.

Another key limitation relates to the underlying cause for increased performance. What we mean
by this is that there is a significant risk of reverse causality; if performance is high, insiders may
sell, or it might attract newer more diverse shareholders. Similarly, family owners might tend to
favor well performing companies, explaining the increased ROA observed in family controlled
companies. Demsetz and Villalonga (2001) argue that ownership structure is not randomly
assigned, nor is it an external variable forced upon firms. Instead, it should be endogenously
determined, meaning it arises from the deliberate decisions of firm insiders and shareholders in
response to internal and external firm-specific factors. For example, Demsetz and Villalonga
(2001) argue that company A with three shareholders each owning 30%, versus Company B with
30 owners each holding 3% of the shares, will not necessarily perform better as the ownership
structure of the two companies are suited and determined based on the observed market
characteristics for that specific company. Once, and that’s if, the endogenous link is established
between ownership and performance, there is no significant relationship pointing to higher

                                      16
                                Does Size and Age matter?

performance from family control. In response, we refer to Anderson and Reeb (2003) when
arguing that this indicates an extreme level of foresight and proficiency in predicting future
performance for the family firms, as the family firm age in our sample is on average 67 years old
(See Table 12 in Appendix). Additionally, it assumes that families are proficient equity investors,
rather than firm-builders or entrepreneurs, and it assumes that families have an informational
edge in relation to the market by knowing that exceptional performance is not already priced into
the current price. However, Anderson and Reeb (2003) are not able to fully neglect the
endogeneity problem because they fail to demonstrate that families are not less likely to exit
firms in times of poor performance, and thus, risk of the reverse causality remains.

                5. Results & Robustness

5.1 Main Results

This section presents the main empirical findings of the study. It begins by analyzing the
relationship between family control and firm performance using accounting-based metrics (ROA
and ROE), followed by market-based metrics (Tobin’s Q and Stock Return). Both baseline and
interaction models are used to assess whether the effects of family control vary systematically
with firm size and age.

The regression results, presented in Tables 3 and 4, offer mixed but insightful evidence regarding
the relationship between Family Control and firm performance. When performance is measured
using accounting-based indicators (ROA and ROE), family-controlled firms demonstrate a
consistently positive and statistically significant performance advantage. These effects are not
only robust across specifications but also economically meaningful in magnitude. Additionally,
interaction models reveal that the positive impact of Family Control diminishes with increasing
Firm Size and Firm Age, suggesting that the benefits of family influence are strongest in smaller
and younger firms. These findings support both H1 and H2.

In contrast, when market-based performance measures are considered (Tobin’s Q and Stock
Return), the results are less consistent. There is no significant association between Family
Control and Tobin’s Q in any specification, and positive but relatively weakly significant for
Stock Return in the interaction model only. Importantly, the interaction between Family Control
and Firm Size is negative and strongly significant in the Stock Return interaction model, which
aligns with the earlier finding that the positive effects of Family Control are more pronounced in
smaller firms. In other words, the results on market-based performance are mixed and therefore
do not provide strong support for either H1 or H2. These results suggest that while Family
Control may enhance internal performance outcomes, the market does not consistently reward
family involvement, particularly in larger and more mature firms.

5.2 Robustness: Accounting-based Performance

This section reports the results from a series of robustness tests to validate the main findings
related to accounting-based firm performance. Specifically, it tests the sensitivity of results to
alternative definitions of Firm Size, varying thresholds for voting rights used to define Family
Control, and the impact of active board or managerial involvement by family members.

The results from these robustness tests consistently support the main findings regarding Family
Control and accounting-based performance. Whether testing alternative definitions of Firm Size
(Table 5), varying the voting rights thresholds used to define Family Control (Table 6, Table 7),
isolating the role of active managerial/board involvement (Table 8), or estimating random effects
models (Table 15), the positive association between Family Control and ROA/ROE remains
robust. Moreover, the moderating effects of Firm Size and Firm Age generally persist across
specifications, indicating further support for H2. Together, these tests provide strong evidence
that the main conclusions are not dependent on specific modeling choices, thereby reinforcing
the robustness and credibility of the empirical results.

Table 5 presents a robustness test in which Firm Size is proxied by ln(Total Assets) instead of
ln(Market Cap), to examine whether the observed effects of Family Control on accounting-based
performance are sensitive to how Firm Size is measured. The table replicates the baseline and
interaction model structure used in Table 3. Results remain qualitatively and statistically
consistent with the original specifications in Table 3. Family Control continues to be positively
and significantly associated with both ROA and ROE in all four models, and the interaction
terms again suggest that this positive effect diminishes with Firm Size (ln(Total Assets)) and
Firm Age (ln(Age)). The interaction between Family Control and Firm Size is negative and
significant for both ROA and ROE (Models 2 and 4), as is the interaction with Firm Age, though
slightly less significant.

These results reinforce the findings from Table 3 and the conclusions drawn in Section 5.1,
namely that Family Control is positively associated with accounting-based performance, and that
this relationship is negatively moderated by Firm Size and Firm Age. Taken together, the results
from Table 3 and Table 5 demonstrate that these conclusions are robust to alternative definitions
of Firm size, whether measured by market capitalization or total assets.

confirms that the conclusion regarding the direct effect of Family Control on accounting-based
performance is robust to moderate adjustments in the Family Control definition. The main
Family Control variable used in earlier models, based on a 20% threshold, thus appears to be a
suitable and empirically supported specification.

Interestingly, the results also offer tentative support for an inverse U-shaped relationship between
family control and performance, a pattern identified in prior literature (Reddy and Wellalage,
2023). Performance effects appear to rise with moderate levels of control (20–30%) but taper off
or lose statistical significance at very low (10%) and high (50%) thresholds. This may reflect a
balance between the alignment benefits of family involvement and the risks of entrenchment or
reduced external oversight at higher ownership levels.

Finally, it is worth noting that the statistical significance of the Family Control coefficient
naturally declines at higher thresholds, likely due to a reduction in the number of firms classified
as family-controlled, leading to less variation and statistical power.

              6. Discussion and Conclusion

6.1 Discussion

The findings of this study on accounting-based performance are consistent with prior literature.
Most notably our findings are in line with Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche (2017), who examine
ROA among non-listed small firms in the US and find a positive relationship between family
ownership and firm performance, where the effect of family ownership is negatively moderated
by firm size and age.

Similarly, Anderson and Reeb (2003), using ROA and Tobin’s Q on U.S. firms, report a positive
impact of family ownership on both accounting- and market-based performance. In contrast to
Anderson and Reeb (2003), this study finds an inconsistent and largely insignificant effect of
family control on market-based performance measures. If anything, part of the results indicate
that there may be a positive effect of family control on stock performance, but that this effect is
significantly and negatively moderated by firm size. In other words, any positive effect of family
control on stock performance appears limited to smaller firms in our sample. Villalonga and
Amit (2006) distinguish between family ownership and family control and argue that control in
excess of ownership, which is a risk associated with family control, can dilute performance
benefits, particularly in market-based metrics such as Tobin’s Q. While our insignificant
market-based performance results do not confirm Villalonga and Amit’s (2006) argument that
control in excess of ownership leads to adverse performance outcomes, it also does not
contradict it. In our sample, family control does not explicitly imply an ownership-control
discrepancy (where owners misuse their disproportionately large vote-holding), but it does
increase the likelihood of such misalignment. Therefore, the lack of significance may reflect
underlying distortions, such as agency risks tied to excess control, which interfere with
market-based valuations.

One interpretation and explanation for the inconsistent market-based results is that the
regressions estimate average effects across all firms, masking heterogeneous effects. Differences
in firm characteristics, other than those controlled for (size, age, leverage, industry), could
neutralize the aggregate effect, leading to overall insignificant results in market-based measures.
Another potential explanation for the diverging results is differences in the sample data, as this
study is based on Swedish listed companies while the majority of previous studies are based on
American companies. This may suggest geographic variation in the effects of family control and
ownership, but such conclusions are beyond the scope of this study and cannot be verified with
the current data. Additionally, this study examines a different time-period than previous studies,
implying that stocks are susceptible to different external price-affecting events that could
complicate comparisons of stock performance and market valuation.

                                      31
                                Does Size and Age matter?

The underlying interpretation of the results suggest that while family control may enhance
internal performance outcomes, market participants do not uniformly value or reward family
involvement. Normally, stronger accounting-based performance would be reflected in higher
market valuations, such as a higher Tobin’s Q or superior stock returns, but the regressions of
this study cannot confirm this. In line with the conclusions discussed regarding Villalonga and
Amit’s (2006) result, this implies that investors may perceive family control itself as a
governance-related risk, potentially discounting such firms due to concerns over misalignment,
entrenchment, or excess control.

A likely explanation lies in the discrepancy between cash-flow rights and voting rights in
dual-class share structures, which are prevalent in Sweden. For example, a family might hold
10% of cash-flow rights but control 20% of the voting rights. This misalignment introduces
agency risks: families with excess control may pursue objectives that are not aligned with
maximizing shareholder value.

As a result, investors may tend to evaluate family firms based on their cash-flow ownership, not
their voting control. In the example above, investors may perceive the family’s influence as
equivalent to 10% ownership, not 20%, and thus assign a lower valuation premium. This aligns
with the inverted U-shape relationship, observed in some previous studies on family ownership
and partially in our results (Table 7, Table 8), where firms with moderate family ownership (e.g.,
20%) tend to outperform those with low (e.g. 10%) or very high (e.g. 50%) ownership levels.
When control exceeds ownership stakes, it could reverse the typical benefits of family ownership
(like reduced agency costs and stronger alignment of interests), and the lower level of perceived
skin-in-the-game (financial alignment) might create trust issues for minority shareholders.

In summary, dual-class share structures and ownership-control discrepancies might explain why
market-based performance measures do not reflect the accounting-based overperformance of
family-controlled firms. Investors may discount the value of excess control, neutralizing any
positive valuation effects from superior accounting-based performance.

6.2 Conclusion

This study set out to investigate the relationship between family control and firm performance in
Swedish publicly listed companies, and how this relationship is moderated by firm size and age.
By distinguishing between accounting-based performance metrics (ROA, ROE) and
market-based performance metrics (Tobin’s Q, Stock Return), this research provides a nuanced
perspective on the role of family control in firm success.

The regression analyses reveal a positive and significant relationship between family control and
accounting-based performance, supporting H1 and consistent with Anderson and Reeb (2003).
However, this positive effect diminishes as firms grow larger and older, confirming H2. Findings

                                      32
                                Does Size and Age matter?

of both hypotheses are consistent with prior research (Lwango, Coeurderoy and Roche, 2017),
suggesting that family involvement is most beneficial in smaller, younger firms, where agency
costs are reduced and stewardship incentives are strongest.

In contrast, market-based performance metrics yield mixed and mostly insignificant results,
indicating that investors do not assign a valuation premium to family-controlled firms, despite
their superior accounting-based performance. This divergence highlights a discrepancy between
internal accounting-based performance and external market perceptions, which is potentially
driven by agency risks associated with ownership-control discrepancies. In Sweden’s prevalent
dual-class share structures, families often wield greater voting control than economic ownership,
which investors may view as a governance risk, thereby discounting firm value. This conclusion
supports (Villalonga and Amit, 2006) claim that control in excess of ownership affects family
control’s influence on performance adversely. However, as accounting-based performance is
positively associated with family control, our results can not reliably verify (Villalonga and
Amit, 2006)’s thesis, instead it only partially helps reconcile a possible explanation for the
differing market-based performance results in comparison to Anderson and Reeb’s (2003).

These findings underscore the complex dynamics of family control in public markets,
particularly within Sweden’s unique regulatory environment as it highlights the difference of
family ownership and family control. While family control can enhance operational efficiency
and financial outcomes, excessive control relative to ownership may undermine investor
confidence, preventing these benefits from translating into higher market valuations.

Future research should further explore the heterogeneity within family firms, such as the
distinction between founding families and non-founding families, and possible effects of the
dual-class shares on these, or the role of active versus passive family involvement in the context
of dual-class share ownership. Moreover, examining family control’s interaction with other
blockholders or institutional investors could provide deeper insights into how ownership
structures shape firm performance. Finally, extending this research to other countries that possess
similar legislative structures, or add a comparative component that observes the heterogeneity of
family control and family ownership between different countries could test the robustness of
these findings across different legal and economic contexts, and bring more light to the effect
that legal regimes and jurisdictions have on firm’s ability to create and nurture performance and
ownership simultaneously.

In conclusion, while family control remains a potent force in shaping firm performance, its
effects are contingent on firm characteristics and market perceptions. Striking the right balance
between control and ownership appears critical, not only for maximizing firm outcomes but also
for maintaining investor trust in public markets.

8.3 Variable Definitions

Below follows a description of the variables used in the regression analysis, as well as

family or individual) holds at least 20% of the firm’s voting rights continuously throughout the
years the firm is listed during the observed time period (2010–2023), and (ii) has at least one
representative actively involved in the firm’s management or board. If either condition is not
met, Family Control is equal to 0. For alternative definitions (used in Table 7 and Table 8),
voting rights thresholds are set at 10%, 30%, 40%, and 50%.
Source: Holdings

excluding the requirement that the family entity must have at least one representative in the
board or management.

(Family Ownership = 1) with active managerial or board involvement, and family-owned firms
without such active involvement.
Source: Holdings (underlying data used for classification)

Source: Refintiv Eikon

Source: Refinitiv Eikon

Source: Refinitiv Eikon

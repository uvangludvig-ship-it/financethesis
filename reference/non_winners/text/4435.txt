How Does the Use of Proceeds Affect the Green Bond Premium?

Abstract:

   Human-caused climate change has created a need for increasing financial flows to
   sustainable solutions. Green bonds are a novel asset type where the use of proceeds
   are earmarked for projects with environmental benefits. In this paper we examine
   whether the difference in yield between green bonds and conventional bonds, known
   as the green premium, varies depending on the type of project being funded. Using a
   matching method to match green bonds with equivalent synthetical conventional
   bonds we find evidence that there is a significant difference in the green premium
   depending on the use of proceeds after controlling for residual liquidity. We show
   that energy projects have the most negative green premium and that projects in
   transportation have a positive premium, i.e. are priced lower than a corresponding
   conventional bond. We argue that this highlights the need for a widely adopted
   taxonomy for green bonds to improve the transparency of the determinants of the
   green premium to issuers and investors alike.

Keywords:

  Green Bonds, Green Premium, Green Projects, Matching Method, Sustainable
  Finance

1. Introduction

Human-caused climate change is the main cause of the observed global warming in the
last 150 years (Masson-Delmotte et al., 2018). As outlined in this landmark report by
the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (abbreviated IPCC), global warming of
1.5 degrees or more will have far reaching impacts on both natural and human systems.
The IPCC recognize this, stating that “The lower the emissions in 2030, the lower the
challenge in limiting global warming to 1.5°C after 2030 with no or limited overshoot”
(ibid.). According to a report by the New Climate Economy (2015), the transition to
low-carbon will require extra investments of around USD 270 billion annually until
2030. The European Investment Bank instead request a more aggressive level of
additional investments of up to €380 billion a year (USD 427 billion at the time of
writing) only in the EU (Kolev et al., 2012).

             According to the IPCC, novel financial innovations will play an important
part in the transition to a low-carbon infrastructure (Masson-Delmotte et al., 2018). One
such innovation are green bonds, a type of bond whose proceeds are earmarked for
projects with environmental benefits. Examples of projects eligible for investment
include those in energy, transportation, water, buildings, land use and waste
management. The first green bond was issued in 2007 by the European Investment
Bank (European Commission, 2016). Since then, green bonds have become a
mainstream vehicle for investment amongst many of the largest investors in both the
private and the public sector. In 2018, over USD 183 billion were raised using labelled
green bonds, taking the cumulative issuance up to USD 563 billion at the start of 2019
(SEB, 2019). Over the last five years, the annual issuance volume has grown by more
than 37% per year (ibid.).

              To date, no singular definition on green bonds exists. As such, multiple
organizations have developed frameworks and criteria to be used in the classification of
green bonds. The most prominent of these are the Climate Bonds Standard by the
Climate Bonds Initiative (CBI) and Green Bond Principles (GBP) by the International
Capital Market Association (IMCA).

             The GBP are voluntary process guidelines on issuing green bonds and are
published by ICMA (2018). The GBP was established by ICMA to promote integrity in
the green bond market. These guidelines put pressure on transparency, disclosure and
reporting and are commonly followed by green bond issuers (Lyubov, 2019). The
Climate Bonds Standard by the CBI fully integrates the GBP and provide the
requirements to be labeled as green by the CBI to consequently appear in their database
(Climate Bonds Initiative, 2015). The labeling done by the CBI is commonly used by
other institutions to define green bonds, among these the database Thomson Reuters
Eikon (Refinitiv, personal communication, May 09, 2019).

                                   4
             As green bonds have become a more widely used mean of financing and
investment, an increasing amount of discussion around the existence of a “green
premium” has occurred, eg. (Karpf and Mandel, 2016), (Ehlers and Packer, 2017),
(Zerbib, 2018) and (Flammer, 2018). A green premium occurs when a green bond is
priced higher, i.e. has a lower yield, than an equivalent conventional bond. When the
green premium is found to be for example -1.5 basis points (bps), this is equivalent to
the yield of the green bond being 1.5 bps lower than it would be for a conventional
bond. A lower yield is positive for the issuer of the green bond as it decreases the
issuer’s cost of debt.

              The existence of a premium is of interest to both issuers and investors. To
investors, a green premium is negative as it would lower the yield of the investor’s
investment. On the other hand, a green premium is positive for the issuer as it lowers
the cost of debt. A green premium on the secondary market could translate into a
negative premium occurring on the primary market as well, as green bond yields on the
secondary market can be interpreted as the yield at which investors will be willing to
buy (Kapraun and Scheins, 2019). This is a line of thought that has been pursued by the
CBI, who in their latest green bond pricing report show that the yield spreads of green
bonds tend to tighten more than comparable conventional bonds once the bonds trade on
the secondary market (Climate Bonds Initiative, 2018a). This may lead to the
emergence of a consistent green premium on the primary market, which has been found
in a recent study by Kapraun and Scheins (2019). Recognizing this phenomenon, this
study will focus on the occurrence of a green premium on the secondary market.

             The asset manager NN Investment Partners (2018) published a report in
2018 where they identify a yield difference stemming from the use of proceeds, i.e.
which type of project the bond will finance. In their report they show that projects in
renewable energy has the largest negative green premium, while projects that finance
green buildings exhibit a discount compared to the issuer’s conventional yield curve.
Hence, their report shows that the type of project being financed may have a significant
impact on the bond’s yield.

             However, the statistical methods employed are not clearly explained and
the robustness of the results is not easily verifiable. While the report thus raises
important questions there is also a need for further research into this area of the green
premium.

                                   5
             The existence of a green premium that may vary based on the project
being financed by the bond in question is of interest to both issuers and investors.
Issuers aiming to finance projects with a larger negative premium may be more
incentivized to issue a green bond and lower their cost of capital. On the other hand,
investors may wish to focus their investments on bonds that are earmarked for certain
types of green projects. This is a hypothesis that has not yet been studied in academic
research. This paper will therefore focus on whether or not a green premium can be
identified in the secondary market for green bonds, and whether this premium is
affected by the project being financed. As such, the research question is:

Does the green premium vary depending on the type of project being financed by the
green bond?

             We use a model free matching method to compare the green bonds with an
equivalent synthetic bond which allows us to isolate a potential green premium. Using a
fixed effect panel regression, we find the green premium as the fixed effect in the
regression of the yield differential on a liquidity proxy. To examine the effect of the use
of proceeds, we then find the determinants of the green premium using a cross sectional
regression.

             In our sample of 166 green bonds issued between the 1st of January 2014
and the 31st of December 2018, we first identify the existence of an average green
premium of -1.4 bps. Focusing on the determinants of the green premium and the use of
proceeds in particular, we find that the use of proceeds has a significant effect on the
green premium for one out of five included project types at a significance level of 0.05,
and three out of five at a significance level of 0.1. Of the five project types, energy is
found to have the largest effect on the green premium. While not all project types reach
statistical significance, the majority of them are shown to have a negative green
premium.

2. Literature Review

Multiple studies on green bonds and the green bond premium have been published in
the last three years, including but not limited to those produced by Reboredo, Quintela,
and Otero (2017), Tang and Zhang (2018), Flammer (2018), Gianfrate and Peri (2019),
and Bachelet, Becchetti, and Manfredonia (2019). However, only a few have performed
research regarding the components of the green premium. In a 2018 paper, Zerbib
(2018) explores how different factors may affect the size of the green premium. Zerbib
uses a matching method where he compares green bonds with close to identical
synthetic conventional bonds. Studying a sample consisting of 110 bonds issued
between July 2013 and December 2017 he finds a green premium of -2 bps. Zerbib
shows that the occurrence and size of a green bond premium largely depends on the
issuer’s rating and issuer type but also explores how other factors including currencies,
maturities and issue amounts may affects the green premium. However, Zerbib does not
include the use of proceeds as an explanatory variable.

             To the best of our knowledge, the only study examining the potential
effect of green project types is a report from NN Investment Partners (2018). NN
Investment Partners gather data for 133 green bonds ranging from December 2014 to
November 2017. The report compares the green bonds’ monthly average yield with the
monthly average yield of equivalent conventional bonds. The conventional and green
bonds are from the same issuer and with a similar seniority. Based on this, the yield
differences for each project are identified. The project type alternative energy on
average leads to the most negative yield difference of approximately -1.3 bps and
transportation of around -1 bps. Green buildings are shown to have a slightly positive
yield difference. The yield difference is presented as the yield of the green bond minus
the conventional bonds’ average yield. We investigate this further with more rigorous
methods and find the project’s effect on the green bond premium.

              Literature regarding the effect of the green label on the cost of debt is of
interest since the underlying components, such as project type, could have different
impacts on the issuer’s cost of debt. In a 2019 paper, Gianfrate and Peri (2019) claim to
have conducted the first study on this topic. Gianfrate and Peri study 121 European
green bonds issued between 2013 and 2017 using a propensity score matching, different
to the matching method used in this paper. Their findings show that issuers of green
bonds on average paid a lower interest of 18 bps to green bond investors in comparison
to what was paid to investors of conventional bonds. According to Gianfrate and Peri,
the issuance of a green bond is beneficial even when the costs associated with the
issuance of a green bond is taken into account. Gianfrate and Peri claim that green
bonds are beneficial not only to the issuing firm, but also to society. This is confirmed
by Flammer (2018) and points to the environmental benefits of the green projects.
Flammer studies the potential of green bonds by empirically examining all the corporate

                                   7
green bonds issued by public and private companies worldwide since the beginning of
2013 to the end of 2017. Flammer argues that “green bonds have real impact, and hence
are not merely a tool of greenwashing” (p.6) by showing that issuers of green bonds
enjoy decreases in CO2 emissions when undertaking green projects, as well as higher
environmental performance and an increase in the filing of green patents. Moreover, in
addition to the benefits found by Gianfrate and Peri (2019) Flammer’s study shows that
the announcement of a green bond issuance causes a positive response on the stock
market, an increase in the issuing company’s long-term value and a higher return on
assets (Flammer, 2018).

            Researchers do not unanimously agree on an overall negative green
premium as found by Zerbib (2018), Gianfrate and Peri (2019) and Bachelet, Becchetti,
and Manfredonia (2019). For example, Tang and Zhang (2018) do not find a significant
green bond premium using a within-firm and between-firm comparison of 41 companies
with data from 2007 to 2017 in 28 countries. Tang and Zhang therefore argue that
“positive stock returns around green bond announcements are not driven by the lower
cost of debt”(p.1). Thus, it is not clear if a green premium exists. Analogies to the effect
of Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) can however be made, as many researchers
today agree on a positive effect of CSR (von Arx and Ziegler, 2008), (El Ghoul et al.,
2010). Among these effects is a lower cost of debt (Albuquerque, Koskinen and Zhang,
2018). Nonetheless, the green premium for different project types might vary even if the
overall green premium is found to be nonexistent.

3. Method

3.1. Methodology

3.1.1. Methodology overview

The major objective of this study is to investigate whether there are any differences in
the green premium depending on the underlying type of green project being funded. To
do this, we employ a similar matching method to the one used by Zerbib (2018). We
first find whether a green premium exists for the entire dataset, as well as its magnitude.
Secondly, we group the green bonds based on the different types of projects being
funded. Lastly, using a cross-sectional regression we find the effect of the project type
on the green premium. The starting point for our data collection is every green bond
issued in the period between the 1st of January 2014 and the 30th of December 2018.
We choose this period to limit the disparity between the maturity in the matched pairs.

3.1.2. Measuring the green premium

We define the green premium as the bond yield spread between conventional and green
bonds less effects that may stem from other sources than the green nature of the bond,
with liquidity effects being the main residual that may affect the yield differential. We
use a matching method to minimize uncertainty and maximize accuracy. By using this
model-free approach we are able to, in theory, eliminate effects caused by variables
other than the green premium and the liquidity. Another option would be to use a
regression method. However, this would require that we include all variables that have
significant explanatory power with respect to the bond yield spread. Covering all
explaining factors is extensive, difficult and comes with the risk of model
misspecification.

3.1.3. The matching method

The matching method enables us to compare green bonds with conventional bonds
without having to consider differences in issuer, currencies, ratings, seniority, collateral,
coupon type and other affecting factors. We can thus isolate any yield differential as
being attributable only to the green label and a difference in liquidity. This is done by
comparing a green bond with an identical conventional bond. However, such pairs
rarely, if ever, exist. We therefore create an identical synthetic conventional bond
consisting of two conventional bonds, both similar to the green bond. The theoretical
result is that the only difference that may affect the yield spread between the synthetic
conventional bond, consisting of two bonds, and the green bond is the liquidity
differential and the green nature of the bond. While the green premium could vary over
time as a result of a changing market risk premium, Zerbib (2018) concludes that this is

                                   9
not the case by finding that the time effect of the green premium is not explained by
market risk after comparing the green bonds' fixed effects with the daily return of three
market indices. A similar methodology to the one used in this paper has successfully
been applied in related areas of research such as ethical funds by Mallin, Saadouni and
Briston (1995) and Kreander et al. (2005).

3.2. Data

3.2.1. Data retrieval

We gather all data using Thomson Reuters Eikon, which label green bonds as those in
line with the CBI’s Climate Bonds Standards (Refinitiv, personal communication, May
09, 2019). We first collect all 1150 bonds that have been labeled as green in Eikon in
the period between the 1st of January 2014 and the 30th of December 2018. This dataset
consists of a range of different bonds and is not limited to a specific type. For example,
it includes municipal, corporate and supranational bonds of different ratings, currencies,
maturities, coupon types and seniorities.

3.2.2. Matching requirements

For each green bond in this dataset we must find two conventional bonds with which to
build the synthetic conventional bond. The two bonds should be as close to a perfect
match to the green bond as possible. Some characteristics have to be identical, including
the issuer, rating, currency, seniority, collateral and coupon type. Other aspects have to
be similar but not identical, including the maturity, issue date and issue amount. When
finding appropriate matches we prevent the maturities from deviating too much from
each other by limiting the conventional bonds’ maturities to be within two years of the
corresponding green bond’s maturity. In the same sense, we constrict the issue dates of
the conventional bonds to be no further than six years before or after the issue date of
the green bond. Lastly, we select only those conventional bonds that have an issue
amount that is either larger than a quarter of the green bond’s or lower than four times
the issue amount of the green bond. We consider the issue amount and issue date as they
affect the bond’s liquidity, as shown by Bao et al. (2011) and Houweling, Mentink and
Vorst (2005), which in turn is known to affect the yield of the bond (de Jong and
Driessen, 2012), (Favero et al., 2009). Similarly, the differences in maturity must be
limited based on the fact that they affect the yield of the bonds (Lenz, 2010). The yields
of the pairs used to create the synthetical bond must not be significantly influenced by
characteristics that differ from the green bond’s, such as maturity and liquidity. We set
these restrictions to further isolate the green nature of the bond and to better control for
liquidity.

                                  10
3.2.3. Matching

For each green bond, we find all conventional bonds that fulfill the requirements
presented in the previous paragraph. If for any green bond there does not exist two
conventional bonds that meet the requirements the bond is deleted from the dataset.
This generates a list of 2386 bonds which make up the pool from which we construct
the synthetic conventional bond. Each green bond is matched with the two closest
matching conventional bonds based on issue date, issue amount and maturity. The
conventional bonds are chosen so that one has a maturity above the green bond’s and
one below it, enabling us to use interpolation to find the yield of the synthetic bond. We
are left with 166 triplets, i.e. 498 individual bonds. For each individual bond, we gather
data on the closing ask yield and bid yield for each date ranging from the issue date, at
earliest the 1st of January 2014, to the 31st of December 2018.

to transportation, six to water, three to land use, one to waste management and eleven

3.2.5. Limiting rating categories

The data of the ratings retrieved from Thomson Reuters Eikon cannot be processed in
its initial state. The ratings are inconsistent as all issuers have not been rated by the
same credit rating agency and thus have been given differently titled credit rating tiers.
Furthermore, our sample of 166 triplets is relatively small compared to the number of
different rating categories present in the sample. We use Standard & Poor's rating tiers
as the standard and convert all alternative ratings to their Standard & Poor’s equivalent.
We limit the number of available ratings to four, including AAA, AA, A, BBB and
include the category not rated for bonds that do not have a credit rating. We round off
the ratings by removing all plus and minus signs, i.e. AA+ becomes AA. All short-term
ratings are converted into the closest equivalent long-term rating. When required the
ratings are rounded down.

              Thus, the test displays that our dataset shows evidence of panel-wise
effect, meaning the fixed effects are non-zero. Hence, a pooled OLS is not appropriate
given our data. It may however be the case that the fixed effects are non-zero but that
they are also correlated with the time-varying explanatory variable in regression (A), the
bid-ask spread. To test this, we also perform a Hausman test which indicates that the
fixed effect estimator is more efficient and consistent than the random effect estimator.

              We then perform robustness tests. First, we test for groupwise
heteroscedasticity in the residuals using the modified Wald test as outlined in Greene
(2002). This is achieved using -xttest3- in Stata as provided in Baum (2001) which
allows for unbalanced panel data, in line with the dataset used in this paper. The test
indicates the presence of heteroscedasticity as shown in table 1.

              We also test for autocorrelation, meaning error terms that are correlated
across time periods. Following Wooldridge (2010) we perform a Wooldridge test for
autocorrelation in our panel data, using the command provided by Drukker (2003). The
Wooldridge test tests the null hypothesis of no autocorrelation, which we reject with
high significance as shown in table 1. As the robustness tests indicate the presence of
both heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation in our panel data we invoke clustered robust
standard errors, in the form of the Huber-White or sandwich estimators of standard
errors following the work by Arellano (1987).

              As the Hausman test outlined above does not support non-default standard
errors we also perform a version of a test of overidentifying restrictions by using the
module put forth by Schaffer and Stillman (2016). The test supports the results of the
Hausman test and substantiates the indication that a within regression is the best
specification for our data. Lastly, we perform a manual adaptation of the Ramsey
RESET test for model misspecification as described by Ramsey (1969). As
demonstrated in table 1 there is no statistically significant evidence of model
misspecification for regression (A). By performing the RESET test manually, we are
able to control the number of powers used. In addition, it allows us to perform the test
using a correct covariance matrix which makes the test more robust. We perform the
test by retrieving the powers of the fitted values of regression (A). We then include
these additional variables as explanatory variables in regression (A) and test the
significance of the regression including the powers and the liquidity measure.

              Thus regression (A) is a fixed-effect panel regression with Huber-White
robust standard errors. The test provides the fixed effect for the whole period for each
green bond. As such, regression (A) produces the same number of estimated fixed
effects, 𝑔𝑝𝑖, as the number of green bonds analyzed.

             After retrieving the green premium for each green bond, we divide the
bonds into subsamples based on the categories project type, rating and currency. To
verify the efficiency of the estimator for the green premium we calculate the mean of

                                  15
the green premium for each sample and test if it significantly differs from zero. We
determine the most appropriate procedure to do so through a Shapiro-Francia test with
the null hypothesis that the subsample being tested comes from a normally distributed
population, following Shapiro and Francia (1972). We only include subsamples
containing more than 10 bonds, the smallest sample size allowed in the Shapiro-Francia
test. We reject the hypothesis of normality for 9 out of the 11 constructed subsamples.
The test indicates that we cannot reject the null hypothesis for AAA rated bonds or
SEK-denominated bonds.

              According to the results of the Shapiro-Francia tests we employ a
variation of the two-sample t-test with unequal variances to test if the subsample mean
is significantly different from zero. While the t-test assumes normality for the samples
being tested, the t-test procedure used in STATA is robust to deviations from the
assumption of normally distributed samples. We find that the subsamples transportation,
SEK, A, and AA are significantly different from zero at a significance level of 0.1.

5. Discussion

5.1. Comparing the results to literature

The finding that energy is the project type with the largest impact on the green premium
is consistent with the only literature on the subject — the previously referenced report
by NN Investment Partners (2018), which states that energy projects generate the largest
yield difference. The green premiums found in this paper are more negative than the
yield differences shown in the report. Additionally, this paper finds a positive green
premium for the project type transportation while the report by NN Investment Partners
finds a negative yield difference for transportation. While NN Investment Partners find
a yield difference of -1.3 bps for alternative energy projects, this paper identifies a green
premium of -3.4 bps for the project type energy. The report examines differences in
yield, rather than the green premium itself. Thus, the report should be interpreted as
further evidence that the project types affect the yield, and consequently, the green
premium. However, it should not be interpreted as a reflection of the magnitude of the
green premium.

              Zerbib (2018) does not include a variable for project type but finds that the
issuer’s sector and rating significantly affect the green bond premium. We show that
three out of five green project types significantly explain the green premium while
Zerbib shows statistical significance for two out of four rating categories. Thus, this
paper complements the findings in Zerbib’s study by identifying further statistically
significant explanatory variables.

             The difference in cost of debt between conventional and green bonds
found by Gianfrate and Peri (2019) could be applied to our project types. As this
difference in financial costs consists of the green bond premium, and the premium itself
differs between project types, this should indicate a difference in cost of debt between
different project types.

                                  23
5.2. Implications for issuers, investors and policymakers

Under the assumption that issuers of bonds seek to minimize their cost of debt, these
issuers may base their choice of financing based on the yield differentials observed in
the secondary market. As such, the green premium observed in the secondary market
could influence the outflow of bonds in the future. According to our results, issuers
benefit from a lower cost of debt when issuing green bonds used to finance projects in
energy and buildings. While it is reasonable to assume that issuers will seek to minimize
the cost of debt, the magnitude of the difference in green premium must also be taken
into account. While the difference of around 9 basis points for projects in energy as
compared to projects in transportation will be of interest to issuers, the difference in cost
of borrowing may not be enough to have an effect on issuer preferences.

             On the other hand, the implications for investors are the opposite. Under
the assumption that investors seek to maximize the yield of their investment, they will
benefit from investing in the type of project which is shown to have the lowest green
premium, i.e. transportation. Again, the scale of the difference in the yield between
green bonds used to finance different project types is of importance. As some investors
take other criteria than solely yield into account when making an investment decision, it
is likely that the difference in yield will have to be of a certain magnitude for it to have
an effect on their decision. This difference would, as shown, arise due to imbalances in
supply and demand caused by a change in the green premium based on the use of
proceeds. That said, it is difficult to determine the difference required for there to be
structural changes in the secondary market for green bonds.

              This raises an interesting question regarding the use of proceeds. While it
makes intuitive sense that investors seek to maximize their yield, there may be other
criteria taken into account when the decision on which project to invest in is made.
Should investors have a preference for certain types of projects due to their investment
mandate, a small difference in the green premium may not be enough to affect their
investment decisions to a larger degree. Such is often the case for institutional investors
such as pension funds whose strategic investment plans include a mandatory allocation
of capital to certain types of assets, for example the Swedish pension fund AP4 (Fjärde
AP-fonden, 2018).

              Regardless of whether investors have a preference for financing particular
kinds of projects or maximizing the return on their investment, this paper indicates that
the bonds' use of proceeds will be of interest to investors in both the primary and
secondary market. Improving the transparency surrounding the use of proceeds in green
bonds would allow investors to make investment decisions more in line with any such
preferences. As a consequence of the lack of a widely adopted taxonomy in the green
bond market there are as of yet no binding regulations which require that the use of
proceeds are reported in detail. While the GBP include guidelines around reporting the

                                  24
use of proceeds both before and after the issue of a green bond, these are only
recommendations and are not mandatory. In practice most issuers are not reporting the
use of proceeds on a detailed project-by-project basis but rather in larger categories of
the different project types being financed.

          Greater transparency would be beneficial for issuers as well, since access
to more complete information would make the market for green bonds more efficient
and ensure that the bonds are fairly priced (Thuesen, 2004). More transparency leading
to fair prices would also more accurately reflect the supply and demand in the market,
which is important for attracting both investor and issuer appetite for green bonds. The
publication by Nationalbanken argues that pre-trade information is of high importance
to ensure that investors can trade assets at fair prices (Thuesen, 2004). Requiring more
extensive reporting of the use of proceeds in the green bond market may thus improve
the pricing of green bonds to the benefit of all market participants.

             This would have implications for policymakers and regulators in
sustainable finance. In March 2018, the European Commission published an action plan
for financing sustainable growth in the EU to meet the goals set out in the Paris
Agreement (European Commission, 2018). In addition to establishing a goal of boosting
the issuance of green bonds, the report sets out that the EU will establish a unified
classification system including EU standards for sustainable financial assets as a whole,
and green bonds in particular. Stipulating more detailed reporting of the use of
proceeds, including project types, would thus be an important aspect to include in future
regulations of the green bond market such as the one proposed by the European
Commission.

                                  25
5.3. Suggestions for future research

As the demand for green bonds increases, so does the need for further research into
green bonds to improve the transparency and access to information for investors, issuers
                                            2
and policymakers alike. Given the low R in regression (B), it is evident that further
examinations of the determinants of the green premium is needed. As the yearly
issuance of green bonds is still rapidly increasing, future studies examining similar
variables such as the ones included in this paper would be beneficial to conduct in the
coming years as the market for green bonds grows larger and access to data on green
bonds with it. Additionally, studies focusing on identifying other variables with
explanatory power to further uncover the determinants of the green premium would be a
positive contribution to the research on green bonds.

              As touched upon in section 5.2, it would also be interesting to examine
investor motives and preferences when investing in green bonds. It could for example
be hypothesized that the environmental benefits found by Flammer (2018) differ
between project types and that this is the underlying cause behind the observed
difference in the green premium as investors seek to maximize the impact of their
investments. Specifically, future research could conduct survey analysis on whether
investors have a preference for investing in certain project types, rather than certain
sustainable financial products as a whole. This would shed light on whether the scale of
the difference in the green premium between different project types is enough to have a
noticeable effect on the market for green bonds.

              Lastly, as there exists only a limited amount of research on the link
between the use of proceeds and yield premiums, applying similar methods in other
areas of sustainable finance such as social impact bonds and ethical bonds would further
reveal whether the use of proceeds is a determinant of bond yields.

6. Limitations

6.1. Data

6.1.1. Project type

The categorization of project types used in this paper does not perfectly mirror the
actual use of proceeds for each bond. Often, the green bonds’ proceeds are dedicated to
multiple project types. Our categorization only reflects the majority project type, i.e. the
one that the majority of proceeds are earmarked for. As we categorize a bond as a
certain project type when more than 60 % of the proceeds are dedicated to a particular
project, the remaining share of proceeds could still affect the green premium. For
example, if investors invest in a bond because a certain portion of the issue amount of
that bond is used to finance a land use project but we define the green bond’s project as
water, the defined bond project does not completely represent the underlying bond’s use
of proceeds. Thus, the difference in yield between water and other projects on the
market does not in this case represent the actual demand for that project type.

             Another limitation comes from the subsample sizes of the different project
types. Waste management, land use and water consist of less than 10 bond pairs and are
therefore not included in regression (B). This limits the scope of the research conducted
in this paper.

6.1.2. Rating

Our sample consists of 166 triplets or 498 individual bonds. Based on their
characteristics, they were divided into subsamples. The raw data from Thomson Reuters
Eikon includes 35 different rating categories. The large number of categories is the
outcome of differently titled and defined rating tiers by various credit rating agencies as
well as the different credit ratings between bonds. To be able to construct statistically
usable subsamples, the number of rating categories were limited to four broader
categories and the ratings rounded off. Thus, the rating subsamples include more rating
tiers than what their subsample title indicates. Because of this, it may be that the credit
rating tiers AAA, AA, A and BBB, have an actual effect on the green bond premium
that is different from our findings.

6.1.3. Currency effects

There may be a bias caused by bonds issued in a currency where overall yields are
comparably large. Even if the percentage difference between the green bonds and the
conventional should be about the same for all currencies, the absolute values between
the green and conventional bonds may be higher for bonds denoted in volatile
currencies. This would not impact the interpretability of the beta coefficients in

                                  27
regression (B) but may make the mean green premium for the entire sample not entirely
representative of the larger population of green bonds.

6.2. Model

A limitation in the cross-sectional regression, denoted regression (B), is the risk of
model misspecification due to omitted variable bias. Specifically, it may be the case that
the project type-variable included in the model acts as a proxy for a missing variable
which is the actual underlying cause of the difference in the green premium. For
example, Zerbib (2018) does not include project type as an explanatory variable but
instead finds that the sector type of the issuer significantly affects the green premium.
When contrasted with the result found by Zerbib, it may be that the project type-
variable included in regression (B) is not the cause of the difference in the green
premium, but that such an effect should instead be attributed to the sector type of the
issuer. This would be the case if there exists a correlation between project type and
issuer sector. However, it should be noted that it may also be the case that the project
type is the causal factor, not the sector type.

              There is also a risk of availability bias in the project types included as
category variables in regression (B). As subsamples with fewer than 4 observations
were excluded, there is a risk that certain project types are more likely to be excluded
from the regression due to an imbalance in data availability between green bonds
financing different types of projects. This would mean that the results outlined above
are not representative of the wider green bond universe as a whole.

              One shortcoming of the Breusch-Pagan test is that it only tests for linear
forms of heteroscedasticity, meaning that as 𝑔𝑝𝑖 increases, so does the variances of the
errors. Accordingly, the test is not suitable for non-linear forms of heteroscedasticity
nor when the errors are not normally distributed. To supplement the Breusch-Pagan test
one could perform the White general test for heteroscedasticity (White, 1980).
However, this test does not work well for smaller datasets such as the one used in this
paper. As such, there is a risk that the result of the Breusch-Pagan test does not
completely reflect the nature of our data. Nevertheless, as we invoke clustered robust
standard errors this would not affect the results of the two regressions.

              Lastly, the estimation of the synthetic bond with which we construct the
matched triplets may suffer from inconsistencies in the interpolated yield and liquidity.
If the two conventional bonds differ from their matched green bond to a large degree
then both the yield and the liquidity of the synthetic bond may not be an accurate
measure with which to compare the green bond. Even though we control for liquidity in
the matching method as well as in the inclusion of the liquidity proxy in regression (A),
there may be a small residual liquidity bias in regression (B).

7. Conclusion

The demand for sustainable financial products has increased as a response to a growing
need for structural changes in global infrastructure and energy systems due to climate
change. In line with this demand, there is a need to maintain information access and
transparency in novel financial products aiming to increase the flow of funds to
sustainable solutions. To examine the effect that the project type being funded has on
the green premium, we use a matching method to identify 166 green bonds which we
subsequently match with a synthetic conventional bond. By controlling for residual
liquidity, we isolate the green premium.

              In the first step we find the existence of a green premium, in line with the
findings of Zerbib (2018) and Gianfrate and Peri (2019). We find a mean green
premium of -1.4 bps and a median of -1.67 bps. In the second step, the main focus of
this study, we find that the use of proceeds has a significant impact on the green
premium for three of the five project types. We find that energy has the largest effect on
the green premium out of the project types studied, while transportation is found to have
a positive premium, i.e. is priced lower than a corresponding conventional bond. three
of the five project types included are found to have a statistical significance, suggesting
that the use of proceeds is of interest to both issuers and investors in the green bond
market. In conclusion, this paper nuances the current research on green bonds by
introducing the underlying project being funded as a significant determinant of the
green premium.

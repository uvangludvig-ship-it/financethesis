           a study of Nordic mutual funds

        Does fund manager gender have an impact on mutual fund performance
                                in the Nordic market?

Ian Strauss Böttiger

Abstract
This thesis examines whether fund manager gender influences mutual fund performance and risk-taking in
the Nordic market, a region known for high gender-equality standards, yet still underrepresentation of
women in asset management. Using a comprehensive Morningstar Direct dataset of 1,132 actively managed
Equity, Fixed Income, and Allocation funds from 2014–2024, the study analyses gender effects through
descriptive statistics, OLS regressions, CAPM alpha and beta, variance tests, and robustness checks.
Performance is evaluated both across the full sample, as well as during the Covid-19 crisis to assess whether
market turbulence amplifies behavioural differences. Results show that gender differences are
category-specific rather than universal. In Equity funds, female managers outperform male managers on
Sharpe ratios and CAPM alphas while exhibiting similar beta, and volatility differs significantly by gender
only. Fixed Income funds reveal no meaningful gender-related differences, while Allocation funds show
similar returns but higher beta among male managers. During Covid-19, female equity managers continue
to outperform, reinforcing the robustness of the findings. Overall, gender appears to matter primarily in
settings with high managerial autonomy, such as equity management, whereas more constrained mandates
remain gender-neutral. The study adds evidence that gender–performance relationships are
context-dependent and shaped by mandate flexibility and market conditions within the Nordic market.

1. Introduction
Gender diversity has become an increasingly important topic in the financial industry, attracting attention
from investors, policymakers and academic researchers. Despite the progress in various kinds of industry
sectors, asset management and the finance industry remains strictly and broadly male dominated (Gavin A,
2024). This imbalance raises questions regarding whether gender-related differences in decision-making and
behavior may translate into systematic differences in investment performance and risk-taking.

A large body of literature suggests that men and women differ, on average, in their behaviors regarding
risk-taking, information processing and trading (Oskarsson ML, 2023); factors that are directly related to
and relevant for fund management. At the same time, institutional investors and regulators increasingly
emphasize gender diversity as an indicator of what makes a company well-governed and reliable in the long
run (Denis E, 2022). Thus, understanding whether a manager’s gender influences fund performance is
therefore relevant not only from a purely return perspective, but also from a broader societal and industry
standpoint.

However, empirical evidence on gender differences in mutual fund performance are showing mixed signs.
Some studies find (Niessen-Ruensi A & Ruenzi S, 2007) that female fund managers take less risk and achieve
a comparable risk-adjusted returns, while others report no meaningful difference once fund characteristics
and market conditions are taken into account (Babalos V, Caporale G.M, & Philippas N, 2015). These
conflicting findings highlight the need for additional, more focused research, particularly in the Nordics
context, where gender equality is strongly emphasized socially yet the fund industry remains unevenly
represented (Pal KK, Piaget K, Zahidi S & Baller S, 2024).

This thesis contributes to this discussion and prior studies by examining whether male- and female managed
funds differ in performance and risk-taking within the Nordic markets. Using a comprehensive dataset from
Morningstar Direct, covering Equity, Fixed income and Allocation funds, we analyze gender differences in
returns, risk-adjusted performance and market exposure over the full sample period of 2014-2024 as well as
during the Covid-19 crisis. By combining descriptive statistics, OLS regressions, variance tests and
robustness checks, we evaluate whether gender-related patterns can be found, after controlling for fund size,
volatility and benchmark exposure.

1.2 Literature review & contribution
Previous literature provides extensive evidence on this topic, but most studies have focused on U.S. or global
mutual fund markets. Atkinson et al. (2003) and Niessen & Ruenzi (2007), for instance, examine large
datasets of American funds and find no systematic performance difference between male- and
female-managed portfolios after accounting for risk. Other studies have discussed potential behavioral
differences, such as risk aversion, overconfidence, and trading intensity, yet the findings remain mixed and
largely inconclusive. Despite this growing body of work, there has been less research focusing on the Nordic
mutual fund industry. This omission is interesting given that the Nordic region combines well-developed
capital markets with some of the highest levels of gender equality globally. The combination of transparent
regulatory frameworks , strong investor protection, and an institutional emphasis on diversity makes the

                                          Herman Renhed & Ian Strauss Böttiger

Nordics a unique empirical setting (Lekvall P, 2014). In such an environment, gender-related differences in
investment behavior may either be amplified due to greater participation and visibility of female managers,
or muted through equality-driven norms that reduce structural biases in for example risk practices,
investment mandates and styles.

Our thesis extends the existing literature in three main ways. Firstly, we provide new evidence from the
Nordic mutual fund market, where gender equality and investor protection standards are among the highest
globally and differ from the rest of the world's capital markets. This setting allows us to test whether the
gender neutrality found in earlier U.S. and European studies also holds in a more equality-oriented
environment. Secondly, we use recent data from 2014 to 2024, capturing a period when diversity and
inclusion have become even more central to the financial industry. Thirdly, we examine whether market
conditions influence potential gender differences during turbulent periods, specifically the years during the
covid-19 crisis. Thirdly, during periods of market distress, risk-taking behaviour and excessive trading
activity tend to increase, which can negatively affect investment performance (Huber Ch, Huber H, Kirchler
M, 2021). Market turbulence therefore provides a setting in which differences in managerial
decision-making are more likely to influence returns. From this background, we examine whether the
Covid-19 period displays performance patterns that differ from the rest of the sample period.

Previous research such as Atkinson et al. (2003), Niessen and Ruenzi (2007), and Niessen-Ruenzi and
Ruenzi (2019) generally finds no significant differences in performance between male and female fund
managers. Based on these findings, we expect similar results in the Nordic context. However, behavioral
studies show that men tend to display higher risk tolerance and overconfidence (Byrnes et al., 1999; Barber
B.M & Odean T, 2001; Croson R & Gneezy U, 2009), which may lead to slightly higher volatility or
turnover, while women may prefer more stable investment strategies. which can also be seen in our data,
where the equity category is the category with the smallest share of FMFs (Table 1). In a Nordic market
characterized by transparency, flat hierarchies, and strong equality norms, such behavioral patterns may be
weaker. This provides an opportunity to examine whether gender equality reduces both risk-taking
differences and potential investor bias.

The contribution of our study is therefore both empirical and theoretical. Empirically, it broadens the
geographic and temporal scope of gender-performance research. Theoretically, it links behavioral finance
insights on risk-taking and overconfidence to a unique institutional context. Together, these aspects help to
clarify whether gender-neutral outcomes in fund performance remain consistent in some of the world’s
most gender-equal countries.

2. Methodology
This thesis follows a quantitative research approach, consistent with prior literature studies examining
gender effects in mutual fund performance (Niessen-Ruensi A & Ruenzi S, 2007). Since the research
questions handle measurable differences in returns, risk-taking and market exposure between male- and
female fund managers, an analytical approach which stands on statistical and econometric techniques is
most appropriate. The entire analysis is based on data collected from Morningstar Direct, a reputable and
widely used database in academic finance, ensuring that the underlying data are reliable and comparable to
previous studies.

The dataset contains actively managed Equity-, Fixed income- and Allocation funds operating in the Nordic
market. Index funds were excluded to ensure that performance outcomes are reflected by managerial
decisions rather than automatic benchmarking. To construct an accurate manager-gender classification, the
thesis adopts a common and justifiable rule; a fund is considered female-managed if women accounts for at
least 50% of the total manager-days over the period examined. This threshold captures sustained managerial
influence and avoids misclassification when short-term manager changes occur. Funds with incomplete
return histories or missing key characteristics were removed to avoid any biased results caused by
inconsistency in the time series. The resulting dataset contains 1132 funds of which 74 are classified as
female-managed.

The empirical analysis focuses on two periods; a full sample covering 2014-20204 and a more zoomed in
perspective of the turbulent period of Covid-19 covering 2020-2022. Analysing these periods jointly allows
the study to include both long-term performance patterns as well as the effects of extreme turbulent periods
on gender difference. For each fund, monthly return and excess return against each fund’s own benchmark
index was extracted from the data base. Using a relevant risk-free rate of Euribor 3 months allowed us to
calculate risk-free adjusted performance metrics, which was obtained from the European Central Bank.
Sharpe ratios were computed as the ratio between average excess return and volatility, while alphas and betas
were obtained through standard CAPM regressions using excess market returns. In addition, raw monthly
returns were used in robustness tests, ensuring that findings are not an artifact of any single performance
definition but rather a justifiable finding.
To examine whether gender predicts performance after accounting for key fund characteristics, a series of
ordinary least squares (OLS) regressions were conducted. Sharpe ratios, CAPM alpha and beta were each set
as the dependent variable in separate specifications, allowing the analysis to capture different versions of
performance; risk-adjusted return, benchmark outperformance and systematic risk exposure. All models
control for average fund size, return volatility and market excess return, commonly used as determinants of
fund performance in literature and studies. To account for structural differences across the fund types,
regressions were estimated separately for each fund category as well as for the full sample in order to find
category-specific patterns.This follows standard practice in mutual fund research papers. (Brad M, ,Barber
B.M, Odean T & Zheng L, 2005).

                                          Herman Renhed & Ian Strauss Böttiger

Risk-taking behavior was assessed using the standard deviation of monthly returns and statistically evaluated
with Levene’s test for equality of variance. This test is robust to non-normality, making it particularly
suitable for financial return data (Brown MB & AB Forsythe, 1974). The test determines whether male- and
female managed funds exhibit comparable variability in returns, thereby linking performance outcomes to
potential difference in risk-taking. This is important in the interpretation of performance, since higher
performance by one group could potentially simply be explained by higher risk-taking rather than superior
skill.

To validate the credibility of the results, robustness tests were performed. First, the performance regressions
were re-estimated using raw returns instead of Sharpe ratios, allowing us to test whether the results are
driven by the choice of risk adjustments. Second, the Covid-19 period’s regressions using alpha and returns
allow us to examine whether the full-sample results persist during the extreme turbulence. This dual-period
approach strengthens the validity of the findings and aligns with methodological recommendations in
empirical finance (Neumayer E & Plümper T, 2017).

Although the methodology is designed to minimize bias and reveal true and robust results, some limitations
must be acknowledged. Manager changes during the sample period may not be perfectly captured despite
the 50% threshold rule, and although Morningstar Direct is widely used in industry and research, it is
noteworthy to mention that these category classifications may not be perfectly precise. However, to address
this concern, a number of spot-checks in the sample have been done in order to confirm the validity of the
groupings and nothing wrong was found. Third, the gender classification is based on the proportion of days
managing the fund, which may not fully capture the timing of managerial transitions and may blur shorter
transition-period effects. Nonetheless, the methodology framework provides a justifiable and rigorous
foundation to address the thesis’s research question and enables to draw credible conclusions about gender
differences in the Nordic fund market.

3. Empirical results
3.1 Overview of and introduction to the empirical approach
The empirical analysis proceeds in four steps for the full sample period, and a more in-depth analysis of the
turbulent period of Covid-19. First, we present descriptive statistics for the full sample, followed by statistics
for each fund category (Equity, Fixed income and Allocation) in order to establish some preliminary
patterns in risk, return and fund characteristics across male- and female-managed funds. Second, we conduct
performance regressions using Sharpe ratios, alphas and betas as dependent variables to evaluate whether
gender is associated with differences in risk-adjusted performance after controlling for fund size, volatility
and market exposure. Third, we perform tests for gender differences in risk-taking using Levene’s variance
tests. Finally, we conduct robustness checks using raw mean returns instead of Sharpe ratios to validate
whether our conclusions hold under alternative performance definitions.

To complement the full-sample analysis, we extend the study to the Covid-19 period in order to examine
whether the gender-performance patterns found under normal market conditions persist during periods of
extreme stress and heightened uncertainty. The empirical analysis for the Covid-19 period follows the same
overall structure as the full-sample approach but focuses specifically on fund performance during conditions
of extreme market turbulence. First, we report descriptive statistics for female- and male-managed funds
across Equity, Fixed Income and Allocation categories in order to assess preliminary differences in
risk-adjusted performance during the crisis. Second, we estimate regressions of excess benchmark returns and
CAPM alpha, controlling for fund size and market exposure, to evaluate whether any gender-based
performance differences persist after accounting for risk. Third, we analyze volatility and downside risk to
determine whether observed return differences can be attributed to risk-taking rather than superior skill.
Lastly, we incorporate interaction terms between gender and fund categories to identify whether potential
performance differences are concentrated within specific segments of the market.

3.6 - Performance regressions - Covid
The results across the full 2014–2024 period show no statistically significant performance differences
between male and female managers in the Allocation and Fixed Income categories. In contrast, female
managed equity funds significantly outperform male-managed equity funds. This implies that, under
normal market conditions, gender does not systematically influence risk-adjusted performance in two of the
three fund categories, while equity appears to be an exception.

However, according to behavioral finance research, differences in decision-making and risk preferences are
more likely to become observable during turbulent market conditions. Prior studies indicate that male
managers tend to exhibit higher levels of overconfidence and more aggressive trading behavior, whereas
female managers generally adopt more conservative and stable investment strategies (Barber B.M & Odean
T, 2001; Niessen-Ruensi A & Ruenzi S, 2007). These behavioral differences may have little impact in
relatively stable markets, where rational pricing and efficiency limit the effect of individual decision-making.

Extreme market disruptions, on the other hand, increase uncertainty and reduce the predictive power of
historical information, potentially amplifying behavioral biases (Goel A, 2025).Therefore, if gender-based
differences in investment behavior influence performance, they are more likely to be visible during periods of
high volatility. For this reason, we conduct a separate analysis of fund performance during the Covid-19
crisis, which represents a unique and highly turbulent market shock in recent times. This allows us to
examine whether gender differences emerge in another way during times of financial stress.

During the COVID period, female-managed funds achieved higher excess benchmark returns than
male-managed funds in all three Morningstar categories. The difference is particularly pronounced within
Equity funds, where female managers outperformed their benchmarks by 0,1850%, compared with 0,045%
for their male counterparts. This gap is statistically significant (t = 2.65, p = 0.013), indicating that the
superior benchmark-adjusted performance among female Equity managers is unlikely to be driven by
random variation. In Allocation and Fixed Income, the differences are smaller and statistically insignificant,
suggesting no systematic gender effect outside the Equity segment. Overall, these findings indicate that
female fund managers performed better relative to their benchmarks during the market turbulence
associated with the COVID crisis, especially in equity management.

4. Discussion & analysis
4.1 Overview
The empirical results provide a clear and coherent picture of how performance and risk-taking differ across
male- and female-managed funds during the sample period. Across the full set of analyses (descriptive
statistics, performance regressions, variance tests, and robustness checks) the findings consistently show that
gender differences in fund performance are vastly category-specific rather than universal, with the most
noticeable difference in equity funds.

For equity funds, the regression output shows that female managers systematically outperform their male
counterparts on both risk-adjusted returns (Sharpe ratios) and benchmark-adjusted returns (alpha) after
controlling for size, volatility, and market exposure. The coefficient for male managers is negative and
statistically significant in both models, indicating that equity MMFs underperform relative to equity FMFs.
Importantly, this performance gap does not appear to be driven by differences in systematic risk-taking, as
the beta regressions reveal no significant gender differences in market exposure (β ≈ 1 for both groups). This
aligns with the variance results, which show that equity funds are the only category in which male and
female managers differ significantly in return volatility, suggesting a gender-related difference in risk-taking
behavior specific to this category. Taken together, these findings imply that the superior performance of
female equity managers reflects managerial skill rather than simply different levels of risk-taking or market
sensitivity.

In contrast, the results for fixed income and allocation funds point to a lack of systematic gender differences
in performance. In both categories, neither Sharpe ratios nor alphas differ significantly between MMFs and
FMFs, indicating that returns do not meaningfully vary across gender once the relevant control variables are
accounted for. However, allocation funds show one notable deviation; male managers exhibit a significantly
higher market beta than female managers, yet this additional exposure does not translate into higher
risk-adjusted or raw returns. This pattern implies that male allocation managers take on more systematic risk
without receiving likewise compensation, suggesting unnecessary or inefficient risk-taking. This is consistent
with the notion that categories with lower managerial discretion tend to present less manager performance
differences.

The robustness checks reinforce the credibility of these conclusions and findings (Neumayer E & Plümper
T, 2017). Re-estimating the models using raw mean returns produces results that reflect the main findings;
female equity managers continue to outperform significantly, while no gender-based performance patterns
exist in fixed income funds. That the equity-performance difference persists even when risk-adjustment is
removed suggests that the observed gap is not a statistical artifact of the Sharpe ratio but rather reflects a
fundamental difference in average returns during the sample period. To the contrary, the absence of gender
effects in fixed income across both raw- and risk-adjusted returns strengthens the robustness of conclusion.
The greater market exposure that male allocation fund managers are exposed to was found to not give any
greater returns, suggesting unnecessary and unjustifiable risk-taking.

Overall, the main results reveal a distinct pattern: female managers outperform in environments with high
managerial involvement and greater exposure to market uncertainty, such as equity funds, while no
significant gender-based return differences arise in more constrained categories. This provides an important
empirical foundation for the subsequent discussion, where these findings will be interpreted through the
lens of financial behavior theory, risk-preference research, and prior literature on gender and fund
management.

4.2 Do gender differences reflect risk-taking?
Since performance differences may stem from different levels of risk exposure, we begin by examining
whether male and female fund managers take on different levels of volatility. Risk is measured through the
standard deviation of monthly returns, and Levene’s test is used to evaluate whether the two gender groups
have equal variance.

For the full sample period, the results show no systematic difference in volatility between male- and
female-managed funds. At the total level, and within fixed income and allocation funds, both genders
operate with similar risk levels in terms of volatility. The only exception is found in equity funds, where the
variance test indicates a significant difference in volatility across genders. Since equity mandates provide
arguably more discretion than fixed income or allocation mandates, gender differences in risk-taking are
more likely to appear in such situations where managers have greater ability to influence the risk-taking
levels. Such settings are commonly linked to behavioural drivers such as overconfidence and tolerance for
ambiguity, which prior research suggests may vary across genders and influence portfolio choices (Beckmann
D, Menkhoff L & Suto M, 2008).

Zooming in on the Covid-19 period supports the previous results. During this turbulent period, allocation
and fixed income funds continue to show no significant volatility differences between male and female
managers, while equity funds again exhibit a statistically significant variation in return variance across the
genders. The fact that this pattern persists under extreme uncertainty indicates that it is unlikely to be driven
solely by temporary market dynamics, and moreover, as previous finding shows, maybe rather driven by
gender. From a behavioural perspective, this aligns with the idea that confidence levels and risk attitudes
tend to remain stable even when markets become highly volatile, meaning that underlying decision
tendencies are not easily offset by crisis conditions.

Overall, the results suggest that the performance differences across gender in equity funds cannot be
explained by broad differences in risk-taking. Volatility differences arise only in equity funds and appear
persistent rather than situational, indicating that the performance gap is more likely linked to investment
judgment within discretionary mandates. This contrasts with the assumption in behavioural finance that
women consistently take less risk. In fixed income and allocation funds, where managers operate under
similar investment constraints, male and female managers indeed take on comparable levels of risk. This
pattern implies that gender effects in mutual fund outcomes arise not from systematic differences in risk
appetite, but from decision quality in settings where managerial discretion is higher.

4.3 Are performance differences still present after adjusting for risk?
To determine whether gender differences in returns remain once risk is accounted for, we evaluate
performance using Sharpe ratios. Sharpe ratios measure excess return per unit of risk, thereby indicating
whether higher returns compensate for risk-taking or reflect genuine risk-adjusted outperformance. This
approach enables us to distinguish between managers who outperform because they assume more volatility,
and those who deliver superior performance without increasing portfolio risk.

For the full sample period, the results reveal that risk-adjusted performance differences are category-specific.
Within equity funds, female managers systematically outperform male managers after controlling for fund
size, volatility and market exposure. In contrast, no statistically meaningful gender differences are observed
in fixed income or allocation funds, where risk-adjusted performance remains largely similar across
managers. This pattern mirrors the earlier volatility analysis, suggesting that gender-linked differences in
performance emerge only in settings that experience greater managerial discretion.

The Covid-19 period provides an opportunity to examine whether this pattern holds under extreme
volatility. During this turbulent period, FMFs exhibit a modest but notable risk-adjusted performance
advantage, still only within equity funds. While the difference in Sharpe ratios for equity approaches
conventional significance, fixed income and allocation funds continue to show no meaningful gap. The
persistence of the equity-specific performance difference during a stressful market episode suggests that
female managers’ relative outperformance is not solely attributable to favourable market conditions, but
remains visible when uncertainty is at its highest, pointing towards greater managerial skill.

Overall, the Sharpe analyses indicate that the superior outcomes observed for female equity managers are not
a result of a simple risk–return trade-off. Rather than taking on disproportionate risk to earn higher returns,
their performance persists when normalizing for volatility, implying that the observed differences cannot be
attributed purely to risk-taking behaviour.

4.4 Interpretation of findings
Combining the volatility and Sharpe results provides a clear picture of how gender relates with fund
performance. In equity funds, performance differences do not appear to originate from broad variance in
risk-taking; rather, it seems to originate from managerial skill and decision-making, still holding true even
after accounting for various control variables. This suggests that gender differences in outcomes are more
likely driven by investment judgment or decision processes rather than by differences in portfolio risk
exposure.

The absence of significant gender effects and differences in allocation and fixed income funds strengthens
this interpretation. These categories are characterised by stricter investment guidelines, more constrained
risk choices and less ability for managerial intervention. When constraints limit individual decision ability,
performance becomes more homogenous across managers and gender effects diminish. To examine whether
these differences instead reflect exposure to systematic market movements rather than individual skill, we
further need to analyse market beta and its role in explaining performance patterns.

4.5 Do gender differences reflect market exposure?
Because fund returns may be driven by exposure to systematic risk rather than a manager stock-picking
ability, we further need to assess whether gender differences in performance correspond to variation in
market beta. Beta, estimated through CAPM regressions, captures the sensitivity of fund returns to
movements in the market index and serves as a proxy for systematic risk-taking. Across the full sample
period, the results show no evidence of difference in beta between FMF and MMF equity funds. Market
exposure remains close to one (β ≈ 1) for both groups, and the gender coefficient in the beta regressions is
statistically insignificant, indicating similar levels of market exposure. The same pattern persists within fixed
income funds, where beta differences are small and insignificant even after controlling for fund size,
volatility and excess market return. Looking at the allocation category, one can observe that male managers’
exposure to the market is slightly greater and this is a significant finding.

Extending the analysis to the Covid-19 period supports these patterns. Despite the heightened volatility and
large swings in global equity markets, beta differences between male and female equity managers remain
statistically insignificant and small. Only allocation funds display a notable difference, where male managers
exhibit significantly higher market exposure than their female counterparts. However, is it crucial to further
investigate if, and how, these different market exposure levels translate into actual return differences across
the genders in the categories.

4.6 Does performance persist after accounting for beta?
To determine whether gender differences in performance stem from higher market exposure or from value
added beyond systematic risk, we assess whether returns persist after controlling for beta. While CAPM beta
captures compensation for bearing systematic market risk, performance that remains after adjusting for beta,
in other words alpha, can indicate return that is unrelated to market exposure risk, such as stock selection or
timing ability which is attributable to the manager as skill. This interpretation aligns with Berk and van
Binsbergen’s (2012) broader interpretation of the Fama and French asset-pricing framework, which argues
that performance exceeding market risk exposures may reflect manager-specific skill rather than
compensation for systematic risk.

Across the full sample period, the results show that gender differences in risk-adjusted returns are solely
found within equity funds. Female equity managers outperform male managers on both Sharpe ratios and
CAPM alphas after controlling for fund size, volatility and market beta, indicating that their excess returns
do not derive from greater systematic risk exposure. In contrast, for fixed income funds, no meaningful
performance differences can be observed once beta is controlled for, suggesting that market exposure does
not drive return variation across managers in these categories. However, for allocation funds, the higher beta
does not compensate in terms of return as seen in the results section (Sharpe & Alpha), since both of these
are not statistically significant. Behavioral finance research consistently shows that men exhibit higher
overconfidence in financial decision-making, which often leads to for example higher risk-taking and market
exposure (Biais B, Hilton D, Mazurier K & Pouget S, 2004). The pattern found in allocation funds seems to
align with these behavioral finance research. The higher beta among male managers is consistent with this
tendency, yet the additional risk does not appear to yield superior risk-adjusted returns compared to females.

While our analysis does not directly test behavioral mechanisms, the pattern resembles overconfidence bias
which has been documented in prior research (Barber B.M & Odean T, 2001).

Extending the analysis to the Covid-19 period reinforces these findings, for all categories. Even under
stressed market conditions, female equity managers maintain a performance advantage; their alphas are
significantly higher than those of male managers, and their Sharpe ratios are close to conventional
significance thresholds. Since CAPM alpha adjusts returns for market exposure, this evidence suggests that
the observed performance gap during Covid is not explained by taking on greater market exposure during
volatility spikes. Overall, the persistence of the equity-based performance advantage after adjusting for beta
indicates that it cannot be attributed to systematic risk-taking. Therefore, it becomes further essential to test
whether these gender-related performance patterns still persist under alternative performance measures,
which we address next through a series of robustness checks.

4.7 Do the robustness checks strengthen the main findings?
By conducting robustness tests one can investigate raw mean returns to replace Sharpe ratios as the
performance metric in order to provide an important validation of the main regression results (Neumayer E
& Plümper T, 2017). These checks address whether the observed gender differences in performance could be
driven by the model of the Sharpe ratio itself, which contains both return and volatility. By using pure
monthly returns as the dependent variable, the analysis isolates performance from any risk-adjustment
mechanism and therefore tests whether the gender effects persist under an alternative performance
definition for validation.

Across all categories, the raw return regressions show a strong degree of consistency with the main results
reported earlier. In equity funds, the gender coefficient remains negative and statistically significant at the 5%
level, indicating that male-managed equity funds generate lower average returns than their female-managed
counterparts even before taking into account volatility or market exposure. The explanatory power of the
model remains very strong (R² ≈ 73%), reinforcing that this result does not arise from statistical noise or
sensitivity to model specification. This persistence of the gender effect in equity funds suggests that the
superior performance of female managers is not an artefact of the Sharpe ratio, but instead reflects a
fundamental return differential.For fixed income and allocation funds, the robustness results continue to
show no significant differences between FMF and MMF. The gender coefficients are small and statistically
insignificant in both categories, mirroring the findings from Sharpe, alpha, and beta regressions. This
consistency across models supports the view that gender-related performance differences do not emerge in
fund types where managers face tighter constraints and less room to influence outcomes through individual
decisions.

All in all, the robustness checks provide strong empirical support for the validation of the main findings.
The fact that the results remain unchanged when the performance metric is varied demonstrates that the
observed equity-specific performance advantage for female managers is not sensitive to model choice or
risk-adjustment technique. Instead, the pattern appears consistent and statistically meaningful. Lastly, the
continued absence of any gender effect in fund return for fixed income and allocation funds across all
robustness tests further supports the findings.

5. Conclusions of findings
The empirical analysis conducted in this thesis provides clear evidence that gender differences in mutual
fund performance within the Nordic market are not universal across all fund types, but rather highly
dependent on the level of managerial discretion that comes with fund type. Across all tests, including
descriptive statistics, OLS performance regressions, variance analysis, and robustness checks, a consistent
pattern is observed; significant gender performance differences arise only in equity funds. Fixed income and
allocation funds show no return differences across gender, although allocation funds reveal that male
managers take on greater market risk without achieving higher performance

The equity category stands out as the only segment in which female managers outperform their male
counterparts. This outperformance is observable both in risk-adjusted measures (Sharpe ratios) and in
benchmark-adjusted returns (CAPM alphas), and it persists even after controlling for fund size, volatility,
and market exposure. Also, the beta regressions demonstrate that male and female equity managers take on
similar levels of systematic risk, suggesting that the observed performance gap is not explained by higher
market exposure. The Levene’s variance test further shows that equity is the only category in which return
volatility differs significantly across gender, indicating that risk-taking behaviour itself differs in this category.
Together, these findings imply that the superior results of female equity managers are more related to
difference in investment judgment, managerial skill or decision quality rather than difference in various
forms of risk-taking.

For fixed income funds, the results indicate no systematic gender-based differences in performance,
risk-taking, or market exposure. These outcomes are consistent with the category’s more constrained
mandate structure, where stricter guidelines limit the scope for discretionary decision-making.

Allocation funds offer even more layers of interpretations. Although returns, whether measured as raw
monthly returns, Sharpe ratios, or CAPM alphas, do not differ significantly across gender, male managers
exhibit statistically significant higher market beta than their female counterparts. Since this additional
systematic risk does not translate into higher returns, male managers achieve the same performance while
taking on more market exposure. Interpreted through a risk-return lens, this means worse risk-adjusted
performance relative to female managers, even though the individual regression coefficients are not
statistically significant. The pattern therefore suggests that male managers in allocation funds take on
unnecessary and unjustifiable risk, reinforcing the idea that gender effects tend to appear only where
mandates allow for discretion.

The Covid-19 findings reinforces the full-sample findings. During this period of heightened volatility and
market uncertainty, female equity managers continue to outperform on alpha, and their Sharpe ratios
remain near conventional significance thresholds. The continuing of these effects in a crisis environment
strengthens the explanation that the gender-performance relationship in equity funds is robust to stressed
market conditions rather than an artefact of stable periods, and supports the findings regarding the
allocation category as well.

Finally, the robustness tests confirm the reliability of the main results. When performance is re-estimated
using raw monthly returns instead of Sharpe ratios, the central findings remain unchanged: female equity
managers continue to outperform significantly, while no gender effects emerge in fixed income or allocation
funds. This consistency across alternative performance metrics, time periods, and model specifications
provides strong validation of the equity-specific nature of the gender effects.

To summarize, the findings of this thesis demonstrate that gender matters for mutual fund performance
only in contexts where managerial discretion is high, such as equity funds. In more constrained fund
environments, gender does not appear to play a systematic role in shaping risk-taking or performance, like
fixed income funds. The results further indicate that excess market exposure among male allocation
managers does not translate into corresponding greater returns, highlighting an unjustifiable position in this
category’s risk-taking. These results contribute to the broader literature by showing that
gender-performance relationships are not universal but rather context-dependent, and the results highlight
the importance of considering mandate structure, behavioural tendencies, and market environment when
evaluating gender differences in professional investment management.

Can the corporate bond issuances of Swedish real estate firms be estimated
                 and predicted by publicly available data?

Lovisa Bernhardsson, 23638 Ellinor Lindstén, 23530

                     ABSTRACT
The Swedish corporate bond market has in recent years experienced increased activity. The real
estate sector has been particularly active, and in 2017 it accounted for 40 percent of the total
outstanding volume of corporate bonds denominated in Swedish kronor. This study sets out to
investigate whether publicly available market factors and company specific factors can on a
significant level be incorporated in a model that estimates corporate bond issuances in the
Swedish real estate sector, and accurately predicts them one quarter before they happen. Using
a logistic regression model applied on a sample of 31 firms in the Swedish real estate sector
and including indicator variables for each specific firm, we find that the interest rate STIBOR,
a residual between a regression of GDP and STIBOR, an indicator variable for the
implementation of bank regulations in 2011, the size of the firm, the leverage ratio, and EBIT
can on an 80 percent confidence level be incorporated in such a model. For the fourth quarter
of 2017, the model correctly predicts 24 out of 31 issuances for the companies in the sample.
However, the model produces a substantial amount of falsely positive outcomes; it predicts
issuances in the upcoming quarter despite these never being realised. Caution needs to be taken
when using the model for predicting future corporate bond activity of Swedish real estate firms,
both because the model overestimates the number of future bond issuance and because the
model is only applicable on the 31 firms in the sample. Furthermore, the model should ideally
be updated quarterly before being used. The results of the independent variables being
significant are interesting in themselves, but for making predictions with the model, users
should be aware of its limitations.

1. Introduction
The Swedish corporate debt market has long exhibited an extensive use of bank loans, but
recently an upswing in the use of corporate bonds and notes has been noted, especially for firms
within real estate rental, development, and operations (hereafter real estate firms). According
to an SEB market review (2017), their share of the total outstanding corporate bonds
denominated in Swedish kronor (SEK) has increased from around 10 percent in 2008 to
approximately 40 percent in 2017. The aim of this paper is to investigate whether it is possible
to develop a model which can estimate and predict future real estate bond and note issuances
in Sweden. By analysing data of Swedish real estate firms from 2008 to 2017, this paper
contributes by showing that the prevailing interest rate (Stockholm Interbank Offered Rate -
STIBOR), the gross domestic product (GDP) from the real estate sector, an indicator variable
for the bank regulations implemented in 2011, the size of the firm, the leverage ratio, and
earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) can be used to predict the likelihood of a corporate
bond or note issuance in the next quarter. We furthermore find that the model can be used as a
tool to predict corporate bond and note issuances in an out of sample period in the short term
future. In the fourth quarter 2017, the model could correctly predict 90 percent of the real estate
corporate bond issuances on the Swedish market. The high activity of real estate firms on the
bond market during recent years suggests that these firms have often found it advantageous to
issue corporate bonds compared to other types of financing. This could be due to market factors,
company specific factors, or a combination of them.
        The prevailing interest rates may prove beneficial for a particular form of financing, as
noted by Sveriges Riksbank (2016). When interest rates are low, it is generally more favourable
to issue corporate bonds, than when they are high. STIBOR is a common benchmark interest
rate for corporate bonds, and at the same time the average lending interest rates of banks seem
to follow STIBOR (see Figure I and Table I in Appendix). It is argued that the bond market
demand may increase in uncertain times, since corporate bond values are less volatile than stock
values.
       Using data from the period 1989 - 2013, Grjebine, Szczerbowicz and Tripier (2017)
show that substituting bank loans for bonds varies with the business cycle. Firms tend to
increase the usage of corporate bonds as a substitute for bank loans as the economy recovers
from a recession. Furthermore, when banks are faced by stricter requirements, as after the
implementation of Basel III, they in turn require more from their clients. Some firms are denied
loans while other firms are asked a higher price, making the bond market a better alternative.

                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

For example, Becker and Ivashina (2014) find strong evidence that bond-loan substitution is a
consequence of the contraction of bank supply. According to Sveriges Riksbank (2016), the
emitters on the Swedish corporate bond market have generally been large firms. These firms
can make issuances of several hundred million SEK, implying that the bond-loan substitution
possibilities differs between firms depending on their size. For real estate firms particularly,
Brown and Riddiough (2003) find evidence supporting that firms with a lower leverage ratio
when the financing need arises are more likely to issue bonds, than firms with a higher leverage
ratio are. Our findings are in line with these theories, as we find STIBOR, the Gross Domestic
Product (GDP) from the sector, and leverage ratio to all have a negative correlation with
corporate bond issuances in the following quarter. We also find that the bank regulations
implemented in conjunction with Basel III as well as categories of firm sizes, (small, medium-
sized and large) increase the likelihood of corporate bond issuances. Additionally, despite less
earlier theoretical support, we find that earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) has a negative
and significant effect on the likelihood of future bond issuance.
       By using the model developed in this paper, investors could make probable forecasts of
upcoming bond issuances of real estate firms before the announcement has been made. In time
periods in the near future, the model is likely to predict valid estimates of issuances, but the
input variables might need to be further adjusted as time passes by. The forecasts of this model
could possibly be used as an indication of future bond issuances, which would give investors
more time to consider investing in the issued bonds. Furthermore, although caution needs to be
taken for falsely positive outcomes (predicted issuances that are not actual issuances), the model
can be used as a tool, signalling a higher future activity of real estate firms in the corporate
bond market. This would be useful as the real estate sector already represents a large part of the
Swedish corporate bond market, and as investors typically seek industry diversification their
willingness to invest in these bonds might consequently be reduced. In addition, real estate
firms can make predictions of competitors’ upcoming bond issuances, and thereby evaluate
whether the timing is right for them to issue bonds.

2. Background

2.1. The Swedish real estate market
The following was clarified in Interview 2 (2018) with an employee at the high yield bond
department of one of the largest Swedish banks. Real estate firms are often considered rather
safe investments as they typically have contracts running for several years forward. Thus, they

                                    5
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

have proof of secured revenue streams, which easily can be communicated to capital markets.
For this reason, real estate bonds are considered to be rather safe no matter if they are secured
or unsecured. In the unlikely case of default, there will be assets in the form of property and
rental agreements or contracts, and investors are likely to get compensation from the
liquidation. However, different types of real estate firms are not equally safe investments. Firms
with a main focus on real estate development are usually riskier than firms heavy in rental or
operational activities.
       Landeman (2017) has made a thorough review of the corporate bond activity of the real
estate sector. The ten largest listed real estate firms increased the average value of their property
bases, from 27 billion SEK in 2011 to 57 billion SEK in 2016, at the same time as the total
liabilities increased correspondingly. When the real estate firms experienced this growth, the
search for external financing increased. This resulted in an upswing of real estate bond
issuances from 10 billion SEK in 2011 to 50 billion SEK in 2016. Out of the newly issued
bonds, many are unsecured. This is to compare with the traditional financing, when real estate
firms often have pledged collateral assets as security to the banks. In 2016, an estimated 40
percent of the real estate firms’ debt was placed on the capital markets, which can be compared
to 20 percent five years earlier.
       In Interview 2 (2018) it was clarified that external financing needs arise when the market
demand on property increases or when debt in the firm’s current capital structure matures.
External financing may also be sought in order to diversify or change the composition of the
capital structure. The expansion that many Swedish real estate firms have experienced in recent
years might in turn have been enabled by the increase in corporate bond issuances.

2.2. Corporate bond features and pricing
The corporate debt market includes both bonds and notes. In the words of Johansen (2017), the
primary difference between these instruments is that all bonds are regulated by securities laws,
while notes may be exempt from these laws. Notes in many cases have a maturity of less than
a year, even though notes with a longer time to maturity do exist. Bonds and notes are
considered to have relatively similar features and usage, and are in this paper referred to as
‘bonds’.
       Bond features are described for example by Swedbank and Sparbankerna (2013). Both
bonds and notes can have fixed interest payments or floating interest payments. Coupon bonds
have attached payments due monthly, quarterly or yearly, and a final payment at maturity. Zero-
coupon bonds on the other hand are issued at a price below its actual value, and at the maturity

                                    6
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

of the bond there is a payment that fully compensates for the term and the default risk that the
investors take on when investing in the bond. The interest payments are priced based on a
benchmark rate adjusted for the term to maturity, a premium for the default risk, and liquidity
barriers on the second hand market.
       The relationship between the benchmark interest rate and its corporate bond issuance
implications was discussed during Interview 1 (2018), with an employee at the investment
grade bond department of one of the largest Swedish banks. In Sweden, 3-month STIBOR, and
less commonly 6-month STIBOR, is used as the benchmark rate to price SEK-bonds. Many
bank loan interest rates are also heavily affected by STIBOR, as the average lending rate of
banks highly correlate with STIBOR (see Figure I and Table I in Appendix). When STIBOR is
low, it is overall more advantageous to issue a corporate bond relative to other financing
options, than it is when STIBOR is high. This is partly driven by the increased market demand
for corporate bonds in times when the interest rate is low, since institutional investors then look
for opportunities to achieve higher yields than they do by placing funds in risk-free assets such
as treasury bonds and T-bills (which have very low risk premiums and hence small yields when
the underlying interest rate is low). An investment grade corporate bond is, compared to a stock
which has varying and undetermined earnings, considered to be a safer investment, as stated by
Sveriges Riksbank (2016). In uncertain times such as in recessions, the demand for these bonds
is typically high due to their relatively low risk compared to stocks.
       Bonds typically have a longer time frame than bank loans, and if market conditions are
favourable, a firm has the opportunity to lock in the low interest rate for a longer time by issuing
bonds, as noted in Interview 1 (2018). Although no difference has been made in this paper
between corporate bonds and notes with fixed or floating rates, or with different terms of
maturity, it is reasonable to assume that this is one of the advantages when a firm issues bonds
or notes. This is a feature that makes it favourable to issue bonds particularly when the interest
rate STIBOR is low. Additionally, as noted in Interview 2 (2018), an important feature of
corporate bonds is that they rarely exhibit any amortizations, instead there is a large payment
when the bond expires. Bank loans on the other hand often have at least yearly amortisations,
leaving less room for the firm to manage their liquidity over a longer time period.

2.3. The Swedish corporate bond market
The reliance on bank loans as the main type of debt is strong in the Nordic region and in
Sweden, where the banking sector is strong. Sveriges Riksbank (2016), in their report about the
Swedish financial market, states that at the end of 2015, the lending of the Swedish banks to

the general public (firms, the public sector, households, and foreign interests) amounted to
3,031 billion SEK. Out of this, Swedish non-financial firms, which here includes real estate
firms, represented 41 percent; 1,243 billion SEK. Furthermore it is noted that since 2009, the
yearly increase in the bank loans of Swedish non-financial firms has been small.
       Sveriges Riksbank (2016) further notes that during the past years the bond market has
grown successively, and the non-financial corporate issuers have increased their share in the
total volume emitted. From 2006 to 2015, the total outstanding amount of SEK-denominated
corporate bonds, issued by Swedish non-financial firms, increased from approximately 120
billion SEK to 380 billion SEK. Corporate bonds composed around a fifth of the debt financing
in these firms in 2015, but the majority was still in the form of loans from credit institutions.
Landeman (2017) states that in 2017, the total outstanding volume of corporate bonds of
Swedish non-financial firms had increased to 420 billion SEK. Out of this, 160 billion SEK was
represented by the real estate sector.
       The Swedish corporate bond market is mostly used by large firms with an investment
grade credit rating, although Sveriges Riksbank (2016) notes that the usage by smaller firms,
and firms with lower credit rating, recently has increased. The corporate bonds of low credit
rated firms are called high yield bonds (or junk bonds) and are separated from investment grade
bonds in many funds. In the beginning of 2017, 40 billion SEK were high yield bonds out of
the total accumulated value of 160 billion SEK represented by real estate firms, according to
Landeman (2017). However, SEB market review (2017) show that in 2016 and 2017, all real
estate SEK-bond issuers were either investment grade or non-rated firms.
       Swedish real estate firms operate mostly, or only, nationally. Consequently, they are
heavy in SEK-denominated bonds. In addition to SEK-denominated bonds, real estate firms
occasionally issue Eurobonds or bonds denominated in Norwegian kroner. Landeman (2017)
acknowledge that the amounts outstanding of these bonds are fairly small in comparison to
SEK-bonds, but Eurobonds in particular have become more common. In 2016, four of the
largest Swedish real estate firms had a total accumulated value of 24 billion SEK outstanding
in Eurobonds.
       Finansinspektionen (2017) states that the Swedish bond market is characterized by a
limited number of market makers and generally large transaction values. A bond issuance
usually amounts to numerous hundred million SEK. The lack of possibility to issue small
amounts of bonds may limit firms with a small capital base or with a small need for financing
in their choice, which reduces their access to the corporate bond market.

2.4. Regulations
The following was discussed during Interview 2 (2018). The component of the pricing of bank
loans that is not connected to STIBOR may change in conjunction with a change in regulations
impacting the capital requirements of banks. Banks have to cover their costs for holding capital
for their outstanding loans and have to price the loans that they give to clients accordingly. The
capital holding component in the pricing does not exist on the corporate bond market. The bond
investors want an expected return, but this return can be positive and still lower than the return
plus capital holding component in bank loans.
       Basel III is an internationally agreed set of mandatory measures, which is developed by
The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision as a response to the financial crisis 2008, refer
to The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (2010). It includes a set of minimum
requirements for banks with an international reach. Foremost, Basel III sets demands on the

3. Theoretical framework and earlier research

3.1. Financing options
When firms need to raise external financing, they can do this by issuing equity in the form of
stocks, or by raising debt for example through a bank loan, or through an issuance of bonds.
The trade-off theory of capital structure, developed by Kraus and Litzenberger (1973), explains
how the company chooses the amount of debt versus equity financing to be used by comparing
costs and benefits. After the determination of debt and equity, management has to decide which
type of debt or equity that should be used.
      By examining the financing choice and liability structure of Real Estate Investment
Trusts (REITs), Brown and Riddiough (2003) find evidence that the average size of debt or
equity issuances is higher when there is an underlying investment purpose. This since it is easier
to gain access to the capital markets when there are investment opportunities than when the
funds are to be used to simply change the firm’s debt structure. They also find leverage ratios
to be determining in the choice of debt or equity financing. According to their paper, bond
issuers target a certain debt ratio in the longer run, which optimally is a minimum investment
grade credit rating. The most significant factor determining the financing choice was found to
be the debt structure at the issuance. The debt structure was a more predictive factor than the
total debt level, even though some evidence was found that firms with a higher total debt level
were less likely to issue more debt. Firms with a debt structure consisting of a high ratio of
senior secured debt before the issuance were more likely to issue equity, while firms with a
higher ratio of junior unsecured debt generally issued corporate bonds. While some firms used
a more complex capital structure consisting of a mix of several types of both unsecured and
secured debt, some firms solely used bank loans. The authors find evidence that REITs that
have not issued bonds in the past are unlikely to do so in the near future.

3.2. Debt structure choices over the business cycle
Using data from the period 1989 - 2013, Grjebine, Szczerbowicz and Tripier (2017) show that
substituting bank loans for bonds is a regular feature of the business cycle. Firms tend to
increase the usage corporate bonds, as a substitute for bank loans, when the economy recovers
from a recession. They further provide evidence that economies with a well-developed
corporate bond market, where bonds account for a large share of total debt securities, generally
recover faster from recessions than other economies do.
       On the note of bond-loan substitution varying with the business cycle, Becker and
Ivashina (2014) state that credit is highly pro-cyclical. They acknowledge the difficulties in
determining whether this depends on the supply side, since banks’ lending willingness is
reduced in uncertain times, or on the demand side, because firms do not desire to borrow. By
using data from the United States and arguing that some firms have a positive demand for debt
but are forced to issue bonds when the banks contract the credit supply, their theory predicts
that the business cycle variation in the issuances of corporate bonds as opposed to bank loans
may be affected by the prevailing credit supply. Kaya and Wang (2016) find similar evidence
for the Eurozone, showing that tightening on loans significantly increases bond issuances,
particularly in the core Eurozone countries with well-developed debt markets.
       Fama and French (1993) state that there is a term premium and a default premium on
bonds, which should capture most of the variation in the return on both government and
corporate bond portfolios. Regarding corporate bonds, the shift in economic conditions change
the likelihood of default and thereby the premiums that has to be obtained. The term premium,
on the other hand, arises from unexpected changes in interest rates. The premium above
STIBOR for both bank loans and corporate bonds depends largely on the riskiness of the firm
and on the time compensation.

3.3. Information asymmetry
Diamond (1984) pointed out the comparative advantage in compiling information and
monitoring that banks have over public investors. Banks spend resources on acquiring
information about the firms which they provide credit to, and monitor them throughout the term
of the debt. This information acquisition component is significantly larger for banks than for
public investors, which according to Boot and Thakor (2009) in general makes bank loans more
expensive than issuing debt on the capital market. Other investors do not have the possibility
to acquire the large amount of information that banks do, and hence bond financing is said to
be cheaper. According to Boot and Thakor (2009) banks have better incentives to invest in

                                    11
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

acquiring information, since they typically have a larger stake in the funding. Fama (1985)
argued that banks have access to inside information about their counterparties, whereas other
investors typically only have publicly available information. Hence, banks are more efficient at
solving the information asymmetry issue related to financing than public investors are, meaning
that these two groups may price debt differently. The exact price difference due to the
information asymmetry between banks and bond investors may nonetheless depend on the
quality and transparency of the firm, and the experience and size of the bond investor. In
Interview 1 (2018), we note that the market’s pricing is affected by the perceived default risk,
and that the most widely used indication of firm default risk among smaller private investors,
is the credit rating of the firm.
       De Fiore and Uhlig (2011) suggest that the market debt, from the firm owners’
perspective, is riskier than bank loans, since the agency costs related to the information
asymmetry are higher. The market has no way of collecting the same amount of information
about a bond issuing firm as a bank. If this is correct, mainly firms with low credit risk should
use market debt, especially in uncertain times such as recession and the beginning of recovery.
Riskier firms should according to this theory not substitute bank loans for bonds.

4. Hypothesis
The purpose of this thesis is to determine whether it is, at a significant level, possible to estimate
real estate bond issuances in a following quarter. As a basis for the research, market factors and
company specific factors are analysed from the first quarter of 2008 to the third quarter of 2017.
The time frame is chosen in order to include both a boom and a recession. The chosen publicly
available market factors and company specific factors are based on theory, background and our
perception of the factors that supposedly benefit or disfavour corporate bond issuances. The
market factors that will be tested are the interest rate STIBOR, GDP contribution from the real
estate sector, the regulations implemented in Sweden following Basel III, and the corporate
bond index NASDAQ OMX Credit SEK. These factors are to be combined in a model with the
publicly available company specific factors EBIT, EBIT over interest, total assets, total debt
over capital, cash and equivalents, current portion of long term debt and capital leases, and cash
from investing. All independent variables will be measured in a quarter previous to the issuance
quarter for which predictive estimations are made. Additionally, it will be tested whether a bond
issuance in one quarter affects the probability of a bond issuance in the following quarter.

                                    12
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

Hypothesis: A model based on selected publicly available market factors and company specific
factors can estimate real estate bond issuances in the following quarter.

Given that the hypothesis is not rejected, the estimation model will also be used as a prediction
tool of the probability of issuances in the out of sample fourth quarter of 2017. This can be seen
as a test of whether the model can be applied outside of the analysed time frame.

5. Method

5.1. Demarcation of study
The chosen time frame is the years 2008 to 2017, measured on a quarterly basis. The estimation
model is based on the first quarter of 2008 to the third quarter of 2017, and data from this period
will be referred to as in sample. The model will be tested on the out of sample period of the
fourth quarter of 2017. Data from this time period is available on both Thomson Reuters Eikon
and S&P Capital IQ.
        The geographical market is limited to Sweden, since bond markets differ among nations.
Real estate firms with Sweden as their primary geographical market were therefore selected.
Only SEK-denominated bonds are included in the analysis.
       The real estate sector was chosen to be analysed in this paper. Many firms within the
real estate industry have for some time used corporate bonds as a type of financing, which
supposedly makes the available issuance data sufficiently numerous to build an estimation
model upon. The Swedish real estate sector is relatively homogenous, and the real estate firms’
main activities are development, rental and operations of residential and commercial property.
Firms are unequally heavy in development, rental and operations, implying slightly different
characteristics, but have all been included in the sample.

5.2. Data
61 real estate development, rental, and operations firms with Sweden as their primary market
were in March 2018 available in Thomson Reuters Eikon. Since a limited access to the bond
market may be a factor that makes it impossible for some firms to issue bonds, only firms that
had, at some time in the period from the first quarter of 2008 to the third quarter of 2017, made
an issuance were chosen and included in the in sample data. This sample includes data on
outstanding and historical corporate bonds issued from 2008 to 2017, and was retrieved from
Thomson Reuters Eikon. There were both coupon and zero-coupon bonds and notes, with either

                                    13
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

fixed or floating rates. The exclusion of firms that have not issued bonds in a single time period,
according to Thomson Reuters Eikon database, ensures that all firms in the sample had access
to the market at some point in time, and hence bond issuance was a feasible choice in at least
one quarter. Thus, formal access to the market in the next quarter can be assumed.
       In addition to the bond issuance data from Thomson Reuters Eikon, company specific
data was retrieved from S&P Capital IQ. The data retrieved from S&P Capital IQ is available
in the financial statements of the firms, and is thus considered to be publicly available. The in
sample data was retrieved for the period first quarter of 2008 to third quarter of 2017. Out of
sample data for the same firms and their company specific factors was retrieved for the fourth
quarter of 2017. The out of sample data was not used to build the model, but to make the final
predictions. Company specific data on the firms in the sample included EBIT, EBIT over
interest expense, total assets, total debt over capital, current portion of long term debt and capital
leases, cash & equivalents, and cash from investing. An attempt was made to retrieve data about
the credit rating of the firms, but this data was missing in most time periods for a majority of
the firms. If a figure or a full quarter has not been reported by S&P Capital IQ for a specific
firm, this has been interpreted as a missing value. The analysis is thus dependent on the accuracy
of the database.
       The data sample, when combining the data retrieved from Thomson Reuters Eikon and
S&P Capital IQ, consisted of 32 firms (see Table II in Appendix). As the model requires a
preformed issuance and company specific data for the previous quarter, Delarka Holding AB is
excluded, as the only issuance made was done during the first quarter of financial statements
reported in S&P Capital IQ. After dropping Delarka Holding AB from the sample, 31
companies remain in the dataset.
       Quarterly data on STIBOR (Stockholm Interbank Offered Rate) from the first quarter
of 2008 to the fourth quarter of 2017 was retrieved from the Central Bank of Sweden’s archive.
3-month STIBOR was chosen, as this is in most cases the benchmark rate for the pricing of
corporate bonds. Additionally, the average pricing of bank loans has in the time period to a
large extent followed 3-month STIBOR, as seen in Figure I in Appendix. GDP figures from the
quarterly contribution of the production side of the real estate sector, was retrieved from
Statistics Sweden. The NASDAQ OMX Credit SEK daily price index was retrieved from
Nasdaq’s historical archive, for the period 2011 to 2017. The daily index was converted to
quarterly measurements by taking the quarterly mean of the price index.

5.2.1. Dependent variable
Corporate bond issuance: The dependent variable is whether or not a company issues SEK-
denominated bonds or notes in a specific quarter. The independent variables are measured in a
quarter prior to the corresponding dependent variable. The dependent variable is binary as it
assumes the value 0 if a bond or note has not been issued, and a value 1 if a bond or note has
been issued. The amount of the bonds and notes issued do not matter for the dependent variable.
Henceforth, the issuances of bonds and notes will be referred to as bond issuances since bonds
and notes, as mentioned in the background, have similar features.

5.2.2. Explanatory market factors
STIBOR: 3-month STIBOR will be incorporated in the model, since the model should capture
the prevailing rate on the market. The correlation between 3-month STIBOR and the Central
Bank of Sweden’s repo rate is close to perfect (see Figure II and Table III in Appendix).
STIBOR is measured in percentage units and is lagged one quarter since the variation is
expected to explain a part of bond issuances in the following quarter.
       Gross Domestic Product (GDP): GDP from the production side of the Swedish real
estate sector has been chosen as a measurement of the business cycle. GDP from this sector
correlates highly with GDP from the production side of the entire economy. The seasonally
adjusted GDP from the sector has been chosen, as the aim is to investigate the probability of
bond issuances depending on the overall business cycle rather than the season. GDP from the
sector is measured in millions of SEK. In the model, GDP is lagged one quarter so that the GDP
of one quarter is expected to explain some variation in bond issuances in the next quarter.
      NASDAQ OMX Credit SEK Index: The NASDAQ OMX Credit SEK price index is
measured in SEK, and is incorporated in the model through a one quarter lag. The price index
in one quarter is thus expected to explain part of the variation of bond issuances in the upcoming
quarter.
       Regulations: Basel III was introduced after the financial crisis of 2008-2009, but in
Sweden further requirement were implemented in November 2011, see Finansinspektionen
(2011). In the model, it has therefore been assumed that there is a shift after the fourth quarter
of 2011, after which issuing a bond becomes more favourable relative to taking up a bank loan.
An indicator variable has been created which assumes the value 0 in the years 2008 to 2011,
and the value 1 in the years 2012 to 2017. The variable is thus not time lagged.

                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

5.2.3. Explanatory company specific factors
Leverage ratio: The total debt over capital, referred to as the leverage ratio, is expressed in
percentage units, and lagged one quarter so that the leverage ratio in one quarter is expected to
explain part of the variation of bond issuances in the upcoming quarter.
       Cash from investing: An attempt to include a variable representing the cash outflow
from investment activities will be made. It will be tested to time lagged the cash from investing
variable one quarter. The rationale behind this is that since it is easier to obtain financing for a
project (and projects typically include several parts that result in large expenses) we suspect
that the bond issuance sometimes might come in the middle of the project, when cash outflows
from investments have already started to increase. Cash from investing is measured in millions
of SEK. The values are negative if the firm is making net investments.
       Current debt: The current portion of long term debt and capital leases, referred to as
current debt, is the percentage of senior bank loans, bonds, or other types of debt, that has an
expiration date within one year from the quarter in question. The current debt is expressed in
percentage units, and lagged one through four quarters in an attempt to find a refinancing pattern
that fits the firms in the sample.
       Cash: Cash and equivalents, referred to as cash, classified as an interest bearing current
asset on the balance sheet, is measured in millions of SEK, and lagged one quarter so that
issuances in the upcoming period are supposedly partly explained by the current cash. The
rationale behind including the cash variable is that we believe that firms issuing bonds
oftentimes hold less cash than had they taken up corresponding bank loans. This is due to that
corporate bonds often have no amortisation requirements.
       EBIT: EBIT was analysed in order to capture the possible effects of the companies’
health and profitability. The variable is measured in millions of SEK and lagged one quarter,
since we believe that the signalling value of the profit in the current period might explain part
of the variation in bond issuances in the upcoming period.
       EBIT over interest: In order to capture companies’ liquidity, EBIT over interest expense
was chosen to be included in the model. This ratio shows how many times over the company
can pay its current interest payments with its earnings for the period. EBIT over interest is a
factor assuming positive values. The variable was lagged one quarter.
        Size of the firm: It was expected to be of importance which size category the individual
companies belonged to in every separate time period, rather than their exact balance sheet value
in SEK. This led to the generation of a class variable with three groups for company size. The

                                    16
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

three groups were small, medium sized and large firms, assuming the values 1 to 3. The small
firms were the companies with total assets less than or equal to 2 billion SEK, medium-sized
firms had total assets larger than 2 billion SEK and smaller than or equal to 50 billion SEK, and
large firms had total assets larger than 50 billion SEK. The expectation is that larger companies
will have better access to the bond market, as there is a minimum issuance amount on corporate
bonds in Sweden. Small firms are expected to be least likely to issue bonds. By this size
division, we expect that most firms in the sample will be medium-sized. We thus assume that
most firms, the medium-sized, have the similar accessibility to the bond market and judging
solely by the categorical size variable are equally likely to issue bonds. The variable was lagged
one quarter.
       Recent bond: A variable that explains whether the company has made a bond issuance
in the previous period was introduced. The rationale behind this is that a recent bond issuance
intuitively should decrease the need of a new bond issuance in the present period. In the
constructed model, this factor was implemented as an indicator variable that assumes the value
1 when an issuance was made the previous period, and 0 when an issuance was not made. Thus
it was not time lagged in the regression.

5.3. Logistic regression model
To start with, a model including the above mentioned factors is constructed. Most probably, it
is not reasonable to include all these factors in the final model, but through stepwise regression,
insignificant variables could be removed. A logistic regression is performed. Important to note
is that this model does not assume dependency over time within firms, which would be assumed
if a model for panel data had been used. The reason for not choosing a panel data logistic
regression model is that while some of the independent variables, such as STIBOR, GDP,
leverage ratio and size of the firm may be dependent over time, our dependent variable for bond
issuances is most likely not. Whether a company has issued a bond in a previous quarter or not,
is in our view not fully determining whether they will do so in the future. We have accounted
for some dependency by including a variable for a recent bond issuance in the immediately
previous quarter. Market conditions change, which may lead firms to be willing to issue bonds
for the first time. Therefore, by using the standard logistic regression we see each observation
as dependent on what happened in the previous quarter (through the usage of time-lagged
variables), but not dependent on what happened even earlier. In order to acknowledge that there
are firms measured at several point in time, at 39 different quarters, we cluster the standard
errors of the model based on the firm.

                                    17
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

Individual companies are interpreted as indicator variables in the regression. This in order to
incorporate the differences between firms, and trace every observation to a specific company,
as the logistic model itself has not used the data as panel data. The companies have to be
separated since they are not equally likely to issue bonds. Not including this would be a very
general model, calculated on the means of all firms in the sample. The firms in the sample have
different issuance patterns, as some firms make an issuance in almost every quarter, and some
firms have only made one, and so treating all companies similar would not be appropriate.
Implications of this is however that it is not possible to make predictions for any firm not
included in this particular sample, as a coefficient is calculated for each firm in the sample. A
new base model can easily be re-estimated based on a slightly different sample.
       When the final model has been constructed, a logistic regression with fixed effects,
specifically for panel data, is performed in order to control that the assumption of independence
over time is reasonable. If it is, the coefficients will not change significantly, and the P-values
will not become too high. If the coefficients would have significantly higher P-values in the
model made for panel data, the sample would suffer from high serial correlation. If this is the
case, using a standard logistic regression would overestimate the accuracy of the model.
       A maximum P-value of 0.20, meaning a confidence interval of 80 percent, was required
to keep the variables in the model. This confidence interval was chosen since this was found as
a reasonable requirement on a new model.

5.4. Tests
Pair-wise correlations between all variables will be investigated prior to creating an initial
estimation model in order to control for possible multi-collinearities. Through this procedure,
it will be investigated if two or more independent variables have an alarmingly high, positive
or negative, correlation. If the pair-wise correlation between two independent variables in
absolute terms is equal to or higher than 0.75, the two variables will not be included at the same
time.
        Since the model is built with a logistic regression, it is appropriate to test whether it
follows the chi-squared distribution. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test using ten
groups and ten minus two (eight) degrees of freedom is performed. This test shows how well
the model estimates values that follow the chi-squared distribution, with a certain number of
groups according to the predicted probabilities of issuances. It has been considered valid to test
for ten groups, since this is a standard number in the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. Thus, the first
group consists of the observations with the ten percent lowest probabilities, and so forth. The

                                    18
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

Hosmer-Lemeshow statistic is calculated as the Person chi-square statistic from observed and
expected frequencies, and then compared to the chi-squared distribution of the number of
groups minus two degrees of freedom. A large Hosmer-Lemeshow statistic or a low P-value
signifies a poor fit of the model, and if the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test is not
satisfactory, the logistic model assumptions may not be fulfilled.

5.5. Prediction
Based on the results of the logistic regression model, a prediction of the probability of corporate
bond issuances will be made on the in sample data, ranging from the first quarter of 2008 to the
third quarter of 2017. The estimation model should return values in between 0 and 1, meaning
a probability of a bond issuance between 0 and 100 percent. Thereafter the predicted
probabilities will be compared to the actual outcomes. An optimisation of the classification of
predicted probabilities will be made, so that as many actual issuances as possible are classified
as predicted issuances by the model. The same optimised cut-off will then be used on the out
of sample data from the fourth quarter of 2017 in order to see how well the model functions for
near-term future predictions.

5.6. Selection bias
Selection bias may be present in the analyses, which limits the generalizability of the model.
We note that the model is only applicable on firms that are included in the selected sample. The
model is therefore very specific, and conclusions cannot be drawn on firms outside of the
sample. This is a consequence of the fact that the model incorporates everything that is firm
specific in indicator variables. A flaw is that we cannot be sure that the firms in the sample
represent firms in the Swedish real estate sector in general. As noted, 61 firms classified as real
estate development, rental, and operations were available in Thomson Reuters Eikon, and only
31 have been included in the sample used to build the model upon. Therefore, the conclusions
drawn are not applicable for the real estate sector in general, but rather for the 31 firms in the
sample.
        In order to limit the selection bias, we aimed at including all firms in the Swedish real
estate sector that had in one or more quarters during the time period from the first quarter of
2008 to the third quarter of 2011 issued a SEK-denominated corporate bond. However, we were
further limited by the coverage of the database S&P Capital IQ which does not cover all of
these firms, and the total sample therefore includes only 31 firms. This is approximately half of
the firms that according to Thomson Reuters Eikon are classified as Swedish real estate

                                    19
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

development, rental, and operations, which we initially aimed at including. Since only half of
the firms in the sector are included, we will not be able to draw conclusions for the sector, but
rather for the firms in the sample.

5.7. Estimation bias
Estimation bias may be present in the analyses, which further limits the generalizability of the
model. Due to missing values in one or more of the factors in the model, a substantial amount
of observations is lost while running the logistic regression analysis, which may cause biased
estimates. An observation where one independent variable or the dependent variable is missing,
is classified as a missing observation and is thus not included when running the regression for
the estimation model.
       The available data for company specific variables in the earliest time periods in the
sample are rather few. The result of this is that the time period and the firms included in the
sample are rather limited. Furthermore, only one recession and one boom is included. The
model is therefore built on actual corporate bond issuances that might be specific for the time
period. A risk is that the estimated effects of the independent variables are capturing a prevailing
trend, which is not due to market factors or company specific factors, but rather a temporary
popularity, or lack of popularity, of bond issuances.
       The sample may include firms that are deviating from a general pattern of bond
issuances. This will be captured in the firm specific variables. If firms in the sample differ a lot
in both directions from the general pattern, the indicator variables will capture a larger part of
the variation in bond issuances, than they would have with a more general issuance pattern. The
other independent variables which are proven to be significant will in this case capture a smaller
share of the total variation in bond issuances, giving these specific variables a lower explanatory
power. Thus, it will be hard to prove that independent variables, aside from the company
indicator variables, are useful when building a model similar to this without accounting for
unidentified company specific effects.

6. Findings and analysis
Our results show that STIBOR, a residual between GDP and STIBOR, the size of the firm, the
leverage ratio, EBIT and regulation on a significant level contribute to the variation in bond
issuances. The contributions of the size category and the regulation variable are positive,
whereas the contributions of STIBOR, the GDP residual, leverage ratio and EBIT are negative.
We confirm our hypothesis that a model based on selected publicly available market factors

and company specific factors can estimate real estate bond issuances in the following quarter
for the real estate firms in the sample.

6.2. Correlations
There is pair-wise correlation between a few of our variables, exceeding 0.75 in absolute terms
(see Table V in Appendix). The index variable had a correlation of 0.90 with STIBOR, and
0.87 with GDP. The index cannot, according to the decision rule of an upper correlation limit
of 0.75 in absolute terms, be included in a model with STIBOR and GDP.
      The pairwise correlation between STIBOR and the seasonally adjusted GDP measured
over the full time period is -0.59 (see Table V in Appendix). This relationship differs from the
one described in the background; that for example in a recession when GDP is low, the interest
rate is typically low to stimulate consumption rather than savings. However it is not very
surprising, as the Central Bank of Sweden, for several years has practiced an easy money policy
with low interest rates, while the economy has been booming. The correlation between STIBOR
and GDP in 2008 to 2011 is positive; 0.77. In 2011 to 2017, the corresponding correlation is -
0.95, close to perfectly negative. Based on the background, the years 2011 to 2017 are
somewhat problematic, as the booming economy disfavours bonds compared to stocks, while
the low interest rate is beneficial for bond issuing firms. There is a trend of increased number
of issuances over time, particularly from 2011 and onwards. This could imply that the booming
economy has not had a large negative impact on the issuances in this sector; the low interest
rates, and possibly other favourable factors, have overrun the business climate development.
Since the correlation between STIBOR and GDP in this later period is close to perfectly
negative, it is likely that including both factors in the model would lead to a high degree of
multi-collinearity, despite the fact that the correlation over the full period does not exceed 0.75.
However, GDP might still capture some of the variation that STIBOR does not, and theories
supporting both of these variables have been presented. A method for including the additional

explanatory power of GDP compared to STIBOR is to regress GDP and STIBOR and to
thereafter use the residual in the model. Thus, we assume that GDP can be denoted as STIBOR

seems as though the time between two issuances is highly dependent on the specific company.

incorporated in the general model.

the chosen confidence level of 80 percent.

6.6. Discussion of variables included in the final estimation model
STIBOR: Interest rates can be used as tools to stimulate or impede the economic cycle. In a
recession, interest rates are typically low to stimulate consumption. Also, as mentioned in the
background, when interest rates are low and economic conditions are uncertain, investors are
keen to invest in bonds, making issuances more frequent, refer to Sveriges Riksbank (2016).
The correlation between issuances and STIBOR is thus supposedly negative, and we thereby
find it reasonable that the model coefficient of STIBOR is negative. The standard error is
notably smaller than the coefficient, implying that we can be fairly sure that STIBOR does have
an observable negative correlation with corporate bond issuances.
       GDP residual: Grjebine, Szczerbowicz and Tripier (2017) show that bank loan to bond
issuance substitution is a feature of the business cycle. According to their research, bond usage
should increase as the economy recovers from recession. By this reasoning, the coefficient of
the GDP variable is supposed to be negative. Due to the high correlation with STIBOR,
especially from 2011 and onwards, we have not included GDP in the model. We however find
that the residual from the regression between GDP and STIBOR is significant and has a
negative coefficient. The variation of the GDP residual over time can be seen in Panel V below.
Since the variation of the GDP residual depends on the variation in GDP and in STIBOR, it is
from this variable hard to determine what sign the coefficient would be expected to have over
time. However, lately STIBOR has been low while the economy has been booming, which
would according to theories imply one positive impact on the probability of issuances through
STIBOR, and one negative impact through GDP. The GDP residual should capture the part that
STIBOR does not. In this time period the high GDP, not correlating with STIBOR, should have
a negative impact on the probability of bond issuances, which should be captured by the GDP
residual. In Panel V below, we note that the GDP residual is highest in the years 2008-2009,
and in 2015-2017. The negative coefficient means that this variable reduces the model’s
estimated probability of corporate bond issuances, especially in the earliest and the latest years
in the sample. The large negative impact in 2015-2017 is most likely due to the high GDP,
which implies that Grjebine, Szczerbowicz and Tripier’s (2017) theory of bond issuances
varying over the business cycle seems to apply to Swedish real estate firms as well. The large

                                    29
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

negative impact in 2008-2009 might on the other hand be due to the high prevailing interest
rates, in line with the reasoning of Sveriges Riksbank (2016). We judge it reasonable that the
GDP residual has a negative coefficient in the final model.

Regulation: Basel III was introduced after the financial crisis of 2008-2009, but in Sweden
further requirements were implemented in November 2011, as stated by Finansinspektionen
(2011). This supposedly made bank loans more expensive, which would make the corporate
bond market more attractive and more frequently used. Becker and Ivashina (2014) as well as
Kaya and Wang (2016) show that the credit supply of banks does have an impact on the trade-
off between bank loans and corporate bond issuances. Regulation has a positive coefficient in
the estimation model, and the odds ratio amounts to 15.80 (seen in Panel IV below), implying
that after the implementation of Basel III in Sweden in the fourth quarter of 2011, a bond
issuance is significantly more likely. This is in line with the previous theories by Becker and
Ivashina (2014) and Kaya and Wang (2016).

Size of the firm: Usage of corporate bonds on the Swedish market has been small and, in many
sectors, limited to large firms. It was therefore expected that the size of the firms should have
an impact on if and how often a bond issuance can be executed. The coefficient of the
categorical variable size is positive, and larger for large firms than for medium-sized firms. The
odds-ratio analysis shows that compared to small firms as a base group, the likelihood of a bond
issuance is 9.55 times higher for medium-sized firms, and 32.30 times higher for large firms.
The larger the firm is, the greater is the likelihood of an issuance. This is in line with what
beforehand was expected to be true. If the size variable would not have been generated, and
total assets would instead had been included in the model, the coefficient of the continuous
variable would have shown the increased likelihood of a corporate bond issuance from an
increase of 1 million SEK on the balance sheet. It is therefore reasoned to be of more valuable
to analyse the size instead of total assets.
       Leverage ratio: Based on research by Kraus and Litzenberger (1973), all firms
continuously make choices of debt or equity. For REITs specifically, Brown and Riddiough
(2003) suggest that the leverage ratio should be a factor to take into consideration in the model.
There was evidence that firms with a higher debt to equity ratio were less likely to issue new
debt, and that bond issuers target a minimum leverage ratio corresponding to investment-grade
credit rating. Firms in the sample that have a leverage ratio below their target should thus be
more likely to issue bonds than firms with a leverage ratio at or above the target ratio. We find
it reasonable that the leverage ratio has a negative correlation with our dependent variable, bond
issuance. Leverage ratio in the model was found to have negative coefficient, and the standard
error is somewhat smaller than the coefficient. For each percentage unit increase in the leverage
ratio, the estimated probability to issue a bond in the next quarter is reduced by roughly four
percent, holding everything else constant.
       EBIT: Our own presumption prior to the regression was that profitable companies have
better access to the bond market due to the signal value of the profitability. We thus expected
the coefficient to be positive. However, EBIT was found to have a negative coefficient in the
results. This was surprising, since we believed there would be a signalling value in the
profitability that could impact the issuances of bonds. We did not have theoretical support for
the positive coefficient, which is why we have chosen to include it in the final model despite
the fact that the coefficient to us first seemed illogical. The negative coefficient might be
explained by the fact that when new projects are under development, costs are typically high,
reducing EBIT. We believe that bond issuances might occur in the middle of a development
process, when cash outflows have already started to increase. It is likely that expenses have

                                    31
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

already started to be recorded as well, but revenues from the project have not come in. This
would reduce the EBIT level. Since the standard error is relatively large in comparison to the
coefficient, it is by using this sample not possible to be sure what impact, if any, EBIT will
have. While this means that the impact of EBIT is questionable, we do not consider it a flaw
that it is included in the final estimation model, since it has a relatively small contribution.
       A partial correlation of the continuous variables included in the final model is shown in
Panel VI below. The contribution of the different continuous variables is fairly evenly

6.7. Hosmer-Lemeshow Goodness of Fit test
In the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness of fit test, the probability of Chi-squared should be as close
to one as possible. Our model produces a probability of Chi-squared of 0.4808, which is
considered good enough. This means that 48.08 percent of the estimated issuances lie on the
chi-squared curve with eight degrees of freedom. The Hosmer-Lemeshow statistic for eight
degrees of freedom should be as low as possible, 7.53 is considered good enough. The test was
performed on models including the variables that were rejected before the final model, and it
was concluded that the final model has the best fit to the chi-squared distribution. As this test
shows that the final model follows the chi-squared distribution, we draw the conclusion that it
is a good enough model describing corporate bond issuances in the tested time period, and that
the data does not violate any model assumptions. Our hypothesis, that a model based on selected
publicly available market factors and company specific factors can estimate real estate bond
issuances in the following quarter, is thereby confirmed for the firms in the sample. The
estimation model is completed, and we proceed forward to execute a prediction model.

6.8.1 Cut-off
As the model predicts a continuous value in the interval between 0 and 1, a cut-off value is
chosen where all the values above this point are interpreted as issuances, and all below or equal
to are assumed to be non-issuances. The default cut-off is at 0.5, but since there are fairly few
issuances in comparison to non-issuances in the dataset, the cut-off needs to be lower than 0.5
in order not to underestimate the number of issuances.
        If the cut-off value is set too low, almost all predictions are classified as issuances.
While only a few actual issuances are then missed by the model, there are many non-issuances
that are classified as issuances and the total correct predictions are therefore relatively few (see
Panel VIII below). If the cut-off value is high, the total correct predictions of the model are
many, but this is due to the fact that non-issuances are more common than issuances. The model
then misclassifies many of the issuances that are actually made. By just guessing that a company
will not issue a bond in the coming quarter, without using the model, one would have more than
a 50 percent chance of being correct, so setting the cut-off high is not producing a model that
is much better than guessing.

7. Discussion and critical reflections

7.1. Constructing and using the logistic regression model
While our results show that using the model for predicting corporate bond issuances is superior
to guessing, it exhibits some important flaws. Firstly, the falsely positive outcomes that are
generated, meaning that the model classifies quite a few non-issuances as issuances, might lead

                                    37
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

users of the model to believe that the real estate sector in total will issue significantly more
corporate bonds than it actually will. Therefore, if the user of the model is interested in
predicting future activity in the real estate bond market, caution needs to be taken, and the user
should be aware that the model predicts an overestimated number of bond issuances. In this
case a cut-off value higher than 0.2 could possibly be used, the classification step re-created.
Secondly, the model has only been tested for future predictions on one quarter. There is a risk
this was a particularly ‘lucky’ prediction, that does not represent the near-term future
predictions of the model. Ideally, the future predictions should have been verified using a
number of quarterly periods. This was not done since we wanted a sufficient amount of data
point to build the estimation model upon.
       The main danger of using the standard logistic regression model for a sample of panel
data is that the sample might exhibit high serial correlation, leading to an overestimation of the
variable significances in the model. We handled this by producing indicator variables for, and
clustering standard errors according to, individual companies. We furthermore re-created the
final estimation model in a panel data logistic regression setting, to control for significantly
changed coefficients and high P-values. As the coefficients in the panel data setting were similar
to the ones in the standard logistic regression, and all P-values remained significant, we drew
the conclusion that the model did not exhibit high serial correlation, and that the assumption
about independency over time held.
        As can be seen, only 799 observations remain in the final regression. This is one of the
problems with the logistic regression model. There is a lot of information that is omitted because
one of the independent variables is missing for that particular observation. The model is built
based on just over half of the observations and might thus not reflect the entire sample. It can
be discussed if the approximately 200 observations from 2008 to 2010 are enough to fit a good
model to those three years, which roughly represent a recession. The good part is that 31
companies remain. There is no company that is fully omitted, and we should be able to use the
model to describe all firms in the sample, although perhaps less precisely those that have few
data point included because they entered the market or were covered by S&P Capital IQ in a
late time period.
        There could possibly be disadvantages with using the 80 percent confidence interval for
keeping a variable in the regression. We chose 80 percent because when building a new model,
we cannot be too strict with the variables that we include. However, the coefficients of the
variables with high P-values are less certain than the ones with low P-values. This problem is
particularly obvious for the EBIT variable with a P-value of 0.17, where the standard error is

                                    38
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

relatively large compared to the coefficient. As already discussed, we cannot be sure what
impact, if any, EBIT has in the model. If a confidence level above 80 percent had been chosen,
EBIT would not have been included, and we suspect that the model would have been able to
make around the same predictions for future bond issuances. While it is interesting that EBIT
is significant for the probability of bond issuances, we note that the final model probably could
have been built without it.
        There is a chance that the model exhibits two-way causality. For some of the variables,
such as the size of the firm and the leverage ratio, there should logically be causality in two
ways. A bond issuance should increase the size of the firm, which could lead to the firm moving
from a smaller size category to a larger one. The leverage ratio often increases after a bond
issuance, if the same amount of debt did not mature at the same point in time as the issuance.
If the size is larger and leverage ratio higher post-issuance, there is an indication of the
probability of a bond issuance increasing due to the larger size, and decreasing due to the higher
leverage ratio. We do not believe that the other independent variables in the final model are as
affected by the event of a bond issuance. Since there is an effect in both directions, and we
tested a variable for recent bond issuances and found it insignificant, we do not consider the
problems arising from possible two-way causality to be particularly large.
       Furthermore, it can be discussed if the model exhibits real causality, or if simply
correlation is found. As we have theoretical support for most of the independent variables
included in the final model, we do believe that this is an indication of causality. For the EBIT
variable in particular, as it lacks support in theory, we however suspect that it might be a
question of correlation. However, as the coefficient of EBIT is small and the standard error
relatively large, we do not see any problems with incorporating it in the final model.
       The time period ranging from 2008 to 2017 has been chosen to include a close to full
business cycle. By controlling for GDP production, the aim has been to get the model to handle
the fact that recessions and booms have very different implications. An alternative could have
been to construct two different models for recession and boom, but since it could be ambiguous
exactly where the economy is at present time, this has been avoided. In the in sample time
period, the Central Bank of Sweden has used an easy money policy meaning that the
relationship between STIBOR and GDP has been reverted from its traditional form. The
traditional relationship is the one described by theories; low interest rates are used in a
recession, when GDP is low, in order to stimulate consumption, and high interest rates are used
in a boom to stimulate savings and stop the pace of inflation. In the traditional circumstances,
the correlation between GDP and STIBOR should be even higher, which ought to mean that

                                    39
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

only one of them would be necessary to build into the model, as their residual then approaches
zero. In the longer run, it is not reasonable to assume the same coefficients of STIBOR and the
GDP residual. Perhaps the GDP residual might not be needed, but STIBOR could alone capture
the entire effect of the business cycle so that GDP could be overlooked, or the opposite.
       The regulation variable is an indicator variable that was created to represent the
implementation of Basel III in Sweden in the fourth quarter of 2011, which reduced the banks’
willingness to lend out funds. Since we cannot be sure that this is the only thing that happened
in late 2011 where we see an increase in the issuances of corporate bonds, we cannot be
completely sure what the regulation variable actually captures. We strongly believe that a part
of the increases in bond issuances is due to the implementation of Basel III, but a risk with
introducing indicator variables is that they may actually capture something more than what was
believed. Consequently, the model might not work outside of the selected time frame, when
those unknown factors captured by the indicator change. Since we have support for believing
that the Basel III implementation is a large part of the indicator, we feel comfortable including
it in the model.
       By including the firm specific indicator variables, we capture the variation in bond
issuances that cannot be captured by those independent variables included in the final model.
The coefficients of indicator capture the differences between firms, while the other variables
capture the differences over time, independent of the firm. This implies that everything that is
particular for a firm, such as strategies, views on the bond market, future outlook, geographic
location within Sweden, risk appetite, or anything else that impacts the firm’s willingness to
issue bonds, is included in the model through the firm indicator variables and is constant over
time. Thus, if these firm specific strategies do not change notably over time, the model can be
used in time periods outside of the sample period. However, it is noted that the firm indicators
typically have rather large coefficients compared to base company 1, and are ranging from a
negative 0.46 to a positive 9.28, meaning that they capture a rather large part of the variation in
issuances. This is a natural consequence of the fact that we have not been able to include
variables such as the financing and refinancing need, and so their effect is captured in the
indicators. If we would have been able to include more variables in the final model, the
coefficients of the firm indicators would have been closer to zero. The model would in that case
have been more generally applicable, because we could have determined the source of more
variation. Below follows a critical discussion of our chosen variables that in some cases can
have led to an estimation bias and rejection.

                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

7.2. Factors that was not captured by specific variables in the final model
Investment opportunities: Brown and Riddiough’s (2003) research suggests that we should
investigate when investment opportunities arise for the firms, since they show evidence that the
financing is usually larger when there is an investment opportunity. Including a variable that
captures future investment opportunities would separate this financing that is driven by demand
on property from the financing that arises because the capital structure is changed, or debt
matures and is renewed. Using only publicly available financial data from the previous quarter
in order to make predictions, future investment opportunities cannot be detected or used. If the
user of the model has access to (consensus) estimates of future cash from investing or revenues,
they could attempt to time lead these variables and include them in the model in order to detect
the investment opportunities’ impact on bond issuances. Since it is easier to obtain financing
when the funds are to be used for investment opportunities, we believe that cash from
investment activities should tend to assume larger negative values following a bond issuance.
Cash from investing is in comparison to bond issuances therefore the dependent variable, hence
the time leading. Considering the scope of this paper, future estimates have not been included.
Instead, we attempted to include cash from investing activities lagged one quarter. We believed
that this could show that issuances are more frequent when projects have been started, although
we realised that the variable does not capture the financing need as well as future cash from
investment activities does. It is not likely that all firms issue bonds when a project has already
been started, but rather when the investment opportunity arises but has not yet lead to cash
outflows. Different firms have different financing strategies. Furthermore, large firms that
continuously have many new project in the loop are likely seeing a fairly constant high outflow
of cash. Therefore we were not surprised when the variable was not found to significantly
contribute to the likelihood of a bond issuance in the next quarter.
       Refinancing need: The portion of debt that expires within a year, current debt, was used
as a proxy for the refinancing need, but turned out not to be significant. Already in the partial
correlation table we could see that the contribution to the variance of issuance was minor. We
have tried lagging it several periods and it remains insignificant. Either it is not a good proxy
for the refinancing need, or the refinancing need does not reflect the issuance of bonds at all.
However, Brown and Riddiough (2003) state that it is easier to obtain financing in the form of
bonds for new investment opportunities. Perhaps it is harder to convince the market that the
financing is needed when there is no particular project to show. The refinancing need is as stated
the part of the financing need that is not driven mainly by new project, but by the maturity of
the debt of the firm. This would offer an explanation for why the current debt is not significant

                                    41
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

in the model. The current debt is still mostly bank loans, and often these can fairly easily be
renewed by the banks if the relationship is good, no covenants have been broken and the risk is
not too high, giving bank loans an advantage over bonds when it comes to renewing loans. We
however believe that it is more likely that current debt lagged one to four quarters is not a good
proxy for a particular firm’s refinancing need, since firms have very different strategies to
renew debt. Some easily enter new revolver agreements approximately a year before the current
one was supposed to expire, while others wait until the debt actually does expire before taking
up new. Also, debt that matures could be replaced by equity in an effort to strengthen the capital
structure, or simply not renewed at all. If the firm has enough cash it might not be necessary,
which is why we also included the cash variable in the model. Since many firms have a target
leverage ratio, it is however fair to believe that in many cases debt will be refinanced with either
the same or another type of debt and we see it as most likely that the different refinancing
strategies of different firms is what makes current debt insignificant.
        Liquidity: It was our perception that the liquidity could capture the ability of companies
to meet its payment obligations, which would affect the willingness of investors to invest in a
company. If the EBIT over interest ratio is low interest payment requirements are harder to
meet, meaning that the default risk becomes larger and corporate bonds supposedly become
more expensive to issue. We therefore suspected that EBIT over interest would have a positive
correlation with bond issuances, but found no evidence of the impact. As we have weak
theoretical support for the impact of this variable, we believe that liquidity in most cases does
not influence the willingness or ability to issue corporate bonds.
       Perceived riskiness of the bond issuing firms: Diamond (1984) and Boot and Thakor
(2009) show that banks have better incentives to acquire information about firms than other
investors do. Hence, as argued by Fama (1985), banks typically have access to inside
information, while most other investors typically only have access to publicly available
information. Despite the fact that both banks and bond investors are assessing the riskiness of
the firm, they thus do this on different bases and the required premium may differ. If the market
perceives a firm as riskier, based on for example the credit rating, the bond will be more
expensive to issue since the payments that compensate the investors for the risk that they take
on are larger. This affects the choice of financing, as banks with access to other information
may have a different perception of the riskiness. Bonds may thus be more or less expensive in
relation to bank loans depending on the perceived riskiness and the information asymmetry. In
the final model in this paper, we have been forced to assume that the market’s perceived
riskiness is captured by the leverage ratio and the size of the firm, since we have not been able

                                    42
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

to extract information about the credit rating of the firm over time. We believe that the credit
rating would have been a better proxy for the market’s perceived riskiness of the firm.
Alternatively, the riskiness could be divided into financial risk and operative risk. The financial
risk has been incorporated in the leverage ratio. A firm with a high leverage ratio should be
riskier and less likely to be well received on the bond market, meaning that a higher leverage
ratio ought to imply a smaller probability to issue a bond. The leverage ratio coefficient in the
final model is negative. The operative risk could potentially have been assessed by classifying
the firms into several types of businesses, but since this is an ambiguous process, it has not been
incorporated in the model. It could be argued that larger firms tend to have less operative risk,
as they are typically more diversified. A larger firm should be perceived as less risky and
increase the likelihood of a bond issuance. The coefficient of the indicator variable for large
firms was shown to be greater than the one for medium-sized firms, which was greater than the
one for small firms.
       The target leverage ratio and debt composition: Brown and Riddiough (2003) state that
bond issuers target a certain leverage ratio, and that firms with a lower leverage ratio are more
likely to issue debt in general. Since some firms have a target leverage ratio, but naturally
deviate around it or below it, there is a chance that it would have been more appropriate to
analyse how the target leverage ratio affects the probability of a bond issuance, rather than the
actual leverage ratio. The reason for including the actual leverage ratio is that it is hard to
determine how the target leverage ratio has varied over time by looking at solely publicly
available financial data. Additionally, the debt composition has not been captured in the final
model, despite Brown and Riddiough (2003) finding it to be the most significant factor
determining the financing choice of REITs. The debt composition has been considered not
easily available information, and the seniority of the currently outstanding debt of the firm was
therefore not tested.
       High yield bonds and investment grade bonds: High yield bonds are likely included in
the sample. This is not shown in data, and we have chosen to analyse high yield and investment
grade bonds in the same manner, not separating the two categories. This has been considered
valid, since market conditions should to a great extent be favourable for high yield bond
issuances at the same time as for investment grade bond issuances. De Fiore and Uhlig (2011)
state that corporate bonds should mainly be used by low risk firms, and we believe that the great
majority of the bonds in our sample are investment grade, or close to investment grade. The
backside of not separating the two categories is that they have very different characteristics;
investors view them as different types of investments used for different purposes. Fama and

                                    43
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

French (1993) describe the term premium and the default premium of corporate bonds, and state
that worsened economic conditions increase the likelihood of default, making the premiums
larger. Firms that have a high default risk and would issue high yield bonds, would by this
reasoning be less likely to issue corporate bonds in an economic downturn. De Fiore and Uhlig
(2011) further state that high risk firms should not use corporate bonds, especially in economic
recessions. Investment grade bonds, on the contrary, have been reasoned to be safer investments
than stocks, and are thus benefited by the economic uncertainties. Additionally, a firm that
issues a high yield bond may have difficulties obtaining bank financing, while an investment
grade bond probably corresponds to a firm that could raise bank financing. Access to the market
and bank loans differs, and the price differences may differ too. Whether it is a high yield bond
issuance or an investment grade bond issuance would be desirable to include in the model, but
due to the lack of accessible information, this could not be done.
       Price indicators: Another factor that is missing in the final model is the bond index, our
proxy for the price of, and demand for, bonds. When the index is high, investors would demand
less compensation for the risk, and so companies should be more willing to issue bonds. The
index was the part of the information asymmetry proxies that captured only the price of bonds,
where STIBOR was the other part acting as a component affecting both bond prices and bank
loan prices. We however found that the relationship between STIBOR and the index was fairly
constant over time, signifying that using this procedure, we were not able to identify changes
in conditions favouring bonds or bank loans, but not the other, or any indication of information
asymmetry in the sample period. This does not mean that there necessarily have not been any
changing conditions in favour of the pricing of one or the other debt financing option, but that
our proxies could not conclude it. Furthermore, since the Swedish corporate bond market is
dominated by institutional investors, we doubt that there actually is any significant information
asymmetry. Institutional investors often have a lot of knowledge about the firms they invest in,
just as banks do. The NASDAQ OMX Credit SEK index reflects a portfolio of risky
municipality bonds and corporate listed bonds trading on a market, and we cannot be sure that
an index for all listed bonds is a good proxy for the demand and price sensitivity of bonds issued
by real estate firms. Since real estate firms in general have a more extensive use of corporate
bonds than other firms, the investor perception of these bonds may be different. We conclude
that the NASDAQ OMX Credit SEK index does not imply any variance in bond issuances that
is not given by STIBOR, and therefore does not show any information asymmetry. However,
despite the fact that the reflected portfolio does not exactly correspond to the firms in our
sample, the index can on a significant level be incorporated in the model, if STIBOR is

                                    44
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

excluded. The demand on real estate bonds should therefore be in line with the demand on this
portfolio, perhaps because real estate firms represent a large part of the Swedish corporate bond
market. The alternative model includes the same variables except the regulation indicator, as
the index limits the sample period to 2011 to 2017. The limited time frame of the index is one
of the main disadvantages with using this alternative model, as it is not built on a full business
cycle. Possibly this could lead to more precise predictions in 2011 to 2017 and in years with
similar conditions, but it is unlikely that it would be a good model in an economic downturn
corresponding to the years of 2008 and 2009. We chose to proceed with the STIBOR model
because we expected it to make future predictions that hold relatively better particularly in
recessions, since the index model at present cannot be based on recessions.
       Currency factors: We note that since we have only retrieved data for corporate bond
issuances in SEK, we have disregarded the fact that a firm may in a quarter be classified as a
non-issuer of bonds, although it might have made an issuance in another currency or market.
Since we have used Swedish market data for STIBOR, GDP and the index, we have also
attempted to only capture when the market circumstances are favourable or not for Swedish
bond issuances in particular. It is plausible that the Swedish market conditions in themselves
are favourable, but in relation to the market conditions in for example the euro area or Norway,
they are not as favourable. An alternative and more detailed approach than the one taken in this
paper would have been to include variables for example the differences in prevailing interest
rates, currency exchange rates, and the differences in spreads in the different geographical
markets. Since the real estate sector mostly has national operations and the firms in our sample
have made the great majority of their issuances in SEK, it has in this paper been considered
valid to oversee the relative market conditions between different geographies.

7.3. Suggestions for future research
We suggest that the model is updated quarterly, in order to incorporate recent trends and re-
estimate the probabilities of corporate bond issuances based on a currently applicable sample.
The first quarter of 2008 has been used as a starting point in this paper, and could be so in the
future too, making the estimation sample larger. Thus, the model can become better suited to
estimate corporate bond issuances in varying business climates.
        If a user of the model has access to unofficial data from banks, it would be preferable
to include price differences between bank loans and corporate bonds. We believe that this is an
important factor when firms determine which type of financing to use, and so incorporating it

                                    45
                                                                     Bernhardsson & Lindstén

in the bond issuance model could potentially produce a more accurate model than the one
developed in this paper which has only considered publicly available data.
        It might also be possible to incorporate future market trends in the model, as they arise
and gain in importance. An example is the trend of increased usage of so-called green bonds.
Today, green bonds are gaining in popularity, and the market demand for them is increasing.
Firms within the real estate sector have been active in the issuance of green bonds, for example
Vasakronan made a green bond issuance already in 2013, as noted by Vasakronan (2017). This
trend should be followed and possibly in the near-term future included in a model corresponding
to the one built in this paper.

8. Conclusions
This study sets out to investigate whether the probability of corporate bond issuances of
Swedish real estate firms can be estimated and predicted using publicly available data. A
logistic regression model for estimating corporate bond issuances in the Swedish real estate
sector has been built using publicly available data from the first quarter of 2008 to the third
quarter of 2017. Several market factors as well as company specific factors were considered
prior to constructing the model. These have been thoroughly tested, and we find that the interest
rate STIBOR, a residual between the regression of GDP and STIBOR, an indicator variable for
the bank regulations implemented in 2011, the size of the firm, the leverage ratio, and EBIT
can on an 80 percent confidence level be incorporated in a model that estimates the probability
of corporate bond issuances. We acknowledge that there are factors with an impact on corporate
bond issuances, such as arising investment opportunities and refinancing needs of specific
firms, which we have not been able to incorporate in the model. However, we have been able
to capture a large part of the firm specific variation by using indicator variables for the Swedish
real estate firms in the sample. For the firms in the sample, the model can furthermore be used
to predict corporate bond issuances in the near-term future. For the fourth quarter of 2017 the
model correctly predicted 24 out of 31 issuances. However, the model produces some falsely
positive outcomes; it predicts issuances that never happen. Furthermore, the model is only
applicable on the 31 firms in the sample, and the conclusions are only valid on these. Therefore,
caution needs to be taken when using the model for predicting future corporate bond activity of
Swedish real estate firms, and users of the model should be aware of its flaws. However, a 90
percent accuracy in the predictions of actual issuances in the fourth quarter of 2017, indicates
that the model could be a useful tool when determining future real estate bond issuances.

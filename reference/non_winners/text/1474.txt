2. Background

The first regulation on lobbying was introduced in 1938, with the Foreign Agents Registration
Act. The legislation strived to improve lobbying disclosure but did not include restrictions of
lobbying activities. Also the Federal Regulation of Lobbying Act of 1946 was mainly a disclosure
regulation. Both acts had little effect as the unclear standards made the legislation voluntary in
practice. (Holman, 2008)

Office of Clerk or Secretary of Senate if it plans to engage in lobbying activities during any three-­‐

act clarified the definition of lobbying activities and made the political system more transparent

3. Previous literature

In research by Brinig, Holcombe and Schwartzstein (1991) the results of a stricter legislation on
lobbying are discussed. Lobbying regulations make it more difficult to lobby by increasing both
the cost of lobbying and the cost of entering the lobbying industry. As a result, the firms with the
highest ability and willingness to pay for lobbying will benefit the most from lobbying after
regulation has been introduced. However, their conclusion has later been questioned by Lowery
and Gray (1997) who argue that the restrictiveness of lobby regulations has little impact on
numbers of organizations registered to lobby.

Borisov, Goldman and Gupta (2011), investigate lobbying regulations from a different angle.
They examine whether corporate lobbying simply adds value by allowing firms to communicate
expert information to policy makers, or if it also adds value by facilitating potentially illegal quid
pro quo arrangements, where lawmakers receive private benefits in exchange for favourable
policy decisions. They use the corruption scandal involving the top Washington D.C. lobbyist
Jack Abramoff as  ǯ  ability to lobby. The results suggest that
firms experience a significant decrease in market value following the guilty plea by Mr. Abramoff
the more they had engaged in lobbying activities. In addition, they find that legislations aimed at
curbing   corrupt   lobbying   practices   significantly   reduces   abnormal   return   around   the
introduction of the legislation. Their results indicate that lobbying creates shareholder value,
and that part of this value can be attributed to corrupt lobbying practices.

4.3 Event study
4.3.1 Assumptions
The event study method is a powerful tool to assess the financial impact of changes in corporate
policy. Using this method, one can determine whether there is an "abnormal" stock price effect
associated with an unanticipated event. However, in order to be able to perform the event study
we have to make some critical assumptions as argued by McWilliams and Siegel (1997).

The first assumption we have to make is that the efficient market hypothesis holds. The
hypothesis implies that stock prices incorporate all relevant information available to market
traders. Hence, any financially relevant information that is newly revealed to investors will be
immediately incorporated into stock prices. This assumption allows us to interpret the observed
abnormal returns as a result of the event and as a reflection of the true value effect of the event.

Even though the efficient market hypothesis has been vividly criticized in recent research 14  we

assume the U.S. market to be reasonably efficient.

The second assumption we make is that the event is unanticipated. This means that the market
previously did not have any information about the event, signifying that the observed abnormal
returns only contain unanticipated information attributable to a specific event. We believe this
assumption to hold since prior to the event dates the public could not have anticipated the actual
implications of the law introduction.

The last assumption we make is that there are no biases from confounding effects from other
events. This means that no other new information is released or events occur simultaneously or

a more favourable way in order to improve their industry competitiveness and financial
situation. By excluding delisted companies, an important part of the data might be missed.

5.1.5 Endogeneity
When investigating the effects of the introduction and implementation of HLOGA we must
consider whether there is an endogeneity problem that might bias our potential results.
Wooldridge (2009) argue that such biases may be caused by measurement error, simultaneity
and omitted variables. We attempt to minimize the potential biases caused by endogeneity by
performing  a  difference-­‐in-­‐difference  estimation  with  HLOGA  regulation  representing  an
exogenous event and hence, the endogeneity problem should be reduced. Furthermore, Meyer
(1995) argue that events such as policy changes allow researches to obtain exogenous variation
in key explanatory variables which is useful for reducing concerns for omitted variables or other
threats to validity.

Moreover, the risk for reverse causality should be fairly low in our study. Reverse causality
would imply that, for example, company performance of companies with high lobbying expenses
might be the initial reason for the introduction of HLOGA. This would mean that the Act was

                                 24
introduced in the U.S. in order to improve financial performance as a result of American
companies having experienced worsened financial performance in recent years. If this would be
true, this would lead to reverse causality and bias results. However, we do not see any logical
explanation for this and we argue that the implementation of the law was first and foremost a
political matter with transparency as its prime argument.

Another problematic factor might be that lobbying is actually a proxy for political contacts in the
U.S. legislative system. Research has been made regarding the value of political connections to
corporations. Agrawal and Knoeber (2001) discovered that firms in need of political connections

tend to have outside board directors with political backgrounds. 24  Further works in the line of

research have been written by Faccio (2006), which discovers  that firms  with political
connections enjoys privileges such as low taxation, easy access to debt financing and high
market share. Albeit these findings are of importance, we believe that the amount of money
spent of lobbying is a good and more quantifiable measure to study the effects of lobbying than
political connections.

5.2.3 External factors influencing the share price
When studying market returns there is a risk that other factors specific to companies within
certain deciles of  prior lobbying  could affect the returns on the event date. If other important
news are released on or surrounding the event dates it is difficult to assume that the abnormal
returns are attributable to the events studied. This would, as a result, bias our results. Trying to
control for such confounding effects, we have performed a news search in conjunction with the
event and we have not been able to find any major news that we believe would bias our results.
In addition, we eliminated firms undergoing acquisition/reorganization or splits and stock
dividends on or surrounding the event dates.

Furthermore, in order to perform the event study we assumed that the information released on
the event dates was new information to the market and that the information was immediately
incorporated into share prices, as stated by the efficient market hypothesis. However, if some
investors had insider information or some unique knowledge regarding the introduction and
implementation of the HLOGA and its effects before the event dates, our results would not take

5.2.4 Inferences with event-­‐date uncertainty
MacKinlay (1997) demonstrate the issue of inference with event-­‐date uncertainty. When
performing an event study it is assumed that the event date can be identified with certainty.
However, in some studies this has proven to be difficult. We have done extensive research trying
to establish the correct event dates but we cannot be absolute certain that the market has not
been informed prior to the official announcement dates. In order to circumvent this problem we
have included cumulative abnormal returns  r1 days and  r3 days. However, there is a cost to
expanding the event window such as decreasing the certainty of the abnormal returns being
attributable to the event alone. Nevertheless, we argue in accordance with MacKinlay (1997)
that the costs are worth bearing rather than to take the risk of missing the event.

5.2.5 Benchmark indices
There are several approaches to computing the estimated returns used to calculate abnormal
returns  (MacKinlay,  1997).  On  the  basis  that  a  market  model  represents  a  potential
improvement over the constant mean return model, we have chosen to employ a statistical
market model in our event study. We chose to use the value-­‐weighted S&P 500 index as our
market proxy since our sample firms are listed on the S&P 500. Alternatively, a multifactor
model could have been used when approximating the normal returns. However, the gains from a
multifactor model is small given that the purpose of the test is to approximate potential
abnormal returns, rather than establishing the exact ones.

6.2.4 Consequences and conclusions
Following our reasoning in previous sections it is reasonable to assume that firms with higher
prior lobbying  expenses experienced a higher change in terms of financial measures affected by
effective tax rates after the implementation of the regulation. This would indicate that firms still
are able to reap the tax benefits, to the same extent as during previous years, even after stricter
regulations on the market. This supports the idea presented in later research by Bowie (2012),
who states that lobbying efforts leads to larger benefits in particular from beneficial taxes.
However, we are cautious to state that such results are attributable to changes caused by
HLOGA. Instead we recognize the possibility that the positive differences between firms with

                                 33
different levels of  prior lobbying  expenditures observed could merely be a result of lobbying

expenditures growing faster after January 1 st  2008 for firms in the higher lobbying group than

those in the lower. In looking at table 3, it is evident that firms in the lower deciles actually, in
percentage, increased lobbying expenditures more in 2008 than firms in the higher deciles.
However, earlier findings by Hill, Kelly and Van Ness (2009) suggest that the marginal benefit
from lobbying efforts are, in fact, absolute and not in relative terms. They report statistically
significant returns on investment of approximately $200 for every dollar spent on lobbying by
S&P 500 companies that registered as clients in Lobbying Disclosure Filings. Again looking at
table 3, it is apparent that firms in the higher deciles increased their lobbying expenditures
significantly more in absolute terms in 2008 than firms in lower deciles. However, research by
Richter, Samphantharak, and Timmons (2008) argue that an increase in lobbying expenditures
by 1% in one year, reduces the effective tax rates in the range of 0.5 to 1.6 percentage points the
following year. This complicates drawing inferences from the regression results. More in-­‐depth
qualitative research, which is beyond the scope of our thesis, has to be made in order to
distinguish how and why firms lobbied more in 2007 and 2008, as well as what financial
measures were affected. Albeit this questions our results still strengthens the assumption that
the regulation did not reduce lobbying activity and that the slow-­‐down in growth of lobbying
expenses in 2009 was predominantly an effect of the weaker global economy at the time.
Additionally, firms with zero  prior lobbying  expenses  on average registered positive lobbying
expenditures after the implementation of the law.

Our reasoning is not in line with the conclusions by Brinig, Holcomb and Schwartzstein (1991),
which stated that firms with larger capacities to invest in lobbying benefits from lobbying
regulations. Furthermore, they argue that increased costs would make it more difficult for those
with fewer resources; however, we argue this problem not to be present among our sample of
S&P 500 firms. Our findings and assumptions are more in line with research by Lowery and Gray
(1997), which discard the findings of Brinig, Holcomb and Schwartzstein (1991). However, we
cannot with certainty, solely based on quantitative results in our study, fully support their
conclusions.

negative changes in company performance for firms with high lobbying expenditures after the
introduction of the law. Furthermore, as already stated, we do not believe that the stricter
regulation leads to overall decreases in lobbying expenditures. Assuming the difference-­‐in-­‐

7. Further research

Since current research on lobbying regulation and its impact on company performance is
limited, it would be interesting to see additional research addressing the subject. At present,
countries with specific rules and regulations governing the activities of lobbyists are rare. We
believe it would be interesting to compare result from the U.S. with other countries where
comparable data is available. This would not only facilitate comparisons of different types of
lobbying activities, but also open up the possibility to perform a more extensive study with an
expanded sample. One possibility would be to look at German lobbying activities. Within the
European Union the German Bundestag is unique in that it is the only Chamber that has specific
and formal rules regarding the registration of lobbyists. The register is available to the public
and  published  annually  (Malone,  2004).  Furthermore,  the  European  Parliament  and  the
European Union have recently agreed to establish a common Transparency Register. Even
though there are several differences between lobbying in the U.S. and the EU, such as that EU
officials are not elected and hence dependent on monetary contributions to their campaigns, we
believe a comparison of the two would be of great interest.

We have chosen to study large firms listed on the S&P 500 throughout the years 2004 to 2011. It
would be interesting to see future research addressing the same issues, but using a set of firms
with different characteristics. Furthermore, the assumptions in our research are to some extent
conditional on the implied effects of the most recent financial crisis. Therefore, it would be
interesting to compare, strengthen or even challenge our results and conclusion with studies not
as directly affected by the latest financial crisis.

We have studied the effect of HLOGA on accounting measures, market value and lobbying
activities of U.S. firms. Nonetheless, the possibility exists that the lobbying regulation might have
had other effects that are not accounted for in this thesis. These effects might be of great
importance when considering the overall effects of the regulation. For example, the regulation
might have been of importance for the political system and its transparency. However, Jansson
argues that increased transparency legislation would make the system less democratic. This
issue might be interesting to for further research.

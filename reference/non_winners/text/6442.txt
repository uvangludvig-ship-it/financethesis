                    Independence in an Era of Interference:
     A Quantitative Exploration of the Relationship between Central Bank
                         Independence and Inflation

                   Herman Nilsson (25785) and Emil Tallberg (25750)

                                    Abstract

By the end of the 20th century, central bank independence (CBI) had become an entrenched
principle of monetary policymaking across many market economies worldwide. This rested
on the belief that CBI provided a credible solution to the time-inconsistency problem in
controlling inflation. However, CBI has become increasingly contested since the global
financial crisis. Rising political interference and concerns over the role of central banks in
macroprudential oversight and democratic accountability have challenged the assumed
legitimacy of CBI. These developments create a rationale for reevaluating the empirical
foundations for CBI. Much of the influential research on the relationship between CBI and
inflation relies on limited empirical methods and data predating key macroeconomic shifts,
and the overall findings are inconclusive. This paper reexamines the relationship between
CBI and inflation with updated data and an improved methodological approach. Using panel
data for 155 countries from 1963 to 2023, the relationship between CBI and inflation is
estimated through an OLS model with extensive robustness measures, complemented by an
instrumental variable specification that instruments CBI with accession to the Maastricht
Treaty. Across specifications, the findings are consistent with the logic that independence
reduces inflation. The findings strengthen the case for central bank independence as a means
of maintaining low inflation and suggest that international frameworks, such as the
Maastricht Treaty, can play a crucial role in facilitating its adoption.

1. Introduction

If the President is competent enough to have his finger on the nuclear button, he is competent
enough to control the money supply.

                                               –Economist Lester Thurow, March 1982

[T]here can be a SLOWING of the economy unless Mr. Too Late [Jerome Powell, the Chair
of the Federal Reserve], a major loser, lowers interest rates, NOW.

                                                    –President Donald Trump, April 2025

While central bank independence rose to become a cornerstone of monetary policy in many
countries during the end of the 20th century, these claims reflect a persistent tension in
contemporary monetary policy that has increased since the global financial crisis.
Policymakers are exerting growing pressure on central banks to align monetary policy with
political agendas. Christine Lagarde (2025), president of the European Central Bank, stated,
“...there is no doubt that the de facto independence of central banks is being called into
question in several parts of the world.”
       U.S. President Donald Trump has repeatedly challenged the legitimacy of the Federal
Reserve, escalating to the point of threatening to dismiss its chair, Jerome Powell. Referring
to Powell, the President stated in April 2025, “I don't think he's doing the job... If I want him
out, he'll be out real fast…” (Samuels, 2025). However, this is only the latest instance
reflecting political interference with central banks in recent years. In 2021, Turkish President
Recep Tayyip Erdoğan replaced the governor of the Turkish central bank with a party loyalist
days after the bank increased interest rates (Partington, 2021). Similarly, Hungarian Prime
Minister Viktor Orbán appointed his political ally and former finance minister as the
governor of the Hungarian central bank earlier this year following repeated pressure on the
outgoing governor to cut interest rates (Inman, 2025; Szakacs and Than, 2024).
       This development suggests that monetary policy is in an era of interference. “In this
environment, credible policy regimes become even more important for maintaining trust in
central banks,” argues Lagarde. She references Alberto Alesina and Lawrence Summers’
(1993) seminal paper “Central Bank Independence and Macroeconomic Performance: Some
Comparative Evidence,” which finds a negative relationship between CBI and inflation. Yet,
                                                                          6

the methods of inference that Alesina and Summers’ paper relies on and the events that have
played out since it was written prompts a need for revision. Against the backdrop of
interference, our study revisits the relationship between CBI and inflation to provide a more
robust assessment of its legitimacy from a price-stability perspective.
       Alesina and Summers examine 16 industrialised countries during 1955–1988. By
contrast, our study is based on panel data covering 155 countries over 1963–2023. Thus, our
time frame includes important macroeconomic events that occurred after Alesina and
Summers’ paper was written. Rather than relying on the correlational inference used by
Alesina and Summers, we provide a regression model that includes multiple robustness
measures. Expanding upon this model, we instrument CBI with accession to the Maastricht
Treaty while controlling for macroeconomic factors to improve validity. With these models,
we aim to provide a more rigorous analysis of the CBI-inflation relationship.
       We find that, although the validity of central bank independence (CBI) is often
challenged by politicians and academics alike, a robust analysis of 155 countries over eight
decades indicates that there is a negative and significant relationship between CBI and
inflation. This strengthens the case for an independent central bank as outlined in economic
theory, implying that the depoliticisation of monetary policy is more important than ever in
today’s era of interference.

1.1 Background
“Central bank independence (CBI) became a globally accepted truth of economics about 30
years ago,” write Paul Wachtel and Mario Blejer (2020, p. 1). Yet the idea had already gained
broad academic support before it became the prevailing model for monetary policy in many
countries. Describing their theory of time-inconsistency in economic policymaking, Finn
Kydland and Edward Prescott (1977) state that:
       If in each period the policy decision selected is the one which maximises the sum of
       the value of current outcomes and the discounted valuation of the end-of-period state,
       the policy selected will be consistent but not optimal… [T]he implication of our
       analysis is that policymakers should follow rules rather than have discretion (pp.
       486-487).
This notion of time-inconsistent policy makers has prompted research into the institutional
features that enable central bankers to credibly commit to low-inflation policies, as noted by
Michael Kiley and Frederic Mishkin. The most prolific such institutional feature is central
                                                                          7

bank independence (2024, p. 9). Wachtel and Blejer write that, by delegating monetary policy
to an independent body, monetary policy is shielded from political business cycles, where
politicians seek to exploit policy surprises to garner voter support, as well as inflationary
financing, whereby governments fund their expenditures by printing more money (pp. 6-7).
       However, since the Global Financial Crisis (GFC) of 2008, central bank independence
and the technocratic delegation model it relies on have increasingly come under scrutiny.
Blejer and Wachtel write that an idealised notion of central banking had emerged by the end
of the 20th century. In summary, this notion was based on an independent body that
conducted monetary policy to ensure price stability. Yet, Blejer and Wachtel write, “a narrow
view of CBI emphasised monetary policy to the virtual exclusion of other roles” (p. 11). By
focusing narrowly on price stability, central banks were unprepared for broader financial
stability dangers and had to engage in politically sensitive crisis responses with distributional
implications, challenging the notion of full independence. Blejer and Wachtel state, “[W]hile
independence continues to be protected, its golden age ended with the crisis a decade ago…
removing partially the implicit taboo involved in asking the unmentionable: perhaps central
banks should not be, nor should they aim at being, so independent after all? [emphasis
added]” (p. 2).
       The justification for raising this question lies in the extraordinary responsibilities
entrusted to the Federal Reserve and many other central banks. As Andrew Levin and
Christina Parajon Skinner (2025) observe, “in exercising its monetary duties, the Fed wields
tremendous power” (p. 1773). Yet, questions arise when such power is in the hands of an
unelected body. Levin and Skinner write that the quantitative easing program implemented
between 2020 and 2022, prompted by the COVID-19 recession, is projected to cost U.S.
taxpayers more than $1 trillion, despite the absence of any thorough external review of its
effectiveness (p. 1769). In the wake of post-GFC critiques of central bank independence, it
becomes relevant to re-examine the foundational rationale for CBI in economic theory:
namely, the notion that more independent central banks are better able to fulfill the mandate
of price stability.

1.2 Research Question and Hypothesis
Early studies linking CBI and inflation have important limitations. Alesina and Summers’
study, considered a seminal work, analyse only 16 industrialised countries over 1955–1988,
excluding developing economies and later major macroeconomic and institutional changes.
                                                                          8

Finally, their methodology does not account for confounding factors or outliers, and they base
their conclusions on correlational inference. We aim to reevaluate the relationship with more
robust methods and an expanded data-set.

Specifically, we define the research question:

       Does the negative correlation between central bank independence and inflation
       documented by Alesina and Summers (1993) hold when tested with a broader dataset,
       improved methodology, and controls for key confounding factors?

Based on our research question, we develop the following hypothesis:

       There is a statistically significant negative relationship between central bank
       independence and inflation. That is, higher levels of CBI are associated with lower
        inflation.

2. Theory and Literature Review

The literature review introduces the theoretical framework describing the link between
monetary policy and inflation. The theory section is followed by a discussion on prior
empirical work exploring the relationship between CBI and inflation.

2.1 Theoretical Framework

2.1.1 Monetary Policy in the Long Run: The Quantity Theory of Money
Central banks are crucial to discussions on inflation because of their influence over the
money supply. “Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon,” asserted
Milton Friedman (1963, p. 16). Although this claim may not fully capture the complexity of
short-run inflation dynamics, any discussion of the long-run determinants of inflation is
incomplete without the foundational framework that links the price level to the money
supply: the quantity theory of money. At the core of the theory is the equation of exchange:

                                       MtVt = PtYt

All variables are defined at time t: M is the money supply, V the velocity of money, P the
price level, and Y the real GDP. As Charles Jones (2020) explains, “the velocity of money
should be thought of as the average number of times per year that each piece of paper
currency is used in a transaction” (p. 218). The equation of exchange states that nominal
GDP, PtYt, is equal to the effective amount of money used in transactions, MtVt.

                                          Vt = 𝑉

                                        Mt = 𝑀t

Given this, rearranging the equation of exchange leads to the following identity, the quantity
theory for the price level:

This expression implies that the effective money supply determines the long-run price level
relative to long-run output. Since real GDP and the velocity of money are treated as
exogenous in the long run, changes in the money supply, implemented by the central bank,
translate directly into changes in the price level. In other words, the central bank’s control
over the money supply makes it a primary driver of long-run movements in the price level
and, by extension, inflation (pp. 218-220).

2.1.2 Monetary Policy in the Short Run: The AD-AS Framework
While the quantity theory of money provides an essential long-run perspective on the link
between money supply and inflation, it disregards important frictions, like sticky inflation,
that arise in the short run. These short-run distortions and their implications for monetary
policy can be understood through the Aggregate Demand and Supply (AD-AS) framework.
       The framework builds on three key models. First, the monetary policy (MP) curve,
which describes a positive short-run relationship between the nominal and real interest rates
under the assumption of sticky inflation in the short-run. Second, the investment-saving (IS)
curve shows how increases in the real interest rate reduce short-run output (real GDP). Third,
the Phillips curve links current inflation to expected inflation and changes in short-run output.
In this model, expectations are adaptive, meaning firms and households form inflation
expectations on the previous period’s actual inflation. This specification of the Phillips curve
follows Friedman (1968), who highlighted that it reflects a temporary tradeoff between
inflation and short-run output.
       Replacing the MP curve with a monetary policy rule and combining it with the IS
curve gives us the aggregate demand (AD) curve, which shows how interest rates set by the
central bank affect output. Specifically, when inflation exceeds the target, the central bank
raises the nominal interest rate, which lowers output below its potential and triggers a
recessionary response aimed at bringing inflation back down to the target. Relabelling the
Phillips curve as the AS curve, we get a supply-and-demand framework where monetary
policy shifts demand by adjusting interest rates based on inflation, determining short-run
output and inflation. However, if firms and households have adaptive expectations, believing
that next period’s inflation will match the current rate and setting prices accordingly, this
creates an incentive for policymakers to use discretionary policy to lower the nominal interest
rate to raise aggregate demand and boost short-run output (Jones, 2020, pp. 356-380).

2.1.3 Commitment and Credibility: Rules vs. Discretion in Monetary Policy
Going from an assumption of agents with adaptive, backwards-looking expectations to one of
rational and forward-looking agents has crucial implications for the conduct of monetary
policy. In their seminal paper “Rules Rather than Discretion: The Inconsistency of Optimal
Plans,” Kydland and Prescott (1977) state that, “discretionary policy, namely, the selection of
that decision which is best, given the current situation and a correct evaluation of the
end-of-period position, does not result in the social objective function being maximised” (p.
473). This suggests that discretionary policy is appropriate for organising policy when current
outcomes depend only on past and current policy decisions. Yet, as they explain, “this is
unlikely to be the case for dynamic economic systems,” where economic agents can
reasonably predict the way policy will be determined in the future (p. 474). In such cases, a
policymaker using discretion may appear to act optimally in the moment, but they ultimately
fail to account for the fact that firms and households adjust their behavior based on
expectations of future policy.
       Kydland and Prescott illustrate this inconsistency of seemingly optimal plans using a
two-period model. In this model, policymakers strive to maximise a social objective function
S, which depends on policy choices (π) and the decisions of private agents (x), such as firms
and households. For two periods (T = 2), policy in period two (π2) is selected to maximise

                                           S(x1, x2, π1, π2),

subject to
                                        x1 = X1(π1, π2)

and

                                        x2 = X2(x1, π1, π2)

where the functions X1 and X2 represent how private agents choose their actions based on
current and expected future policy. If agents in period one expect the policymaker to
re-optimise π2 in period two, they anticipate that the actual policy will be different from what
was initially announced. Consequently, they adjust their behavior in period one (x1) based on
that expectation. This leads to a socially suboptimal outcome where S is not maximised,
                                                                         12

because the awareness that policymakers might have incentives to renege on the initial plan
for period two undermines the credibility and effectiveness of the policy (pp. 475-476).
       Kydland and Prescott extend this logic to the realm of monetary policy, stating that,
“The conventional approach is to assume that expectations depend in some mechanical ad
hoc way upon past prices.” Yet, as they explain, this assumption of adaptive expectations is
overly simplistic: a shift in a policymaker's priorities, like placing more weight on
employment relative to inflation, can immediately influence the expectations of firms and
households (p. 478). This introduces an inflationary bias, where credibility problems lead to
higher inflation without corresponding gains in output. If the credibility of policymakers is
essential for anchoring inflation expectations, there is a clear need for an institutional
framework in monetary policy that ensures both credibility and insulation from short-term
political pressure.

2.2 The Empirical Case for CBI
In the paper “Political and Monetary Institutions and Public Financial Policies in the
Industrial Countries”, Vittorio Grilli, Donato Masciandaro, and Guido Tabellini (1991)
construct a measure of CBI based on economic and political dimensions of monetary policy.
They regress inflation on their index using data from 18 industrialised countries between
1950 and 1989. They find a significant negative relationship between CBI and inflation (pp.
367-373). A more comprehensive measure of CBI is developed by Alex Cukierman, Steven
Webb, and Bilin Neyapti (1992), who construct an index based on 16 de jure factors
associated with CBI. Using data for 72 countries over 1950–1989, they also find a significant
negative relationship between CBI and inflation. These findings are supported by Alberto
Alesina and Lawrence Summers’ (1993) landmark paper “Central Bank Independence and
Macroeconomic Performance: Some Comparative Evidence.” Alesina and Summers point to
a “near perfect negative correlation between inflation and central bank independence,” using
data for 16 industrialised countries over 1955–1988 (pp. 151-154).

2.3 Critiques and Conditional Relationships
However, this empirical support for CBI has been subject to critique. Thomas Cargill (1995)
questions the findings of Cukierman, Alesina, and Summers, arguing that:
       [T]he lack of statistical robustness suggests that the claimed statistical association
       between low inflation and formal central bank independence should not be given the
                                                                         13

       importance it has been afforded in discussions of how to structure a price stabilising
       bank (p. 169).
Subsequent studies elaborate on Cargill's skepticism about the relationship between CBI and
inflation. Adam Posen (1995) argues that the relationship between CBI and inflation depends
on the political environment. He states that, “The preferences for price stability embodied by
CBI require political support” (p. 254). Daron Acemoglu, Simon Johnson, Pablo Querubin,
and James Robinson (2008) build on the political economy argument of Posen. They suggest
that policy reforms towards increased independence are most effective in societies with
intermediate political constraints. However, when constraints are either strong or weak, the
impact of reform diminishes. In societies with strong political constraints, reforms are only
marginally effective; in societies with weak political constraints, reform toward independence
is undermined by a lack of checks on political authority (pp. 1-5).

3. Methodology

The methodology section introduces the data sources and variables, followed by a description
of the methodological approach.

3.1 Data
To test our hypothesis, we use a panel data set. The following section describes the sources of
our data and the variables included. The coverage of the variables differs and while the CBI
index spans back to 1923, data for controls are available from 1963 and onwards.
Consequently, the main regressions are based on panel data covering 155 countries between
1963 and 2023.

3.1.1 Central Bank Independence Index
The central bank independence index used is the widely recognized ‘Cukierman Index,’
created by Alex Cukierman, Steven Webb, and Bilin Neyapti (1992). They analyse and
evaluate CBI using 16 de jure variables across four broad categories: appointment, dismissal
and term of office of the central bank governor; formulation of monetary policy; objectives;
and limitations in lending to the government. Each category is evaluated independently and
weighed into an overall index ranging from zero to one, with one representing maximal
independence. The original Cukierman index covers 72 countries between the 1950s and the
1990s (Cukierman et al., 1992). Davide Romelli (2022, 2024) updates and extends the
Cukierman Index to cover the period 1923–2023, incorporating data on 155 countries across
different stages of economic development. In our study, we use Romelli’s updated version of
the Cukierman index.

3.1.2 Inflation Rate Data
The inflation data is collected from the Global Macro Database (GMD), developed by
Karsten Müller et al. (2025). The inflation variable is calculated as a yearly percentage rate
based on consumer price index (CPI) data.

3.1.3 Control Variables
We collect data for subsequent control variables from GMD. These include: real GDP growth
rate; government deficit (% of GDP); government debt (% of GDP); and real effective
exchange rate (REER). According to the AD-AS framework, economic booms increase
                                                                         15

aggregate demand and thus inflationary pressure, motivating the inclusion of real GDP
growth rate as a control. To capture the possibility that the relationship between CBI and
inflation is conditional on the business cycle, we include an interaction term between CBI
and GDP growth. Inclusion of the fiscal variables is motivated by the nature of the
government budget constraint: when tax revenues are insufficient and borrowing is limited,
the government might resort to printing more money to finance its expenditures, thereby
fueling inflation (Jones, 2020, pp. 228-229).
       We choose not to control for political conditions. Prior research (Posen, 1995;
Acemoglu et al., 2008) suggests that political conditions are fundamental to the existence and
functioning of CBI. Recent developments in countries such as Turkey and Hungary further
illustrate that CBI is heavily shaped by the political environment. Controlling for political
conditions would effectively estimate the link between CBI and inflation in a political
vacuum and misrepresent the real-world relationship that this study seeks to capture.

3.1.4 Maastricht Instrument
To address endogeneity concerns, we include a specification where CBI is instrumented with
accession to the Maastricht Treaty. Thus, we create a binary variable, MaastrichtAccession.
MaastrichtAccession takes the value of one for the year a country signed the Maastricht
Treaty, as well as subsequent years until the end of the data set. For countries that signed the
agreement and later exited, such as the United Kingdom following Brexit, we continue to
code the variable as one to reflect the assumption that the structural shift towards increased
CBI resulting from accession persists even after formal EU membership ends.

3.1.5 Post-Soviet Dummy
After removing outliers, we still observe instances of extreme inflation, particularly in
post-Soviet states during the 1990s. As Kaser (1999) explains, the Commonwealth of
Independent States (CIS) retained the Russian Ruble after the dissolution of the Soviet Union
and experienced extreme levels of inflation when Russia decontrolled prices during its market
transition (p. 24). These inflation spikes are unlikely to reflect changes in central bank
independence. To address this, we introduce a dummy variable, PostSoviet. PostSoviet takes a
value of one for Soviet states from 1989 to 1998, spanning the fall of the Berlin Wall to the
stabilisation of the inflation in the CIS countries (p. 24). To avoid excluding the entire region
                                                                         16

and introducing selection bias, we use this approach rather than relying on stricter outlier
removal.
       Some might suggest a similar adjustment for Latin American countries that also
experienced extreme inflation in the 1990s; however, Luis Ignacio Jácome and Samuel
Pienknagura (2022) argue that the subsequent reduction in inflation across the region was
largely driven by institutional reforms and increases in CBI (pp. 23-31). As this variation
relates to our independent variable, we do not introduce a dummy for this period, treating
these observations as part of the effect we are trying to estimate.

3.2 Methodological Approach
To test the hypothesis that there is a significant negative relationship between CBI and
inflation, we begin with a baseline model designed to reflect the simplified specifications
commonly used in earlier studies, like Alesina and Summers (1993). Next, we extend the
model by treating outliers, controlling for confounding factors, and including a time-lag in
CBI to reflect delays in monetary policy. To address potential endogeneity, we specify a
model using accession to Maastricht as an instrumental variable and conduct a placebo test to
evaluate the exclusion restriction of our instrument.

3.2.1 Fixed Effects and Clustered Standard Errors
Throughout specifications, we include country and year fixed effects to control for
unobserved heterogeneity. Country fixed effects control for time-invariant differences across
nations, like cultural, historical, and institutional characteristics that may influence both CBI
and inflation. Year fixed effects control for shocks that affect all countries in a given year,
such as financial crises or oil price shocks, preventing this variation from being mistakenly
attributed to CBI. By controlling for these sources of unobserved heterogeneity, fixed effects
reduce omitted variable bias, allowing us to focus on the within-country, over-time
relationship between CBI and inflation.
        In addition to fixed effects, we cluster standard errors at the country level, consistent
with the level at which CBI varies. This mitigates biases introduced by serial correlation
within countries over time, which is likely given that inflation is often considered sticky in
the short run. Furthermore, clustering addresses concerns about potential heteroskedasticity,
improving the reliability of our significance tests.

3.2.2 Baseline Model
To estimate the untreated relationship between CBI and inflation described by Alesina and
Summers (1993), we start by regressing inflation on CBI using a simple OLS without
controls or data transformations. This establishes a baseline for comparison with later
specifications, helping to understand the contribution of cumulative controls. Mathematically,
the specification is described by

                                          πit = αi + β1CBIit + εit

where πit is the annual inflation rate for country i in period t, CBIit is the central bank
independence for country i (as measured by the Cukierman index) in period t, and εit is the
idiosyncratic error term.
       To extend the comparability of the baseline model, we add fixed effects, resulting in
the following specifications: OLS with country fixed effects, OLS with year fixed effects and
OLS with two-way fixed effects (country and year) as illustrated by the following
specification:

                                        πit = μi + λt + β1CBIit + εit

Here, μi is the fixed effect for country i, and λt is the fixed effect for year t.

3.2.3 Extended Model
With a reference point established, we extend the model by treating outliers, adding
macroeconomic controls, and incorporating lag in the independent variable.
       We exclude observations with a Z-score above three, removing 0.19% of the data,
minimising bias from outliers. This removes observations unlikely to represent typical
variation without significantly reducing the sample size.
       According to the AD-AS framework, the classical dichotomy does not hold in the
short run because prices, and consequently inflation, are considered sticky. As a result,
changes in monetary policy, shaped by institutional factors like central bank independence,
may affect inflation only with a delay. To account for this delayed effect, we transform the
CBI index into a five-year backward-looking moving average.
       We incorporate the macroeconomic controls referenced in section 3.2.2 and include a
specification with the post-Soviet dummy. Mathematically, the complete model is
                                                                         18

                               πit = μi + λt + β1CBI_ma5it +β2Xit+ εit

where CBI_ma5it is the 5-year moving average of CBI and β1 is its coefficient, Xit a vector for
the controls: real GDP growth; government deficit (% of GDP); government debt (% of
GDP); real effective exchange rate (REER); interaction term between GDP growth and CBI;
and the post-Soviet dummy, and β2 is the vector of the controls coefficients.
       The extended model addresses key limitations in the baseline specification, but
concerns about potential endogeneity between CBI and inflation remain. This motivates the
use of our instrumental variable.

3.2.4 Instrumental Variable Model
Potential Confounders
Inflation could be affected by many unobservable factors that might also influence the
independence of a country's central bank. Bias might also be introduced by measurement
error in the CBI index, as it is retrospectively constructed and based only on de jure factors.

Maastricht as an Instrument
To address these issues, we regress a two-stage least squares (2SLS) model with accession to
the Maastricht Treaty as a binary dummy to instrument CBI. The Maastricht treaty was
originally signed by twelve European countries in 1992, with additional countries joining in
subsequent years. It established the European Union, as well as the European Economic and
Monetary Union (Baliño and Cottarelli, 1994, p. 290). The treaty required, and still requires,
prospective parties to meet certain economic and legislative criteria, including a guarantee of
CBI in national law (European Central Bank, 2024). The external pressure to reform the
structure and governance of central banks across countries that opted to be part of the treaty
creates a potentially exogenous source of variation in CBI.
       The first stage regression predicts the effect of accession to Maastricht on CBI. The
specification is

                      CBIit = μi + λt + γ1MaastrichtAccessionit + β2 Xit + εit

where MaastrichtAccessionit is a binary variable equal to one if a country has adopted the
Maastricht reforms by year t, and zero otherwise, and γ1 is the coefficient of
MaastrichtAccessionit. The second stage regression, estimating the effect on inflation, is
specified as

where 𝐶𝐵𝐼it is the predicted CBI value from the first stage regression, for country i in period
t. For consistency, the IV is estimated using the same set of controls and fixed effects as in
the extended model, except that the interaction term between CBI and real GDP growth is
omitted, since CBI is the dependent variable in the first-stage regression. To assess
robustness, we estimate the model both with and without the post-Soviet dummy.

Relevance
Since countries that signed the Maastricht Treaty were required to undertake legal and
institutional reforms to strengthen central bank independence, we expect a strong relationship
between treaty accession and CBI. To explore this, we initially plot the time series of CBI for
two subgroups: Maastricht countries and non-Maastricht countries. More formally, we test
the relevance of the instrument in the first stage of the 2SLS regression.

Exclusion Restriction
Using Maastricht as an instrument relies on the assumption that it affects inflation through no
other channel than CBI. That is, the exclusion restriction holds. However, some potential
concerns about the validity of this assumption should be acknowledged.
        First, there is an issue of selection bias due to the non-random selection of signatories
to the Maastricht Treaty. Accession may be correlated with country characteristics, given that
all signatories are European countries exhibiting similarities like advanced institutions and
higher economic development. However, this selection bias should not be overstated; for
instance, some less developed countries, such as Greece, signed the treaty, while some highly
developed countries, such as Norway and Switzerland, never did.
       Furthermore, there is a risk of unobserved heterogeneity, as factors affecting both
accession and inflation may bias the relationship. Beyond the compatibility of national
legislation with Maastricht, which included central bank independence, signatories had to
                                                                         20

meet four convergence criteria related to the following areas: price stability; sound public
finances (government deficit and debt); exchange-rate stability; and long-term interest rates
(European Central Bank, 2024, pp. 5-31). The latter three criteria are either directly or
indirectly controlled for in the specification. However, the price stability criterion raises the
possibility that countries undertook inflation-reducing measures independently of CBI,
potentially violating the exclusion restriction. Nevertheless, the macroeconomic controls and
fixed effects absorb a lot of potential anti-inflation policy. Furthermore, we conduct a placebo
test to evaluate the exclusion restriction by empirically examining whether there were
preexisting differences in inflation between Maastricht and non-Maastricht countries.

Placebo Test
We conduct the placebo test by creating a ‘placebo Maastricht accession’ variable, assigning
all signatories a fabricated accession year of 1960. The sample is restricted to the
pre-Maastricht period, spanning 1923 to 1991, thereby creating a dataset that includes
approximately 30 years before and after the placebo accession, mirroring the structure of the
actual IV-model (1963–2023). We re-estimate the 2SLS model using the placebo treatment,
maintaining the same set of controls for consistency. Since the placebo sample ends in 1991
and the post-Soviet dummy only becomes relevant from 1989 onward, we exclude it.
       If the placebo instrument significantly predicts inflation, the exclusion restriction
would be challenged, as it indicates that the Maastricht countries share pre-existing trends
that correlate with inflation; however, if the placebo variable does not significantly predict
inflation in the pre-Maastricht period, it supports the credibility of the instrument by
suggesting that the accession to Maastricht primarily affects inflation through CBl. A
non-significant placebo result would thus support the validity of the instrument and the
findings of the IV model.

5. Discussion
5.1 Interpretation of Results
Both the extended and IV models support the hypothesis of a significant negative relationship
between central bank independence and inflation. The findings suggest that the conclusions
from Alesina and Summers (1993) hold when tested with a broader dataset, improved
methodology, and controls for key confounding factors.
       The CBI coefficient of the baseline model is –18.14 and insignificant (Table 2,
Column 4). However, when developed into the extended model, the coefficient shifts to
–33.63 and becomes significant at the 1% level (Table 3, Column 6). The change in
magnitude and significance underscores the importance of accounting for outliers and
including relevant control variables. This is further validated by the progressive improvement
in model fit, as indicated by higher adjusted R² and lower RMSE values.
       According to the extended model (Table 3, Column 6), a full increase in CBI from 0
to 1 corresponds to a 33.63 percentage point reduction in inflation when GDP growth is zero.
A more realistic 0.2 increase in CBI is associated with a 6.73 percentage point decline. The
interaction term suggests that this effect diminishes at higher growth rates; for instance, with
2% GDP growth, the same increase in CBI yields a 5.04 point reduction in inflation.
However, the interaction term is only weakly significant, so this moderating effect should be
interpreted cautiously.
       After adding the post-Soviet control in the extended model (Table 3, Column 7), the
magnitude of the CBI coefficient decreases from –33.63 to –23.81, now significant at the 5%
level. This decrease suggests that some of the variation previously attributed to CBI was
capturing the effect of extreme inflation in the post-Soviet republics following the dissolution
of the USSR. Controlling for this historical shock likely improves the precision of the
estimate. According to this specification, a full increase in CBI from 0 to 1 is associated with
a 23.81 percentage point reduction in inflation when GDP growth is zero, and a 0.2 increase
in CBI implies a 4.76 percentage point decline in inflation.
        As in earlier specifications, the coefficient on the interaction term with GDP growth is
positive (3.60), indicating that the effect of CBI diminishes as growth rises. This pattern is
intuitive, as the political and fiscal incentives to expand the money supply are likely stronger
during economic downturns. However, the interaction term is still weakly significant.
                                                                         33

       The IV model reinforces the findings from the extended OLS specification and
provides additional support for our hypothesis. The first-stage results (Table 4, Column 2)
show that Maastricht accession is a strong and relevant instrument for central bank
independence, with a highly significant coefficient of 0.34. This implies that, on average,
accession increased the CBI substantially among signatories. In the second stage (Table 4,
Column 1), the coefficient on the instrumented CBI variable is –46.11 and highly significant,
indicating that a 0.2 increase in CBI corresponds to a 9.22 percentage point reduction in
inflation. When including the post-Soviet dummy, the coefficient decreases to –34.64,
implying a 6.93 point decline for a 0.2 increase in CBI. The larger magnitude of the IV
coefficients relative to the OLS estimates suggests that the latter may be subject to
attenuation bias caused by measurement error or omitted variables. Thus, the IV approach
may provide a more accurate estimate of the causal effect of CBI on inflation. However, as
the identified variation of the IV is limited to a subsample of the Maastricht countries, the
external validity of the IV findings may be limited. Therefore, one should be cautious to
generalise the IV results beyond the Maastricht subsample.
        The placebo test result supports the exclusion restriction and the internal validity of
the IV model, as the second-stage result is insignificant. The first stage regression shows
weak significance but does not invalidate the instrument, as this can likely be attributed to
region-specific differences in independence levels that do not carry over to an effect on
inflation. The contrast in significance and magnitude of coefficients between the IV and the
placebo results reinforces the interpretation that Maastricht’s impact on inflation operates
through changes in central bank independence and not other confounding channels.
       Overall, by addressing endogeneity concerns and incorporating a broader dataset with
improved controls, our study complements the findings of Alesina and Summers (1993) by
providing more robust evidence for a possible causal interpretation of the relationship
between inflation and CBI.

5.2 Implications
The robust relationship between CBI and inflation identified in our findings aligns with the
theoretical case for shielding monetary policy from politics, even though the legitimacy of
CBI has become increasingly contested since the GFC. Recent critiques raise valid concerns
about the role of independent central banks in macroprudential oversight and democratic
accountability, but these issues do not contradict the fundamental theoretical argument for
                                                                         34

CBI: by credibly committing to rules rather than discretion, independent central banks help
resolve the time-inconsistency problem in monetary policymaking.
       Although not the primary focus of this study, if the relationship observed were to
reflect a causal effect, countries faced with persistently high inflation could potentially regard
greater central bank independence as part of a policy response; however, we acknowledge
that there are typically significant legal and political hurdles to enacting such reforms. While
we acknowledge that political constraints play an important role, the instrumental variable
used in our analysis points to the role that international frameworks can play in overcoming
such hurdles.

5.3 Limitations
While the Cukierman Index is one of the most recognised measures of CBI, it is important to
note that it only captures de jure factors and is a proxy for true independence. Because a
central bank's legal framework does not necessarily translate precisely into de facto
autonomy, there is a possibility of measurement error.
       Furthermore, although our dataset is extensive, covering many countries over a long
time horizon, we acknowledge the potential for sample selection bias. Data availability varies
across countries and is likely correlated with country characteristics such as institutional
quality and economic development.
        Additionally, inflation is influenced by a wide range of economic and political factors,
many of which are difficult to observe or measure fully. While we control for major
confounders, some relevant variables might be omitted. Simultaneously, including too many
controls can oversaturate the model. We, therefore, aim to capture meaningful variation while
avoiding overfitting.
        Finally, we emphasise the difficulties with validating the exclusion restriction of the
instrumental variable approach. Although Maastricht provides a possibly exogenous source
of variation in CBI, there are characteristics correlated both with accession to Maastricht and
inflation that could violate the exclusion restriction. Yet, we have tried to control for these
influences by including variables that capture the economic Maastricht convergence criteria.
Furthermore, the placebo test supports the exclusion restriction, indicating that there were no
substantial preexisting differences in inflation trends between Maastricht and non-Maastricht
countries. However, it does not fully eliminate the possibility that Maastricht affects inflation
through channels other than CBI.
                                                                         35

5.4 Further Research
Examining the relationship between CBI and inflation variability would complement our
findings by clarifying whether CBI is associated with both lower average inflation and lower
inflation volatility. A negative relationship with inflation volatility would further strengthen
the case for CBI.
       Furthermore, our paper assumes that the relationship between CBI and inflation is
linear. Exploration of a non-linear relationship would be a meaningful contribution to the
literature. It is reasonable to suspect that the relationship between CBI and inflation may
exhibit diminishing returns under certain circumstances, as suggested by Acemoglu et al.
(2008). Gains in terms of credibility and inflation expectations are likely more substantial
when CBI increases for heavily politicised central banks, compared to central banks that
already enjoy high independence.
       Moreover, based on the critique of how independent central banks failed in terms of
macroprudential oversight before the GFC, investigating the relationship between
independence and financial stability could be a valuable opportunity for further research.
Exploring whether independence affects a central bank's ability to act efficiently in economic
turmoil could provide important insights into the role of central banks in the post-GFC era.

6. Concluding Remarks

Although the legitimacy of central bank independence is increasingly contested, our analysis
finds a strong, negative relationship between CBI and inflation, consistent with its theoretical
foundations. Using a larger sample than previous studies and addressing endogeneity
concerns, our study confirms and reinforces the findings of Alesina and Summers (1993). If
interpreted as causal, the results strengthen the case for insulating monetary policy from
politics, especially in today’s era of interference.

 The Alleged Value of Active Management

 A study of the alleged value added benefits from actively managed mutual funds in Sweden

   Are Swedish all-equity mutual funds actively managed and do they provide superior returns for investors?

                                                       1                      2
                            Pär Hugo-Persson and Simon Lönnvik

                                           Abstract
The activity of Swedish all-equity mutual funds have been subject to widespread debate and even
brought to court. This paper investigates the alleged value of Swedish all-equity mutual funds by
performing a holding analysis, i.e. comparing the allocation of investments against index. We find
that (1) funds have become more active since 2002; (2) fund managers tend to be less active when
investing in their domestic market compared to when investing abroad; (3) during 2013, there was a
strong relationship between activity and the amount charged by the fund managers; and (4) fund
managers do not achieve abnormal returns, in comparison to the global market index, as found in
prior research. In conclusion, the benefit that fund managers deliver to investors is a high degree of
diversification, which could be achieved by replicating the performance of an index as well. Although
a mutual fund does not have the same underlying holdings as an index, it offers an alternative way of
pursuing a diversification strategy.

Keywords: Mutual funds, Active Share, Tracking Error, Closet Indexing, Performance

1. INTRODUCTION

Investors let fund managers handle their wealth, in order to leverage both their expertise and effort
in evaluating potential investments for their mutual funds. However, there has recently been an
ongoing debate in the media, driven by journalists such as Joel Dahlberg, concerning whether or not
investors should be able to expect any added value from investing in actively managed funds, over
index tracking funds. Their claim is that many of the allegedly actively managed funds are closet
index funds (i.e. index tracking funds). Hence, these funds should not be able to charge a fee for
being ‘actively managed’ as they do not provide any stock picking services. The debate culminated in
Aktiespararna, which is an interest group for small investors, taking Swedbank, which is one of the
big Swedish banks, to court.

                                                                                                                                3
The number of mutual equity funds has increased rapidly in Sweden during the last few decades.
This paper will only investigate all-equity mutual funds which, under Swedish law, are defined as
funds with at least 85% of their assets in public equities. The debate in Sweden has been centred on
these funds because they are the most common, 55% of the total Swedish fund assets were in all-

                                                                                                                             4
equity mutual funds at the end of 2013, and it has been easy to benchmark them against an index.

Equity mutual funds are generally categorized as either passive or active. The sole goal for a passive
fund is to track a benchmark index. The fund manager’s service is to make sure that the fund is
weighted in accordance with its benchmark index. In general, the cost of investing in these types of
funds is cheaper, since fund expenses are not driven by research costs or active investment selection.

An actively managed fund is a fund that does not track its benchmark index, for which the fund
investor pays the manager to take an active position. There is a wide array of investment strategies
and the spectrum of actively managed funds consist of a broad range of stock pickers, speculators
and closet indexers. Actively managed funds are generally more expensive than passive funds, due to
the fact that managers of actively managed funds have to analyse and select investments on their
own. Despite the higher cost, there is no guarantee that the funds will yield a higher return than
passively managed funds.

Papers such as Jensen (1968) and Carhart (1997) have shown that the average fund manager is not
able to generate abnormal returns (i.e. beat the market), net of fees. Fund managers, on the other
hand, argue in fund fact sheets and prospectuses that they, in fact, are able to provide abnormal

1.1 THE LEVEL OF ACTIVITY

There are several ways of measuring the level of activity in a fund and there are different definitions
of activity. The two major definitions of activity are either when a fund yields a higher return
compared to its benchmark index or a deviation in allocation of underlying assets compared to its
benchmark index. Most of the previous research available has focused on fund performance, when
evaluating activity.

Grinhold and Kahn (1999) suggest evaluating funds by analysing the volatility of the absolute
difference between the fund return and its benchmark return. A deviation would indicate some sort
of activity since fund managers ought to attempt to outperform the index. This measure is called
tracking error. Given the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) a tracking error can be achieved in two
ways, either by having a high leverage, which is not applicable to mutual funds in Sweden, producing
a high beta or through superior skill, producing a high alpha. Managers want to maximise the
expected return whilst minimising tracking error, since a high tracking error implies a larger risk of
underperformance relative to the benchmark index, according to Cremers and Petajisto (2009).
Noteworthy is that tracking error by definition puts a large emphasize on systematic risk (i.e. risk that
is increased by industry specific investing).

Cremers and Petjisto (2009) introduced a new measure called active share. It is a measure of the
difference between the fund weights in the underlying assets and the benchmark index’s constituent
weights. Papers such as Fama (1972) and Daniel et al. (1997) have discussed the basic idea of active
share, but they never developed the measure. Intuitively, active share for an all-equity fund can take
values between zero and one, where zero indicates pure index tracking and one indicates perfect
divergence from the index. This type of activity analysis is called holding analysis. In contrast to
tracking error, active share does not capture systematic risk.

Cremers and Petajisto (2009) provides the theoretical framework for this paper. However, we have
found new aspects and developed their method further. Firstly, this paper is based on Swedish all-
equity mutual funds instead of American ones, since it is not clear that Swedish managers are

                                                                                5
behaving in the same way as American ones. Secondly, this paper is using another approach when
assigning benchmark indices. Thirdly, this paper presents some important variables that need to be
controlled for that Cremers and Petajisto (2009) have not noticed when studying what affects
activity, e.g. benchmark capitalization, number of index underlying assets and cash. Cash holdings are
important since they too are holdings which affect the fund performance. Given a typical fund, with
no derivative investments, cash holdings result in lower exposure to the market. Fourthly, we are
using a measure called selectivity, introduced by Amihud and Goyenko (2014), to study if it has an
explanatory power on active share. Finally, this paper is, to our knowledge, the first to look at
differences in active share and alpha depending on the funds’ investment region.

In this paper, as well as in Cremers and Petajisto (2009), both tracking error and active share are used
to categorise active funds. Unfortunately, all active funds are not active in the same way and there
are huge differences in trading strategies and performance amongst active funds. One can categorise
active funds into the following groups, according to Cremers and Petajisto (2009): diversified stock
pickers, concentrated stock pickers, factor timers and closet indexers.

This paper has found that most funds are either diversified stock pickers or closet indexers. In
general, actively managed funds have become more active between 2002 and 2013, since average
active share has increased from 49% 2002 to 57% 2013. The tracking error volatility has been low,
and decreasing, for the entire period. Our results indicate that the average actively managed fund is
a closet index fund. We also note that the number of actively managed all-equity mutual funds has
decreased by approximately 40% over the last two years.

One explanation for the decreasing number of funds and increasing activity level might be that the
number of pure index funds has increased and that the savings market has become more mature.
Cremers et al. (2013) are able to prove that actively managed funds active in a market with many
cheap and passive investment products are more active, i.e. have higher active shares, than actively
managed funds active on markets with less passive savings products.

1.2 FUND PERFORMANCE

This paper uses two different approaches to try to evaluate fund performance. First, standard alphas,
(i.e. fund return minus the global index return) are calculated. Second, the Carhart four-factor model,
presented by Carhart (1997), is used to see if actively managed all-equity mutual funds are able to
produce alpha. We will examine whether or not a higher alpha can be explained by a higher active
share, which one might expect, since the only way to beat the index is to deviate from it. Cremers
and Petajisto (2009), as well as Petajisto (2013), find that the funds with the best performance, i.e.
the highest Carhart alpha, are the funds with the highest active shares.

In contrast to previous literature, this paper finds that the highest alphas are not found at funds with
the highest active shares. The Carhart four-factor model suggests that the funds with the highest
alphas are to be found amongst funds with the lowest active shares. Funds with a high Carhart alpha
generally have higher tracking errors. The average fund has a slightly positive Carhart alpha net of
fees, 0.17%, per month over the entire period 2002-2013. Surprisingly, the average abnormal return
is negative 1.88% per year.

1.3 EXPLANATION OF ACTIVE SHARE AND CARHART ALPHA

In addition, we will try to explain what affects active share and alpha. Finally, we try to explain the
unexplained factor, i.e. alpha, from the Carhart four-factor model. Also the alpha results show good
explanatory power and both the regression on active share and alpha is statistically significant. The
regression results are presented in the results section, with a more in depth discussion in the analysis
section.

2.1 PREVIOUS LITERATURE ON THE LEVEL OF ACTIVITY IN MUTUAL FUNDS

Tracking error is a widely used measure of activity. Since Grinhold and Kahn (1999) introduced the
measure, there have been various different definitions of tracking error. Grinhold and Kahn (1999)
provide the most common version, i.e. the volatility of the absolute difference between the fund
return and its benchmark. Cremers and Petajisto (2009) defined tracking error as:

                                                                                                 (Equation 1)

They argue that this definition does not assume a market beta of one. Any fund beta deviating from
one will result in a tracking error using the traditional definition. Furthermore, it is beneficial that this
method allows for excluding the cash holding of the fund. However, it is important to notice that
Petajisto (2013) used the common version of the measure. He argues that it better reflects the risks
funds are exposed to as well as captures the important exposure to cash holdings.

The two most relevant papers for this study are Cremers and Petajisto (2009) and Petajisto (2013). In
the first paper, the authors introduced a new measure called active share. Papers such as Fama
(1972) and Daniel et al. (1997) have also evaluated funds by looking at their underlying assets, but
primarily by looking at returns for the underlying assets. Previously, activity has been measured on
the basis of standard deviation in returns between a fund and its benchmark, the so called tracking
error. Cremers and Petajisto (2009) argue that tracking error is a bad measure of activity since well
diversified stock pickers can have the same risk as the index, even though they have a completely
different portfolio composition.

Nevertheless, tracking error is an important measure when one tries to categorise different levels of
activity. A concentrated stock picker, for instance, can have the same active share as a diversified
stock picker even though the concentrated stock picker has a much greater risk, since he is taking
more concentrated positions. Another active strategy mentioned in Cremers and Petajisto (2009) is
factor bets. A fund with such a strategy bets on systematic factors and can generate a large tracking
error without having a large active share. Actively managed funds that are tracking their benchmark
closely usually end up with a low tracking error and a low active share, 20-60%. They are therefore
called closet indexers, see graph 1 below.

                                                                                8
In order to calculate active share, one needs both the weightings for funds and their respective
benchmark indices. With the data in place, one needs to determine how to assign benchmark indices
for the funds. Cremers and Petajisto (2009) assigns benchmark indices based on which index that give
the lowest active share for each fund. In contrast, Petajisto (2013) assigns benchmark indices based
on the funds’ pronounced benchmark indices and Daniel et al. (1997) constructs benchmark
portfolios that are matched with the characteristics of the stocks held by mutual funds. It is possible
to have a high active share and a low tracking error since a fund can have the same sector allocation
as its benchmark index, even though it does not own shares in all companies in all sectors.

Both Cremers and Petajisto (2009) and Petajisto (2013) calculate tracking error. With both tracking
error and active share, it is possible to group so called active funds into different types of active
funds (see diagram below).

                                        Graph 1

Based on these four types of actively managed funds, it is possible to draw conclusions about their
returns. Cremers and Petajisto (2009) show that funds with a high active share also have high alphas.
Also, the degree of tracking error has generally low impact on fund returns, but is rather a measure
of the risk of the fund and a good way of distinguishing different strategies amongst fund managers.

Amihud and Goyenko (2014) propose a new way of predicting fund performance. By using the
Carhart four-factor model, one can regress how much of a fund’s return is due to tracking the
benchmark index. The measure is called R-squared, which can take values between 0 and 1, where 0
indicates that none of the fund’s return can be explained by the benchmark and where 1 indicates

                                                                                9
that all the fund’s return can be explained by the benchmark. Amihud and Goyenko (2014) are able
to show that funds with high R-squared have strong negative correlation with fund alpha. It is quite
intuitive that if the benchmark has high explanatory power, alpha cannot be large, either positive or
negative.

                                                                                                 (Equation 2)

High selectivity implies that the benchmark has low explanatory power, i.e. that the fund is active.
The problem with active share is that it is difficult to measure for two reasons. First, it is difficult and
time consuming to acquire all the data on fund holdings and index weights. Second, one needs to
choose which benchmark index to compare the fund to.

Amihud and Goyenko (2014) discuss the important question of how activity ought to be defined. If
activity is defined as a fund deviating from its benchmark index, active share is a proper measure
since it compares fund holdings with index holdings. However, if activity is defined as a fund
outperforming its benchmark index, R-squared is a more proper measure since it is calculated with
any set of indices and it is therefore better at detecting funds that disguise as active even though
they are doing passive multi-index investing, i.e. tracking different indices depending on which one
performs the best.

Finally, Amihud and Goyenko (2014) study the relationship between R-squared and active share. The
authors calculate the cross-fund correlation between R-squared and active share every month. The
median correlation over the sample period is -0.45. It is expected that the correlation should be
negative. To conclude, if fund return is to a large extent explained by the market, the active share
should be low.

The discussion regarding active management is complicated due to a lack of consensus in academia
on the definition of activity. Some argue that activity ought to be evaluated solely against divergence
from an index whilst others focus on the diversification benefits to be had allocating investments
differently than the index’s allocation. Based on previous research, Jones and Wermers (2011)
conclude that even though actively managed funds on average do not outperform their benchmark
indices, they still add value. Value added when investing in actively managed funds means that an
investor can have another asset allocation and/or risk than the index, according to the authors.

                                                                               10
Jones and Wermers (2011) criticises Cremers and Petajisto (2009) on three areas: they do not control
for market capitalisation, strong manager conviction is not always a good thing and the point of
measuring tracking error, since differences in tracking error do not indicate any difference in return.
It might seem kind of strange that Petajisto (2013) did not choose to control for market
capitalisation, since it is believed to have an impact on active share. A small cap index would
normally contain more stocks than a large cap index. Small cap funds would therefore be likely to
obtain an unmerited active share due to the benchmark containing more investment opportunities.

Cremers et al. (2013) examine the relation between indexing and active management in the mutual
fund industry worldwide. There are a couple of interesting thoughts and conclusions in the paper
that are relevant for this paper. First of all, Cremers et al. (2013) try to examine what investors are
actually looking for in actively managed funds and whether an increasing number of ETFs and index
funds have put pressure on fees. The paper defines truly active funds as those with an active share
over 60%. Cremers et al. (2013) find that in more mature markets, where index funds have higher
market shares, there has been pressure on actively managed funds’ fees and that the actively
managed funds are forced to be more active, i.e. generate higher active shares, and/or generate
higher returns to maintain their fees.

2.2 PREVIOUS LITERATURE ON FUND PERFORMANCE

There has been extensive research regarding whether or not investors can produce alpha, compared
to their benchmark index. The first introduction and definition of alpha was Treynor (1961) and
Sharpe (1964) where they developed the capital asset pricing model (CAPM). The model implies that
the return on an investment can be predicted by knowing the risk-free rate, market risk premium and
the beta of the investment, where the beta is the correlation with the market. CAPM model:

                                                                                                 (Equation 3)

Returns that cannot be explained by these factors are labelled alphas. This model has been
developed further by Fama and French (1993), in their three-factor model, and Carhart (1997), in the
four-factor model. Instead of predicting alphas through the first three variables, Fama and French
(1993) controlled for market capitalisation as well as book-to-market ratios. In addition, Carhart
(1997) implemented the momentum strategy into the model. The momentum effect was first
observed by Jegadeesh and Titman (1993). The Carhart four-factor model is defined in Equation 4.

                                                                               11
                                                                                                 (Equation 4)

SMB, small minus big, times a β factor explains how much of a stock’s return is explained by the size
of the company. HML, high minus low, times β factor explains how much of that return is attributable
to a company being a value stock or a growth stock. SMB is based on market capitalisation whilst
HML is based on the book-to-market value.

Furthermore, by definition the market performance is the average performance of all assets
constituting that specific market. Consequently, there will be assets that underperform as well as
outperform the market during different time periods. Performance can be illustrated through using a
normal distribution curve centred around an alpha of zero. Carhart (1997) found that few funds were
able to generate large positive alphas and that the mean alpha is close to zero. Daniel et al. (2010)
use a different measure of alpha and find that Carhart gives the lowest alphas, consistent with
research suggesting that the four-factor model has high explanatory power (Carhart (1997)).

Lately there has been some criticism of the four-factor model. Cremers et al. (2010) find that when
regressing benchmark indices on the four-factor model, the authors end up with quite substantial
alphas, both positive and negative ones. This is important since academics usually evaluate funds
using factor models, while practitioners just subtract the index return from the fund return. Cremers
et al. (2010) finds that the problem with both the three-factor and four factor models are the SMB
and HML factors. The authors modify the factors, which significantly reduces the alphas.

On the level of mutual funds, Jensen (1968) was the first paper to evaluate the performance of
mutual funds with a risk-adjusted measure, called Jensen’s Alpha. The paper do not find evidence
that funds on average are able to outperform the market, nor that any individual fund was able to
perform significantly better than the market. The results obtained by Jensen (1968) are consistent
with those observed by Malkiel (1995), which suggests that, after looking at American all-equity
mutual funds, fund managers in general fail to provide alpha net of fees as well as exclusive of fees.

Grossman and Stiglitz (1980) argued that traders need to obtain an information advantage in order
to achieve abnormal returns. However this information will be expensive to obtain reducing the
expected abnormal return. It is possible to draw two relevant conclusions from this paper: Firstly
markets are not efficient, otherwise traders would not invest in more information. Secondly,
managers might choose not to obtain the information and thereby reduce costs and become a closet
index fund.

Moreover, Jones and Wermers (2011), summarise the findings from research on actively managed
funds. They argue that past research shows that average active funds do not generate abnormal
returns, abnormal returns being defined as:

                                                                                                 (Equation 5)

Building on the findings of Malkiel (1995), the average alpha of a large sample, without survivorship
bias, should equal to zero. Though the average active fund does not generate alpha, Jones and
Wermers (2011) sees huge differences amongst funds, depending on which strategy the fund has.
Stock pickers are generally more likely to generate alpha than those focusing on factor timing.

Carhart et al (2002) discussed the implication of survivorship bias. Bad performing funds will in the
long-run go out of business. Consequently researchers will, if not controlling for the survivorship bias,
obtain a normal distribution curve shifted to the right, centred on a positive alpha. This effect is due
to the fact that many negative alpha observations have been excluded from the sample.

Moreover, investors form beliefs about mutual funds partly by looking at historical returns. Chevalier
and Ellison (1997) show that funds experience convexity in flow performance i.e. that funds which
provide positive alphas will experience positive inflows. A high inflow or outflow might affect fund
performance, since a fund with a high net inflow has to buy more shares, even though the manager
might find all shares unattractively priced, and a fund with a high outflow has to sell shares in order
to distribute money.

Also Berk and Green (2004) develop the argument further suggesting that there are skilled managers
and that they experience fund inflows and increased compensation until they do not produce any
more alpha. Consequently one might argue that managers are effective in extracting the benefits of
their superior performance and thus investors should not expect to receive any alpha, which is
consistent with Carhart (1997). Berk and Green (2004) argue that investors are fully rational since
they invest in the funds with the best historical returns. The problem is that the greater the inflow a
fund experiences, the harder it is to generate any abnormal returns.

                                                                               13
Cohen et al. (2010) argue that fund managers should hold more concentrated portfolios, due to the
fact that they only favour some specific stocks in their portfolios, so called Best Ideas, and that these
stocks tend to outperform the market. Most managers hold many stocks in their portfolios, but only
really see the great potential in a few. The rest are just for diversification purposes. The conclusions
in Cohen et al. (2010) support the findings by Cremers and Petajisto (2009) that the funds with the
highest active shares have the highest alphas.

3.1 DESCRIPTION OF INITIAL DATA SET

3.1.1 DATA SET OBTAINED FROM FINANSINSPEKTIONEN
Finansinspektionen is the Swedish financial supervisory body, equivalent to the Securities and
Exchange Commission in the U.S. All Swedish mutual funds need to disclose their underlying assets to
Finansinspektionen. The agency publishes the underlying assets of all funds quarterly on their
homepage. The data includes observations on a quarterly basis from September 2000 to December
2013. However there are some missing quarters for which Finansinspektionen, for undisclosed
reasons, could not provide the general public with data for: March 2001, June 2001, September
2001, March 2004, June 2004, December 2004, March 2005 and June 2005.

Each cross section of data includes observations on date of disclosure, institute name,
Finansinspektionens classification number for each institute, fund name, Finansinspektionens
classification number for each fund, market value of underlying assets per fund, total value per fund,
value of each fund unit and information on leverage. The data from Finansinspektionen also include
data on each underlying asset, such as: name, ISIN, listing, country, exchange rate, price per unit,
number of units that each fund owns and total value of each underlying asset per fund.

In addition, Hans-Olof Berqvist at Finansinspektionen provided a list of all equity mutual funds. The
list is on a yearly basis, this ensuring that all potentially dead funds are included. Moreover the
segmentation enables an analysis regarding funds’ alterations in their investment status e.g. changed
from being a mutual fund to a hedge fund. The data sets mentioned above include, to our
knowledge, all funds which are under the supervision of Finansinspektionen. In total, the dataset
from Finansinspektionen consists of 625 funds.

3.1.2 DATA SET OBTAINED FROM MORNINGSTAR
Morningstar is an international company that collects data on mutual funds. Karl Marthon at
Morningstar Sweden has provided return data for all-equity mutual funds, their ISIN-codes,
Morningstar category, inception date, date, obsolescence date, geographic coverage and their
benchmark indices. The data is from January 2000 to December 2013 and includes dead funds, as
well as funds originating during the period. The data was given on a monthly basis and each fund is
separated with regard to fund name, ISIN and organization number. The total number of funds in the
Morningstar data set is 680.

3.1.3 DATA SET OBTAINED FROM BLOOMBERG
To be able to measure tracking error more precisely, data on fund returns per day were needed.
Morningstar was unfortunately not able to provide daily data, so we downloaded the data from
Bloomberg instead. The data downloaded from Bloomberg only includes all-equity mutual funds that
are used in this study. The reason for using Bloomberg data instead of Morningstar data is that the
Morningstar data set was not complete, e.g. returns for some months were missing, and we found
that the Bloomberg data had a higher degree of completeness with regards to fund returns.

3.1.4 DATA SET OBTAINED FROM KENNETH FRENCH DATABASE
Kenneth French is a prominent economist and has, amongst other things, developed the Fama
French three-factor model in collaboration with Eugene Fama. Mr. French has his own website with
data on factor loadings for the Carhart four-factor model. The data sets include year, month, risk-free
interest rates, market risk premia and factor loadings. For each region there is one specific file. We
have downloaded data on factor loadings for the global index for the period 2002-2013.

3.2 ADJUSTMENTS TO THE INITIAL DATA SETS

The intention of the adjustments made to the original data sets is to make them more applicable to
the research question. Hence we have in some cases disregarded analysing some data since it is not
directly relevant to our study. A more in depth explanation concerning our adjustments follows
below.

3.2.1 DATA SET OBTAINED FROM FINANSINSPEKTIONEN
Initially all observations not belonging to all-equity mutual funds were dropped, since all-equity

4. METHODOLOGY

This chapter discusses how our study was conducted. It is divided into one section on sample
selection and geographic limitation, index construction, definitions of activity and performance
measures, course of action and one section explaining our robustness checks.

4.1 SAMPLE SELECTION

This study is conducted on all-equity mutual funds registered with the Swedish financial supervisory
body Finansinspektionen. As mentioned in the data section, all funds not classified as all-equity
mutual funds by Finansinspektionen were dropped. The rest of the data includes actively managed
funds, passively managed funds and even some hedge funds. We have gone through the remaining
funds to ensure that they are properly classified with regard to focus region, fund type and active vs.
passive. The passively managed funds, i.e. index funds, are kept in the sample for the purpose of
pooling together benchmark indices. Unfortunately, five additional funds had to be dropped from the
selected sample due to missing information on fund returns. After all adjustments have been made,
we end up with a final dataset of 217 unique all-equity mutual funds.

Due to lack of access to index data this study is limited to funds active on a theme or geography that
does include enough funds able to be pooled together, and thus create a representative index
against which our selection of funds can be evaluated. Therefore this paper analyses funds focusing
on the following regions: Sweden, Sweden Small Cap, the Nordics, Europe, Global and the U.S. Due to
a limited number of sector focused funds, those have been excluded as well. However our index
construction method could also be an advantage since the relative position between the funds ought
to have higher explanatory power, since the pooled benchmark indices take into account
instruments, owned by funds, which might not be present in a standard benchmark index.

4.2 INDEX CONSTRUCTION

We have, when calculating active share, constructed our own indices that we have used to compare
the funds with. They are constructed as value weighted indices in conformity with any normal value
weighted index, described in Newbold (2010). In addition, when developing our method we chose to
look at equally weighted indices as well, but the pooled benchmark indices looked fairly equal for
both methods and chose to use the value weighted one.

There are both advantages and disadvantages with creating our own benchmark indices. An
advantage is that we capture all investments made by all funds, even if all investments are not made

                                                                               18
in the funds’ focus regions. A disadvantage is that the pooled benchmark indices will look differently
depending on which funds are included.

4.3 DEFINITION OF ACTIVITY AND PERFORMANCE MEASURES

In this paper we define active management as the deviation in fund holdings compared to a
benchmark index. An actively managed fund is supposed to diverge from its benchmark index, i.e.
sector or country. Normally actively managed funds charge a higher fee than passively managed ones
since the active fund manager will have to pick stocks on his own. A passively managed fund has just
one objective: to follow its benchmark index, i.e. sector or country, and the fund manager’s only task
is to track the index.

In the beginning of this paper both tracking error and active share were introduced as the two
important measures of activity used this paper. With the help of these measures we can classify
funds into categories that will show how active they are. Tracking error is calculated as a six month
average and active share is calculated quarterly. Below is the definition of tracking error:

index whilst a high active share implies that a fund is deviating from its index.

In the regressions of this paper we use a measure called selectivity, presented in the literature part,
to test if that measure has any explanatory power on active share and alpha. Selectivity is defined as
one minus the explanatory power, R-squared, of the Carhart four-factor model. In other words,
selectivity is the unexplained factor of the four-factor model.

To be able to compare the performance of different funds it is necessary to use some sort of
performance measure. The standard way of evaluating a fund is to calculate the alpha of the fund.
There are several methods to compute the alphas, but for the purpose of this paper we have chosen
to use the Carhart four-factor model. The Carhart model is more sophisticated than other, similar
models since it takes more variables into account and research has shown that the model has a
higher explanatory power than other models.

4.4 COURSE OF ACTION - ACTIVITY

The intention of this paper is to investigate whether or not active funds add value, in terms of
diverging from their benchmark indices, and whether or not they are able to generate superior
returns for their investors, defined as Carhart alpha and abnormal return. Data from March 2002 to
December 2013 is examined on a quarterly basis starting by calculating each fund’s weight in its
different assets, both with and without cash. Before merging the FI quarterly files all observations
that are not needed for the study are dropped, except for index funds.

The next step is to bring forward the benchmark indices. It has unfortunately been almost impossible
to get hold of index weightings on a quarterly basis. To be able to compare the funds, we have
pooled together all funds active in the same region to get benchmark indices. We sum up all
positions for all funds in all assets per region, later we sum up total net asset values per region and
finally we divide the sum of each asset by the total net asset values per region to get the index
weights. This method is a fairly good approximation of an index. For the purpose of this pooling, the
index funds are kept in the sample and included in the pooled indices. This paper is using a value
weighted approach, but the difference between value weighted and equally weighted pooled indices
is negligible. All underlying assets in the funds are dropped, after merging, to get as small a data set
as possible.

After pooling together six benchmark indices, the index returns are calculated. By using index returns
from the pooled indices and the fund returns from Bloomberg and Morningstar, it is possible to
calculate tracking error. Tracking error is calculated on a six month rolling basis. Six month averages

                                                                               21
are the common way of calculating tracking error, but it has one implication. For funds established
during the research period, there will be a lag of two quarters before tracking error is captured. The
implication is that our results include more observations on active share than on tracking error. We
have tried to correct for this by including returns six months prior to the Finansinspektionen available
data. However, there will still be a gap since some funds have an inception date after 2002.

In this stage, active share is calculated. The selected regions are classified with a number and pooled
benchmark indices are assigned to each fund based on the region number. First, active share is
calculated for each fund and for each holding. Second, we investigate how much of the index that is
represented in the fund. A fund should thus gain active share if it doesn’t invest in underlying assets
that are represented in the index or if the fund’s holding in an asset diverges from the index holding.
Thereafter we sum up all active shares per fund and quarter and divide the sum by two.

Using active share and tracking error, the actively managed funds are divided into the following
subgroups: closet indexers, diversified stock pickers, concentrated stock pickers and factor timers.
Intuitively, a fund that has an active share of less than 50% is not very active, while a fund with an
active share in excess of 60% is defined as active. It is hard to put a threshold on tracking error, since
previous research has found quite small deviations in tracking error between funds. Furthermore, we
have looked at the differences in active share depending on focus region of investment.

After categorising all funds into subgroups, it is time to see which variables have an impact on active
share. To do this, we run a regression on active share. The regression is outlined in equation number
nine (9).

4.5 COURSE OF ACTION – PERFORMANCE

The second mission of this paper is to see whether or not the activity level has any impact on the
fund returns and if actively managed funds are able to generate alphas. This paper uses two
measures to evaluate fund performance: the Carhart four-factor model and abnormal returns,
defined in equation 8 and 5 respectively. We start by regressing fund excess returns, defined as fund
return minus the risk free interest rate, on the Carhart four-factor model to see if there are any
unexplained returns. The Carhart model is run on excess returns on a monthly basis and we regress
all funds on the factor loadings of the global market, since we do not have factor loadings for all
regions.

After generating the Carhart alpha, we split the funds into quintiles (5x5 matrix) based on active
share and tracking error to see whether the most active funds generate a higher alpha than the more
passive active funds. We also look at differences in alpha between the different investment regions.

                                                                               22
To be able to observe if Carhart alpha and abnormal returns generate different results, we present
results for abnormal returns by region and year as well.

The next step is to regress the alpha, generated by the Carhart four-factor model on active share to
see if active share is positively correlated with the Carhart alpha. Cremers and Petajisto (2009), as
well as Petajisto (2013), do not control for cash or benchmark capitalisation, which we think might
have a great impact on fund returns. Take, for instance, the financial crisis in 2008-2009: can the
actively managed funds generate any alpha or are they, in fact, beating the market since they are
holding a lot of cash? Benchmark capitalisation might have a quite substantial impact on active share
since small-cap indices normally include a lot more companies than large-cap ones.

4.6 ROBUSTNESS OF RESULTS

We have undertaken a series of robustness validation techniques in order to validate our results. This
has been done by firstly dropping data and running regressions on different time periods. It is not
possible to divide our research period into several long time periods due to the already short time
span since it would have a large effect on the significance level of the results obtained. We have
therefore dropped smaller time periods in order to validate the results. Secondly, we have in going
through our regressions chosen to add and drop variables in order to ensure that signs and
magnitude of betas do not change when dropping or adding new dependent variables. The variables
chosen for this part of the robustness checks are the ones that we find correlate the most with each
other. Furthermore, we have undertaken tests for homoskedacity, autocorrelation as well as
multicollinearity in order to ascertain that out results are valid.

6. ANALYSIS

In this section, the results presented in the previous section will be discussed and analysed into
greater detail. For the avoidance of doubt, all Carhart alphas are calculated on average monthly
bases.

6.1 THE LEVEL OF ACTIVITY

In contrast to newspapers and magazines, which argue that actively managed funds in reality are
passive, our research finds that Swedish actively managed all-equity mutual funds are becoming
more active, in terms of diverging from their benchmark indices. In the results section we presented
findings that the mean active share for all funds in our study has increased steadily between 2002
and 2013. All focus regions, except Sweden Small Cap, experience increased active shares.

One explanation for increased activity might be that closet indexers have become index funds or
simply been ousted from the market. In graph 3 we are able to show that the number of index funds
in Sweden has increased rapidly between 2002 and 2013. Our results are consistent with the results
obtained by Cremers et al. (2013), i.e. that active share tends to increase when the competition from
pure index products, e.g. exchange traded funds and index funds, increase.

The mean active share for all funds over the entire period is lower in our study than in Cremers and
Petajisto (2009). We believe that this is caused by our method of constructing benchmark indices,
which might be a better measure than just assigning already defined indices, since funds in general
do not just invest in instruments found in their pronounced benchmark indices. Another potential
explanation, with support from Cremers et al. (2013), is that the Swedish savings market is more
underdeveloped than its American counterpart. The lower degree of competition might lead to less
active funds. Both in this study and in Cremers and Petajisto (2009) the highest active share values
are close to 100%. We have looked closer into this and found that the funds with the highest active
shares are funds that are in liquidation and, for that reason, have few underlying assets left in their
portfolios.

Both Cremers and Petajisto (2009) and Petajisto (2013) argue that funds with an active share below
60% are closet indexers and the theoretical minimum active share that an active manager can have is
50%. Based on the previous discussion, only funds focusing on Europe, Sweden small cap, Global and
the U.S. have been active. Funds focusing on Sweden (in general) and the Nordics have on average
been closet indexers, which imply that those funds have not added any value, in terms of diverging
from their benchmark indices, to their investors. As noted earlier, the standard deviation in active

                                                                               34
share amongst funds and regions is high and the reader shall not draw too big conclusions of the
average results presented.

An interesting observation is that the number of funds has decreased rapidly lately. The fund
industry has for some years experienced increased regulation from financial authorities. The burden
has grown after the financial crisis and after the Madoff scandal was revealed. We are not able to
prove whether or not this is the motivating cause and further research is required.

6.2 PERFORMANCE

In the results section we present results for two different measures of performance that give quite
different results. The results obtained on Carhart alpha suggest that funds within the first, i.e. lowest,
active share quintile are those with the highest alpha. We are quite surprised that the highest alphas
can be found among funds with that are the least active. One explanation, probably the most likely,
why the results turn out as day do is that the t-statistics for table 6 are very low, on average 0.55 (see
table 11). There are in turn a number of reasons to the weak significance. First, our dataset of funds
is fairly small and we are looking at a short time frame, eleven years. Daniel et al. (1997) criticises a
paper that examines about 300 funds over a period of ten years for having a too small dataset and
horizon. Also note that in our regression to obtain Carhart alphas we find that the momentum
variable is negative, which should not be the case. Prior research, Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) and
Carhart (1997), suggests that pursuing a momentum strategy will earn an abnormal return.

Another potential explanation might be that the funds generating the highest active shares are those
that are in liquidation. This implies that there is a big turnover in the highest, most active, active
share quintile. The results obtained on alpha, both Carhart alpha and abnormal returns, are not
consistent with the ones obtained by Cremers and Petajisto (2009). We think that this is caused by
the facts presented above.

We want to remind the reader of the criticism from Cremers et al. (2010), where they criticise the
four-factor model since it is possible for benchmark indices to have substantial, both positive and
negative, alpha This is a likely reason why abnormal returns are substantially lower than Carhart
alphas in this paper.

Our results regarding the relation between activity and performance are to some extent consistent
with the results obtained by Cohen et al. (2010). A fund manager that takes active positions in stocks
that he really believes in, and follow the market with the rest of the money, should be able to
outperform a manager that is just tracking is benchmark or taking large active positions just for the
sake of being “active”.

                                                                               35
One thing that distinguishes this paper from other, similar papers is that we study which focus
regions the funds have to be able to see if there are any differences in performance and activity. As
presented in the results section, there are big differences in fund performance depending on which
region the fund is focused on. The fact that Swedish small cap funds on average have higher alphas is
not surprising. Grossman and Stiglitz (1980) argued that managers need an information advantage to
generate abnormal returns and it is not too farfetched to argue that Swedish small cap fund
managers in general should be able to have more information on small companies in their proximity
than Swedish managers focusing on global companies can obtain.

Finally, since all performance measures are compared against a global index, the results are not too
relevant for all regions. The results on Carhart alpha on global funds, net of fees, are consistent with
the results obtained in Carhart (1997), i.e. mutual funds in general are not able to generate positive
alpha, net of fees. However, when it comes to small cap stocks it is reasonable to think that the alpha
would decrease if one would use factor loadings for a small cap index, since academics have been

                                                                                               6
able to prove that small cap stocks on average outperform large cap stocks .

6.3 EXPLANATION OF ACTIVITY

Cremers and Petajisto (2009) had a very low explanatory power in their attempt to explain active
share. This paper presents a somewhat different regression setup that generates higher explanatory
power. The new variables added in this paper are selectivity, benchmark capitalization and the
number of underlying instruments in the benchmark index. Selectivity turns out to have a high
explanatory power on active share, as suggested by Amihud and Goyenko (2014).

Another thing worth mentioning is that tracking error lacks statistical significance in our regression,
whilst it is significant in related papers. The reason that tracking error has a low explanatory power is
hardly surprising for a number of reasons. First, by looking at graph 4 one can see that there seem to
be no relationship between active share and tracking error. Second, our dataset excludes funds that
might have a somewhat higher tracking error, i.e. sector focused funds, which might also give other
results in a regression on activity.

We are also able to show that funds beating the index for the past 36 month are more inclined to
take larger active positions. The results obtained for past 12 month abnormal returns are not
statistically significant while the results for 36 month abnormal returns are. The results obtained
show a positive correlation between the abnormal returns (for the past 36 months) and active share
are consistent with previous research by Cremers and Petajisto (2009). The results suggest that

managers need to have a quite long track record of good performance behind them before they dare
to deviate from their benchmark indices.

In addition, we find evidence that a higher management fee entails a higher active share, see table 8.
The results are relevant and statistically significant, but due to lack of data we have not been able to
analyse the effects over the entire time period. However, when running the regression on active
share presented in table 7 with management fee, the statistical significance is still adequate.
Consequently, the fund investor pays the manager to diverge from the benchmark index. The more
the investor pay, the more active will the manager be. Our results are coherent with previous
research by Cremers and Petajisto (2009).

Finally, some comments about the impact of the total number of instruments in each index. This
paper finds a positive correlation between the number of instruments in benchmark indices and
active shares. These results are new and have not been tested in previous papers on the topic of
active share (Cremers and Petajisto (2009) and Petajisto (2013)). The results are significant at z=2.26
and by looking at the coefficient we can see that for one more company in the benchmark index, the
average active share of funds will increase by 0.01%. By looking at table 4 and table 14, the results
are quite intuitive. The highest average active share is found in the region with most underlying
assets to choose from.

6.4 EXPLANATION OF ALPHA

The funds with the highest active shares are not those that produce the highest alpha. Therefore it is
not surprising that active share has a negative correlation with alpha, even if the negative effect is
very modest. Cremers and Petajisto (2009), as well as Petajisto (2013), find that active share is
positively correlated with alpha. Note that this is consistent with the results obtained in our
regression on active share, table 7, where selectivity is negatively correlated with active share.

The fact that selectivity is positively correlated with alpha is hardly a surprise, since selectivity is

                   2
defined as 1-R , where R-squared is the explanatory power of the Carhart four-factor model. The
reason for a positive correlation between selectivity and alpha is due to an overall positive Carhart
alpha for our sample. Tracking error is also positively correlated with alpha, which is not the case in
related articles.

Change in weights, i.e. how often a fund completely shift instruments, is negatively correlated with
alpha and statistically significant (z=-2.14). The results are consistent with most of the previous
research (Carhart (1997) and Cremers and Petajisto (2009)). Wermers (2000) studies more in detail

                                                                               37
fund performance and expense ratios. The paper finds evidence that funds with a higher turnover on
average generate higher returns before fees, but not net of fees.

Finally, the regression on alpha in this paper generates a higher explanatory power than in Cremers
and Petajisto (2009). There are some potential explanations for this divergence. First, we have
included selectivity in the regression which, by definition, should have a high explanatory power on
alpha. Second, we have included both the number of underlying instruments in the benchmark index
and the benchmark index capitalisation in the regression. Both variables are highly significant and
might therefore also be one explanation to an increased explanatory power.

7. ROBUSTNESS CHECKS

As mentioned in the method section, we are running different robustness checks to ensure that our
results are valid. The correlation matrix, table 15 and table 16, in appendix, implicates that there is a
correlation problem in the data set. Consequently there is a need for evaluating this problem further.

In table 15, we present a correlation test for the variables used in our regression on active share. We
observe high correlations between the return variables quarterly fund return, i12, i36, r12 and r36.
This is quite expected since, for instance, the past twelve month return will naturally have a large
effect on the past 36 month return. Also, by construction quarterly fund return, r12 and r36 will take
exactly the same value for the first three months of the time period. Consequently this will have a
large effect on the correlation between them. Similar correlations between the return variables can
be observed in table 16.

Furthermore, we observe a high degree of correlation between variables such as age/selectivity and
the pooled index market capitalization/number of index instruments. Consequently, there is a need
for testing the sample for collinearity. When running the collinearity test on the regression of active
share, we obtain a mean VIF-score of 1.51. The mean VIF-score obtained indicates that the
independent variables do not correlate to a large extent. The collinearity test on the regression of
Carhart alpha gives a mean VIF-score of 1.57. Thus we can reject the possibility of having a
collinearity problem in the data set. Also we test the data for heteroskedacity and autocorrelation
and conclude that they do not occur to a worrying degree in the sample.

Finally, we have tested the regressions by removing independent variables to see if the coefficients
change dramatically. The results obtained are satisfactory, however we can observe that when
removing variables that are correlated to a higher degree, the coefficient of the variable left are
affected more compared to when removing a variable with less correlation.

8. CONCLUSION

The purpose of this study has been to investigate whether Swedish all-equity mutual funds add any
value in terms of (1) diverging from their benchmark indices and (2) generating abnormal returns.

Our results indicate that all-equity funds have become better at adding value, in terms of deviating
from their benchmark indices over the period 2002 to 2013. The funds do provide alphas under the
Carhart four-factor model but simultaneously do not provide abnormal returns. However, the funds
do provide an increasing amount of diversification throughout the time period. Consequently, we
conclude that funds have become more active in terms of deviating from index holdings. Additionally
we find a strong relationship between active share and management fees during 2013. Thus our
paper indicates that investors do not pay fund managers for beating the market but rather to provide
an alternative way of diversification, instead of buying the index itself. Noteworthy is that we in our
study compare fund performance with the global market index.

In contrast to what could be expected, we find that the active share of funds vary over regions.
Swedish funds are less active when investing in their domestic market and most active when
investing in a global portfolio, despite controlling for both the number of funds in each respective
index and the market capitalization of the index. To conclude, we find that there is a significant
relationship between the activity of funds and the management fees charged. However, it is
important to notice that this analysis is only conducted on funds during 2013, since we do not have
historical fees available.

It would be relevant for future research to conduct an analysis before fees were introduced, in order
to study if fund managers did produce alphas, as prior research indicates, and investigate to what
extent Swedish fund managers absorb that alpha, without distributing it to investors. Moreover, it
would be interesting to investigate how fees affect the level of activity in funds over time.
Unfortunately, historical fees were not available to us but if we had them available we would like to
complement our study with this information. Unfortunately Finansinspektionen was not able to
provide us with all the requested data. We believe that, given a larger data set over a longer time
period, it would be possible to detect more long-term trends in active share. It would be interesting
to see for how long the above mentioned trend has been developing.

The Effect of Increasing House Prices on Household Debt in Sweden

Abstract:

        By using data on house prices and household debt during the years 2010-2016 the paper
        aims to determine the effect of increasing house prices  on housing debt and debt-to-
        income across Swedish municipalities. Via an IV approach we find a significant causal
        relationship between house prices and household debt. Dividing the populations based
       on   net   income   and   population   size   respectively,   indicate   that   municipality
        characteristics dictate the relationship between rising house prices and borrowing
        behavior among households, and that the magnitude of the effect varies significantly
        within the country. We find a stronger tendency to increase debt to income ratio among
       households  in both lower income municipalities and in municipalities with less
        population.

Keywords:
Household debt, Housing debt, Mortgages, House prices, Debt ratio, Instrumental variable

                              I.  Introduction
Rising house prices have been a major topic for policymakers and researchers in recent years.
Whilst increasing house prices are arguably beneficial for existing homeowners, the aggregate
impact on the wider economy remains less clear.

The aftermath of the 2008 financial crisis sparked a discussion regarding the potential dangers
of rapidly increasing house prices. The area of concern in our analysis is the impact of increased
house prices on household debt. Households in the US doubled their outstanding  debt from the
years 2002-2007. Increasing house prices, stemming from an increase in availability of
mortgage credit, may have impacted leverage for already existing homeowners, allowing them
to borrow via the home equity channel and push household debt to  levels ultimately resulting
in the global financial crisis. (Mian & Sufi, 2011)

Swedish households have increased their leverage in the form of debt over disposable income
in recent decades (Rima.A Turk, 2015). Likewise, the total outstanding housing debt among
households has increased. At the same time, house prices have also been on the rise, potentially
being an underlying factor of the increased debt levels. On a global stage, the leverage among
Swedish households consistently ranks among the top, along with some of its nordic peers
(Trading Economics, 2021). The country also ranks high in the share of variable rate mortgages
issued (Damien Puy & Kimiaki Shinozaki, 2022),   increasing households' risk exposure to
rising interest rates. In an economic climate where inflation requires policy rate hikes, the
interest rate exposure among households will in large part dictate their ability to cope with
changes in the economic climate.

Our paper aims to study the impact of increasing house prices on household leverage and
housing debt, by analyzing house price development, housing debt and debt to income ratios
in Swedish municipalities from 2010 to 2016. We also investigate whether the relationship
between housing debt and house prices varies between municipalities with different per capita
income levels and population size. Our empirical analysis is largely based on a unique data set
completed by the credit reference agency UC on the request by the Swedish Central bank,
published in 2017. Apart from that report, household debt on a municipality level has not been
measured regularly in Sweden since the abandonment of the wealth  tax in 2007 . The novel
dataset  provides  an  extraordinary  opportunity  to  observe  the  debt  development  on  a
municipality level during  the majority of the 2010´s.

Our empirical analysis shows a significant impact from house prices on household housing debt
and debt to income. We find that a one percent increase in house prices results in a 0.5%
increase in housing debt and a 0.24% increase in household leverage on an aggregate level
across all municipalities. We also find evidence of different magnitudes in the relationship
between prices and debt depending on municipality characteristics. Average net income in the
municipality, as well  as population size, appears to affect the households borrowing behavior
in regards to house prices.

                                     2
                      II.  Theoretical background

Many papers have previously investigated the relationship between house prices and household
debt. Mian and Sufi  (2011) investigate the effect of rising house prices on household behavior
in an American setting in the years 2002-2006, leading up to the financial crisis. They
emphasize how rising house prices led to increased home equity-based borrowing for already
existing mortgage takers, and thus increased household leverage, ultimately setting the scene
for the economic downturn. They find that American households during the period increased
their debt by 25 cents per dollar increase in home value, and that  a  one standard deviation
increase in house prices results  in a quarter standard deviation increase in the debt  to-income
ratio among households. Additionally, they report a stronger tendency to borrow via the equity
based borrowing channel among households with borrowing constraints.

Foote, Lowenstein, Paul,  and  Willen (2016) argue against the results of Mian and Sufi (2011),
concluding that the housing debt increase in the beginning of the 2000’s occurred throughout
the income distribution. They disregard the notion of housing debt being relocated to low
income individuals, and show that the debt-to-income levels remained equal throughout the
income distribution. They find that the accumulation of debt stemmed not from eased
borrowing constraints but rather from expectations  regarding increasing house prices.

Rima. A Turk (2015) studies the relationship between the housing market and the household
debt in Sweden. Unlike Mian and Sufi (2010), Turk makes use of a three equation model in his
analysis. The paper aims to determine the driving factors of debt and prices, by observing both
short-  and long-term dynamics in the housing market, and finds that the increase in home value
is the main driver of the increase in household debt levels.

An important aspect to consider is how debt and house prices may be determined and affected
jointly as a result of an omitted variable. Growth in expected income may be such a variable,
resulting in an increase in borrowing as well as increased house prices (Muellbauer & Murphy,
1997). Mian and Sufi (2011) address the endogeneity  problem by use of an instrumental
variable (IV) in their model, namely, housing supply elasticity provided by Saiz (2010). The
usage of housing supply elasticity as an instrument in examining the relationship between
house prices and consumption or debt has been criticized. Mainly, the exogeneity of housing
supply elasticity with regards to unobserved factors determining household consumption and
borrowing has been questioned. Furthermore, housing supply elasticity is often only measured
for specific areas, and for specific points in time, making usage of the instrument difficult for
longer time periods (Graham & Makridis, 2023).

Our primary aim is to attempt a replication of the aforementioned paper by Mian & Sufi (2011)
but applying the question to Sweden. The change of setting to Sweden in particular is deemed
meaningful as the country’s households have behaved similarly to households in the US in the
years prior to the financial crisis. That is, house prices have increased at a high rate in Sweden
during recent decades, whilst household debt has also increased. Therefore, in this thesis we
expand the literature regarding the relationship between household debt and house prices by
investigating the relationship in a new period and geographical setting, using a novel dataset.
Additionally, we investigate the  relationship  on a municipality level, as we deem it likely that
effects differ depending on municipality characteristics. Figures 1 through 7 display how house
prices,   housing   debt,   debt-to-income   ratio   and   net   income   municipality   level   differ
geographically across  municipalities.

Debt, house price and income developments in Sweden

Debt to disposable income has seen a steady increase during the last two decades, in line with
Nordic peers (Figure 8).  Compared to the OECD average however, households have increased
their leverage at a faster rate. Debt to total assets, on the other hand, has seen a decrease in the
same time period (Rima A. Turk, 2015), indicating that the rising debt comes at the backdrop
of increasing assets. The Swedish housing tax was removed in 2008, and with the tax deductible
of 30% on mortgage payments, amortization is disincentivized. The absence of amortization
requirements in the period also resulted in households not paying back their debt (Hull, 2017),
resulting in an accumulation of household debt.

In the period subject to our analysis debt over disposable income has continued its increase,
with a total growth rate of around 3,7% during 2010-2016, whilst the housing debt debt has
seen a greater increase, of around 31%. Both debt-to-income and housing debt have increased
less than house prices (Figure 9 and 10). The lesser growth in debt over disposable income
likely reflects an increase in net income (Figure 11). Figure 12 and 13 displays strong increases
in net income both in relation to house prices and housing debt. Figure 14 indicate that net
income have increased differently within municipalities depending on their mean income over
the period, and figure 16 show substantially different housing debt to net income ratios across
municipalities with different population sizes.

                      III.  Data and Summary Statistics

    The dataset used in our analysis consists of detailed information on the level of Sweden’s 290
    municipalities during the years 2010-2016. Mortgage data for house owners is provided by
    Sveriges Riksbank. The analysis is based on all house owning households, regardless of house
   ownership form. Moreover, a mortgage or housing debt is defined as a loan with a house as
    security. The definition thus includes both loans taken to purchase houses and any additional
    loans taken backed by a house as security (equity extraction or borrowing via the home equity
   based borrowing channel). Data on house prices has been provided by Svensk Mäklarstatistik
   and provides prices on two types of houses: houses and vacation homes. We deem that adding
   the two categories together fits the Riksbank’s definition of houses. The house price data
    includes the number of houses sold along with the mean sale price in each municipality during
    the period. Furthermore, the  data on house sales is limited to houses sold by a realtor and
    includes 355,661 house sales in total. Data from Statistics Sweden on mean net income, mean
    age, population, the foreign-born population share, and the share of the population with at least
    a  three-year university education is also used in the empirical analysis, as well as data from the
   Swedish Employment Agency on unemployment rates (all on a municipality and year level).
   All data expressed in monetary terms has been inflation adjusted to 2010 price levels in
    accordance with Statistics Sweden’s consumer price index. In total our final dataset contains
    information regarding each variable in 2030 municipality-time combinations. We remove one
    municipality due to incomplete data, leaving us with observations from 289 municipalities. We
    consider the number of observations (2023) to be sufficient for the purpose of our analysis.

Data Limitations

Much of the data used to execute our analysis is thorough and readily available through the
aforementioned sources. However, the data obtained from these sources is not on an individual
level. Instead, the information on all parameters is given as the average value for each
municipality and year. This limitation does not impede the possibility of conducting an
empirical analysis based on the data, however it does somewhat restrict our ability to replicate
the study by Mian & Sufi (2011). In having data on a sample of individuals, their study could
investigate developments in specific individuals' home debt and debt-to-income  ratio, whilst
controlling for factors such as the individuals credit score, age, and income. This conceivably
allows for more precision in the effects they estimate in their study than looking at
developments   in   municipalities   containing   average   values   for   thousands   of   different
individuals.

As stated, the data on household debt provided by Sveriges Riksbank is unique and an important
contributing factor to our analysis. However, the data does limit our analysis to a certain extent.
Since it is the only available source on household debt in Sweden during the last two decades,
it only allows us to analyze the period of 2010-2016, leaving out recent years which have seen
a shift in trends with regard  to both house prices and interest rate policy, as well as the years of
the Global Financial Crisis. Additionally, as the data does not categorize debt further than total
housing debt for house owning households, we are unable to distinguish if the share of the
increased housing debt in Sweden mainly stems from mortgages for new house purchases or
from equity extraction during the period.

Moreover, for the purpose of this study we were not able to reconstruct an instrument like the
one provided by  Saiz (2010) and used by Mian & Sufi (2011) for application in a Swedish
setting. This was due to data limitations. For that reason, we have opted to use municipality
population as an instrumental variable in our model.

                         IV.  Methodology

To find the causal effect of house prices on mortgage debt and the debt-to-income ratio, our
study uses an instrumental variable approach comparable to that of Mian & Sufi (2011). As
previously mentioned however, we have chosen to use the population of municipalities as our
instrumental variable. The chosen instrument needs to meet two strict conditions to be
considered valid: relevance and exogeneity. We perform a relevance test of the instrumental
variable with the null-hypothesis and hypothesis:

                                        H0: 𝜋1  = 0

                                       H1: 𝜋1  ≠  0

                                     9
To determine whether the instrument is relevant we run the following first-stage regression to
determine if the instrument coefficient is significantly different from zero:

     HPit  = 𝜋0  + 𝜋1Pit  + 𝜋 2NIit  + 𝜋  3mait  + 𝜋 4FBit  + 𝜋 5Uit  + 𝜋 6Eit  + ai +  at  + 𝜀it      (1)

HPit  is defined as the mean house price in municipality  i  and year  t,  NIit  is the mean net income,
mait  is the mean age,  FBit  is the level of the foreign-born population,  Uit  is the level of
unemployment  and  Eit  is the population level with  at least three years of university education.
The municipality population is the instrumental variable denoted Pit. The municipality and time
fixed effects are denoted  ai  and  at  respectively. 𝜀it  is the error term. All variables denoted in
capital letters are logarithmic.

Testing the exogeneity of our instrument cannot be done since it is not possible to test the
correlation between a single instrumental variable and unobservable factors in the error term.
Nevertheless,  we argue  the instrument can be considered exogenous given the circumstances.
Firstly, the population of a given municipality and its change in population over time is likely
to affect the demand for housing in that municipality. By  extension, the housing demand will
likely affect house prices in the municipality which in turn affects the level of debt house
owners take on in their purchase. However, we argue that the households amount of debt is
correlated to their municipality’s population only through the population levels effect on house
prices. We deem it unlikely that population levels are directly related to unobserved factors
affecting households mortgage levels.

Furthermore,  we  include  a number of  control  variables  that reduce the risk of the instrument
being correlated with omitted variables. For instance, the mean net income in each municipality
could be correlated with both house owners amount of debt and how populated the municipality
is.   Additionally, the model includes fixed effects that control for municipality-  and time-
varying effects which have the potential of being correlated with both the instrumental variable
and the dependent variable, further improving the likelihood that the exogeneity condition is
met.

By  means of the first-stage regression in (1), the predicted value of house prices,  𝐻𝑃% it, is
obtained on which we regress the mortgage level,  HDit:

    HDit =  β0  +  β1𝐻𝑃% it +  β2NIit + β3mait +  β4FBit +  β5Uit +  β6Eit +  ai + at + 𝜀it          (2)

Likewise, to find the effect of the predicted value of house prices on the debt-to-income ratio,
DIit, the following regression is performed:

    DIit =  β0  +  β1𝐻𝑃% it  +  β2NIit  + β3mait  +  β4FBit  +  β5Uit  +  β6Eit  + ai  + at  + 𝜀it           (3)

Assuming the assumptions hold and that the coefficients  β1  of the predicted value in
regressions (2) and (3) are significant, the regressions show a causal effect of house prices on
house mortgage levels and on houseowners total debt-to-income ratio, as well as the
magnitude of this effect.

                                     10
Clustering

After conducting a Breush-Pagan test, heteroskedasticity was indicated within the error terms.
To account for correlations and heteroskedasticity of standard errors, we make use of robust
standard errors.  The standard errors of our observations are clustered at the municipality level,
in accordance with the study of Mian and Sufi (2011). Clustering standard errors contributes to
mitigating the risk of biased standard errors which could affect the results of  the analysis. By
clustering the observations at the municipality level, more consistent standard errors are
obtained, which may enable more reliable inference from our model.

Multicollinearity testing

To measure and estimate multicollinearity in the model we perform a VIF-test. Moderate
correlation between the variables of the model may be expected, however a high level of
multicollinearity in the model requires evaluation and possibly exclusion of one or more of the
chosen variables. Conducted VIF  test does not indicate high multicollinearity among the
independent variables (appendix A).

Logarithmic transformation

To increase the performance of our model all variables except for the mean population age,
mait, are transformed logarithmically.  This is done to address several possible concerns. Firstly,
the relationship between the dependent and independent variables in our data are not suited for
linear approximations when using absolute values from the observations, as observed in figures
16 through 19. Logarithmic transformation of the variables may improve the approximation of
a linear relationship in the model. Furthermore, by using logarithmic variables the variance of
residuals can be reduced, which to an extent reduces heteroskedasticity of the residuals.
Additionally, the influence of extreme values in the regressions are lessened when transformed
logarithmically. Moreover, the coefficient estimates of the analysis can be interpreted as the
percentage change in the dependent variable for a  one percent increase in the independent
variables.

Robustness testing

In order to robustness test the validity of our results we run the same regression analysis on a
manipulated dataset. In order to ensure that the effect is not isolated, or significantly magnified,
by the major metropolitan areas, the three biggest municipalities in terms of population
(Stockholm, Gothenburg and Malmö) are removed from the dataset. The results differ
marginally and indicate that the effect is not inflated by the  major metropolitan areas.   See
appendix B.

With the total increase in house prices, we calculate the total increase in debt attributable to
increasing house prices. Given the total growth in house prices in the sample during the period

increasing house prices, holding all else equal. Likewise, the increase in house prices
corresponds to an increase in the debt-to-income ratio of 7.7% during the period, or 210% of
the real increase, if all else, including  net  income, is held constant.

                                            18
                           VI.  Discussion

As shown in our results,  an  increase  in house price has a significant effect on the level of
housing debt and the debt-to-income ratio among house owning households in Sweden, in line
with academic consensus. However, the magnitude of these effects is interesting when
considering their economic implications. As previously mentioned, both house prices and
household debt increased considerably in Sweden during the period of 2010-2016. Our findings
suggest that 52.9% of the increase in housing debt is attributable to the growth in house prices
during the period, keeping other parameters constant (Table 4). This result is in line with that
of Turk (2015), in which the growth in home values was the main driver of the increase in
household debt. Still, this suggests that a large portion of the growth  in housing debt during the
period has been caused due to other factors. For instance, net income per capita is one
parameter  showing  a strong correlation with household housing debt,  and its increase over the
period is  likely  an important determinant for  the growth in housing debt.

The data on household debt includes the mean housing debt for all house owning households
during the period. This means that the effect of house prices on housing debt estimated in our
results is an estimate of the effect on  both mortgages taken on during the period and housing
debt already accumulated prior to 2010. Hence, the growth in households’ housing debt during
the period of 31% (Table 2) consists of mortgages taken for new purchases of houses and new
mortgages taken with already owned houses as security, so called equity extraction. Because
of the lack of distinction in our debt data between mortgages taken for purchases of houses and
equity extraction, it is not possible to accurately assess their respective share of  the housing
debt growth during the period. In this regard our analysis differs from that of Mian & Sufi
(2011) in which changes in individuals’ housing debts and house purchases were observed,
making it possible to differentiate equity extraction and mortgages  taken  with  the purpose of
purchasing new homes. Although it is not possible to draw conclusions on home equity-based
borrowing for already existing mortgage takers, given our results it is still possible that the
growth in housing debt is to a large extent due to an increase in home equity-based borrowing.
It could also be the case that equity extraction is more sensitive to house price increases than
the estimate for the total accumulation of housing debt.

With our division of municipalities during  the period into five groups based on their mean
annual net income per capita, the results suggest that the estimated effect of house prices on
household borrowing varies significantly between income categories (Table 5). This points to
the fact that municipality characteristics affect the relationship between house prices and
household debt. One noticeable finding is that a 1% increase in house prices in municipalities
within the highest income category on average only corresponds to a 0.039% increase in
housing debt. In contrast, the corresponding relationship estimate in the mid municipality
income category is over ten times greater at 0.417%. This discrepancy suggests that with higher
average incomes, house prices become a less important determinant in the amount of housing
debt taken on by households. Instead, it could arguably appear as if higher incomes may allow
households to take on more debt almost irrespective of house prices, due to already relaxed
borrowing constraints. The low estimate of 0.177  for household in the lowest income quantile,
amounting to less than half the estimated coefficient in the mid income category, could possibly
be explained by a reversed position in terms of borrowing constraints. Lower income takers
arguably have a tighter  borrowing constraint. If that is the case, equity extraction could be

                                    19
limited. The effect of increasing house prices on household debt for the lowest income category
could hence be limited by the households' borrowing constraints, regardless of the increase in
house prices.

Table 7 further emphasizes the finding that municipality characteristics have an influence on
the effect of house prices on household debt. In grouping municipalities in accordance with
their population levels it is evident that the  estimated relationships vary significantly between
the five groups. One noteworthy result is that a 1% increase in house prices in municipalities
with the highest number of inhabitants on average corresponds to a 0.786% increase in housing
debt. This estimate is 1.8 times greater than the second largest estimate of 0.431 (Category 2)
and 3.8 times greater than the smallest estimated effect of 0.206 (Category 3). Although the
underlying reasons for this discrepancy cannot be obtained based on our result, the significantly
larger causal relationship between house prices and household debt in the most populous
municipalities could stem from the high  house  prices in largely populated areas. Figures 1 and
2 display a visual representation of this, showing that house prices are higher in areas known
to have higher populations. Figures 3 and 4 show that households in the most populated areas
have substantially higher housing debt to net income ratio. This implies that households in well
populated areas borrow heavily to afford their houses. If prices increase, households must
borrow additional funds to purchase their homes since prices rise at a faster rate than net
incomes (Figure 14). According to our data, less populated municipalities have a lower mean
housing debt to income ratio (Figure 15). Increases in house prices in those municipalities may
perhaps not require households to borrow additional funds to purchase their homes. The large
discrepancy in housing  debt  to  income between the most populated urban areas, such as
Stockholm and Gothenburg, compared to the rest of the country, may serve as an explanation
in why the coefficient estimate in column 5 stands out (Table 7).

The effect of house prices on the debt-to-income ratio is also uneven across  the municipality
income categories, unlike the findings of Foote. et.al which considered an American setting
during the financial crisis. From the results in Table 6 we find that house prices have the greatest
effect on the mean household debt-to-income ratio in municipalities with the lowest average
net income levels during the period. This finding is in line with that of Mian and Sufi (2011),
who argue that there was uneven effect in home equity borrowing during the financial crisis
between low-  and  high-income individuals, concluding that households with borrowing
constraints were the most inclined to make use of equity extraction when credit availability
increased. Furthermore, the results suggest that the effect of house prices on household
leverage is  smaller the higher the mean income of a municipality is. One possible explanation
for this finding could be that  home purchasing in  low-income municipalities  results  in
households taking on more debt relative to their income than households in municipalities with
higher average income. While this explanation is perhaps consistent with our findings, it does
not clarify the results obtained regarding income categories 4 and 5 in Table 6. Those estimates
suggest that an increase in house prices has a significant negative effect on the household debt
to-income ratio keeping all other variables constant. Regarding these results one should note
the assumption of a constant level of net income across municipalities during the period.
Therefore, a plausible contributing reason behind the coefficient estimates of category 4 and 5
is that net incomes have increased to a larger degree in those municipalities during the period
than in other municipalities. This is again supported by Figure 14, which plots the growth in
mean net income  across the  net income distribution.  According to Dieckelmann & Metzler

                                    20
(2022), households with the lowest incomes are also probable to be the most financially
vulnerable in times of economic hardship. The estimates in Table 6 suggest a higher inclination
among households in lower income municipalities to increase their leverage as house prices
rise. Consequently, such exposure may constitute a substantial risk in the event of economic
shocks. Furthermore, if the portion of mortgages with a floating rate is high among lower
income groups, the risks could be induced further.

Our non-uniform results continue when observing the effect of house prices on the household
debt-to-income ratio when grouping municipalities according to their populations. The results
in Table  8  show that households in the least populous municipalities on average experience an
increase in their debt-to-income ratio of 0.912 percent per 1% increase in house prices. The
estimated coefficient is more than two times greater than for municipalities in category 2 which
have the second largest estimated coefficient of 0.412.  The  results suggests  that the effect of
house prices on household leverage is smaller the higher the population is in a municipality.
However, our results indicate that the municipalities with the lowest populations are noticeably
more inclined to increase their leverage when house prices rise  compared to  municipalities in
the lowest part of the income distribution. This finding implies that municipalities with low
populations may be at risk of considerably increasing their leverage in times of substantially
increasing house prices.

                       VII.  Further Research

The housing market and its effect on household debt levels and leverage is an economically
important matter  which ought to be subject to substantial examination. Given the large
indebtedness of Swedish households, the country may pose higher risks in the event of shocks
to the housing market or interest rates. For future research within the field, it is our
understanding that a larger and more precise dataset is required. With a dataset on debt where
a distinction is made between housing debt taken on for the purpose of new house purchases
and equity extraction, an estimation of house prices’ specifically on equity extraction can be
determined. Additionally, our analysis is conducted on the effects of house prices on household
debt for homeowners, due to data limitations, but large parts of the Swedish population lives
in condominiums. Consequently, further research should also investigate how condominium
prices may affect household debt levels and leverage in order to obtain a better understanding
of the field as a whole.  Perhaps most important for future research though, is performing an
analysis based on individual level data. This would allow for more precise estimation of the
effects on household debt and debt-to-income ratio, making it possible to control for how
certain factors affect individuals, rather than grouping them to the municipality they reside in.
As there are other factors influencing household debt than house prices, it would also be
interesting to identify such variables and measure the magnitude of their effects. Additionally,
investigating how such parameters affect various segments of the population differently may
provide further understanding of the driving causes identified, as well as a notion of potential
policy measures that should be taken to mitigate risks in the event of shocks to the housing
market or interest rates. We found population to be a suitable instrument for the purpose of our
analysis, however future research could make use of additional or other instrumental variables
altogether, for which exogeneity is less likely to be doubted.  Finally, a thorough investigation
                                    21
into borrowing constraints in the Swedish housing market would provide further insights into
why the housing debt among low-income takers are relatively insensitive to increasing house
prices.

                          VIII.  Conclusion

Our analysis provides evidence of a significant causal relationship between house prices and
household debt. The unique data provided by the Swedish Central Bank allows us to estimate
the magnitude of the effect of house prices on household debt on an aggregate level across all
municipalities in Sweden during the period. We find that the majority of the housing debt
increase stems from rising house prices, in line with the findings of Turk (2015), with house
prices also significantly increasing the mean leverage across households.

We also find that municipality characteristics such as population size and average net income
per capita result in a non-uniform effect of house prices on housing debt across municipalities.
We find that dividing the municipalities into groups based on net income results in differing
coefficient estimates within the group. This is also the case when performing our regressions
on municipalities grouped based on population size. We find a stronger effect of house prices
on the debt-to-income increase for households in lower income municipalities, which is in line
with the findings of Mian and Sufi (2011), and for households lower in the population
distribution. The large discrepancy found across municipalities indicates that certain parts of
Sweden may be more at risk during economic  downturns, and that households in certain
municipalities are more inclined than others to increase their leverage considerably in Sweden
during the years 2010-2016.

In order to quantify the risks associated with the increased leverage for households in lower
income municipalities and in municipalities with smaller populations, we argue that individual
household specific data on debt is required. The lack of public data on household debt across
Sweden makes research difficult, marginalizing the possibility for academia to observe and
analyze the levels of household debt in a country where variable rates and household leverage
are notoriously high.

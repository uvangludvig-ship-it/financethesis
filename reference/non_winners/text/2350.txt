      Analysts’ Forecast Accuracy in the United States:
          Optimistic and Pessimistic Bold Forecasts

  A Study on Bold Sell-side Analysts’ Forecast Accuracy between 1987-2012
   and the Impact of Regulation Fair Disclosure and The Global Settlement

                                                1                  2
                        Adrian Brun     Carl Oscar Nyh

Abstract:

This thesis examines earnings per share forecasts for U.S. companies, issued by sell-side analysts, within
the time frame 1987-2012. We classify forecasts as herding or bold (non-herding), before dividing the latter
into optimistic or pessimistic. We find that (i) bold last revision forecasts are more accurate than herding;
(ii) optimistic bold last revision forecasts are more accurate than pessimistic; (iii) the implementation of
Regulation Fair Disclosure, in 2000, had an uncertain effect on the accuracy of optimistic bold last revision
forecasts, compared to pessimistic; and (iv) the relative accuracy of optimistic bold last revision forecasts
has increased since the Global Settlement, in 2003. In conclusion, investors may find a more accurate
consensus by restricting it to optimistic bold forecast revisions, since such revisions appear to incorporate
more private information than pessimistic or herding forecasts.

Keywords: Earnings per share forecasts, Sell-side analysts, Bold, Herding, Optimism, Regulation Fair
Disclosure, the Global Settlement

1. Introduction

In 2012, sell-side analysts (financial analysts who are employed by brokerage firms) issued
nearly 200,000 one-year earnings per share forecasts for U.S. companies. The mean or median of
all available forecasts, for each company, is usually referred to as the consensus forecast.
Consensus forecasts are often seen as proxies for market expectations, and stock prices are
affected by whether companies beat, meet, or fail to beat the consensus (American Association of
Individual Investors, 2014).
     Consensus forecasts can supplement investors’ own research, by allowing them to gauge
market expectations. The fact that consensus expectations affect stock prices suggests that
investors do, indeed, listen to analysts’ joint belief.

     Investors’ reliance on consensus forecasts has led researchers to seek a more informative
consensus than one that is based on all forecasts. Clement and Tse (2005) find that a consensus
based solely on bold (both above the consensus and above the analyst’s previous forecast, or
below both) forecast revisions is, in fact, more accurate. Parallel research by Chen and Jiang
(2006) shows that optimists give a greater weight to their private information and a lower weight
to public information, compared to pessimists. The two research findings motivate further
investigation as to whether a consensus that is based on optimistic bold forecasts is more accurate
than the ordinary consensus, which is based on all forecasts. To the best of our knowledge, no
previous study has addressed this issue, and the purpose of this thesis is to fill this gap.

     We also address two recent changes that have affected sell-side analysts. The Securities and
Exchange Commission (SEC) implemented Regulation Fair Disclosure in August 2000, and it
reached a settlement in December 2002 that is known as the Global Settlement. The settlement
came shortly after two self-imposed regulations by the industry (NASD Rule 2711 and NYSE
Rule 472).

     Since the adoption of Regulation Fair Disclosure, corporate managers are forbidden to
disclose private information to stock analysts without making the information public. Prior
research suggests that the regulation was effective at reducing analysts’ access to private
information from company management, thereby reducing the conflict of interest that arises when
analysts want to please corporate managers for access to such information (e.g. Gintschel and
Markov (2004) and Mohanram and Sunder (2006)).

                                       1
     The early 2000s stock market crash raised further concern about bias in analyst research.
This led to two pieces of self-imposed regulation (Rule 2711 and Rule 472) in an attempt to
reduce the conflict of interest that career concerns cause (Kadan et al. (2009)). Later on, the SEC
announced that it had reached a settlement with ten of the largest U.S. investment banks,
addressing the conflict of interest between stock analysts and investment banking. The
settlement, known as the Global Analyst Research Settlement (the Global Settlement), required
the banks to pay 1.4 billion USD in total, while also forcing them to comply with new restrictions
on their future practices (e.g. a required physical separation of research and investment banking
departments). These restrictions are closely related to the two aforementioned rules.

     In summary, this thesis attempts to re-affirm the earlier finding that bold forecasts are more
accurate than herding. Second, it compares the accuracy of optimistic and pessimistic bold
forecasts. Finally, it analyzes the impact of Regulation Fair Disclosure and the Global Settlement.
    We use data from I/B/E/S and CRSP for the time period 1987-2012 and (i) employ the
method of Clement and Tse (2005), who examine the association between last revision one-year
earnings per share forecast accuracy and whether a forecast is bold or herding, while controlling
for analyst and forecast characteristics – e.g. experience and the number of days since the last
forecast for the same company and fiscal year by any analyst; (ii) develop Clement and Tse’s
method by classifying bold last forecast revisions as optimistic or pessimistic, depending on if
they are above (optimistic) or below (pessimistic) both the announcement consensus and their
previous forecast; and (iii) divide our sample into three time periods: 1987-1999, 2000-2002, and
2003-2012, based on the implementation of Regulation Fair Disclosure in 2000 and the Global
Settlement in December 2002.

     We re-affirm the finding of Clement and Tse, which suggests that a consensus based solely
on bold forecast revisions is more accurate, for our entire time frame (1987-2012) and its three
periods. The explanation for this finding is, most likely, that bold forecasts reflect private
information to a higher extent, as Clement and Tse reason.

     Second, we find that, among bold forecasts, those that are optimistic are more accurate than
pessimistic forecasts. The higher accuracy of optimistic bold forecasts can be explained by Chen
and Jiang’s (2006) finding that optimistic forecasts tend to be based on a greater weight of private

                                       2
information. This implies that restricting the consensus to optimistic bold forecasts renders it
even more accurate than just restricting it to bold forecasts.

     Third, our finding is not clear-cut with regards to the effect of Regulation Fair Disclosure.
We find that optimistic bold forecast revisions have become less accurate after the Regulation,
compared to those that are pessimistic. However, the finding appears to be affected by imperfect
multicollinearity. Furthermore, the finding is only significant at the 5% level, and there is a lack
of support from the descriptive statistics. The uncertainty in our finding reflects Mohanram and
Sunder’s (2006) discovery that while analysts receive less private information from corporate
managements after Regulation Fair Disclosure, analysts compensate by looking harder for private
information; the net effect is uncertain.

     Lastly, we find that optimistic bold forecast revisions have become relatively more accurate
after the Global Settlement. The increase in accuracy can be explained by a decrease in the
relative number of optimistic forecasts that are driven by career concerns, resulting in an
increased share of bold optimistic forecasts that are based on private information.
     In conclusion, while Clement and Tse’s findings “[…] suggest that consensus forecasts that
are restricted to bold forecasts may be more accurate than consensus forecasts that weight all
forecasts equally”, we can further nuance their statement: Our findings suggest that an investor
could find an even more accurate consensus by restricting the consensus to only optimistic bold
forecasts, especially after the Global Settlement.
     This thesis has the following structure: Section 2 features prior literature and our hypotheses.
Section 3 presents our dataset. Section 4 explains how we construct our variables and regressions.
Section 5 presents the results, both descriptive statistics and regressions. Section 6 concludes the
thesis by discussing the implications of our findings, and provides suggestions for future
research. All tables are found in the Appendix.

2. Prior literature

2.1 Herding behavior and boldness
Herding behavior is similar to conformity, a tendency that was first studied by social
psychologists (e.g. Jenness (1932) and Asch (1951)). Scharfstein and Stein (1990) were among
the first to examine herding behavior in a financial context. They develop a model that shows
how managers might ignore their own expectations and rather mimic the investment decisions of
other managers, in order to protect their reputation in the event of an inferior investment decision,
as the blame is then shared among the herders.
     Trueman (1994) looks at herding behavior among financial analysts and finds that analysts
might ignore private information and issue earnings forecasts that are close to other analysts’
prior forecasts to “[…] favorably affect investors’ assessment of the analyst’s forecasting ability”.
    Clement and Tse (2005) – whose study provides the theoretical basis for this thesis – further
examine the differences between herding and non-herding (referred to as bold) accuracy of one-
year earnings per share forecasts. They analyze sell-side analysts’ last forecast revisions per
company and fiscal year (firm-year). To classify forecasts as herding or bold, Clement and Tse
use Gleason and Lee’s (2003) earlier method (although Gleason and Lee refer to herding
forecasts as low-information and bold forecasts as high-information). According to this
classification, last forecast revisions are bold if they are both above an analyst’s previous forecast
and above the analyst consensus or if they are both below an analyst’s previous forecast and
below the analyst consensus. All other last forecast revisions are classified as herding.
     Clement and Tse run a regression with scaled absolute forecast error (forecast compared to
actual earnings per share) as the dependent variable and boldness as an independent dummy
variable. They control for other factors, that previous studies have shown affect forecast
accuracy, namely: general experience (Hong, Kubik, and Solomon (2000)), firm-specific
experience (Brown (2001)), forecast horizon (Mikhail, Walther, and Willis (1999)), employer
size, number of companies followed, number of industries followed (Clement (1999)), previous
accuracy (Sinha, Brown and Das (1997)), forecast frequency (Jacob, Lys, and Neale (1999)), and
days elapsed (Clement and Tse (2003)).
     Using data from 1989-1998, Clement and Tse (2005) “[…] find that bold forecasts are more
accurate than herding forecasts. This suggests that bold forecasts impound more private
information about upcoming earnings than do herding forecasts, and, therefore, consensus

                                       4
earnings forecasts that are based on bold forecasts may be more accurate than consensus
estimates based on all forecasts, whether bold or herding.”
     We expect to re-affirm their finding for a longer time span, 1987-2012 (hypothesis one).
Prior literature on optimism and pessimism is presented next, before we hypothesize on whether
optimistic bold forecasts are more accurate than pessimistic bold forecasts, or vice versa.

2.2 Optimism and pessimism
Easterwood and Nutt (1999) find evidence of systematic optimism in that “[…] analysts
underreact to negative information, but overreact to positive information”. In a later study, Hong
and Kubik (2003) provide an explanation for the occurrence of systematic optimism: career
concerns. They find that “Controlling for accuracy, analysts who are optimistic relative to the
consensus are more likely to experience favorable job separations [becoming employed by a
brokerage firm with a higher status than the analyst’s previous employer].” Hong and Kubik
write that their “[…] finding is most likely due to investment bankers and stockbrokers at
brokerage houses wanting analysts to promote stocks so as to generate underwriting business and
trading commissions.”
    Ke and Yu (2006) find that – before recent regulatory changes, which are explained in the
next subsection – analysts who follow an optimistic/pessimistic pattern (optimistic forecasts early
on in the fiscal year, followed by pessimistic forecasts closer to the earnings announcement)
“[…] produce more accurate earnings forecasts and are less likely to be fired by their employers.”
They conclude: “These results are consistent with the hypothesis that analysts use biased earnings
forecasts [optimistic forecasts, in this case] to curry favor with [gain support from] firm
management in order to obtain better access to management’s private information.”
    Libby et al. (2008) conduct an experiment on analysts and find evidence of an optimistic-
pessimistic pattern, even after recent regulatory changes. Furthermore, they find that the analysts
are aware of the pattern and that the analysts believe the pattern improves their relationships with
investment clients and management. Their findings thus support those of Hong and Kubik (2003)
and Ke and Yu (2006).
     Chen and Jiang (2006) find that analysts who issue more favorable forecasts (optimistic)
relative to the consensus weigh their private information higher and their public information
lower than analysts who are less favorable (pessimistic) than the consensus. Their result can be
explained by prior literature: Pessimistic analysts risk worsening their relationships with

                                       5
corporate managers, thereby receiving less private information (Ke and Yu (2006)). Moreover,
pessimistic analysts are less likely to receive positive job separations, as optimism can reward
institutions that also conduct investment banking (Hong and Kubik (2003)).
     Our second hypothesis is that optimistic bold last revision forecasts are more accurate than
pessimistic bold last revision forecasts, because they are likely to be based on a greater weight of
private information (Chen and Jiang (2006)). Recent regulation is presented next, before we
hypothesize on its effects.

2.3 Recent regulation

2.3.1 Regulation Fair Disclosure
In August 2000, the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) adopted Regulation Fair
Disclosure. The regulation forbids corporate managers from the practice of disclosing private
information to stock analysts without making that information public. Gintschel and Markov
(2004) examine earnings forecasts before and after Regulation Fair Disclosure and conclude that
the regulation “[…] has been effective in curtailing selective disclosure”. Likewise, Mohanram
and Sunder (2006) find support for the effectiveness of Regulation Fair Disclosure and that
analysts seem to have compensated for the loss of private information from managers by “[…]
investing more effort in idiosyncratic information discovery”.
     We assume that the private information uncovered through an increased search effort does
not outweigh the loss of such information from corporate managers, resulting in a net loss of
private information. A reduction in private information should affect optimists more than
pessimists, as optimists give private information a greater weight (Chen and Jiang (2006)). Our
third hypothesis is that optimistic bold last revision forecasts have become less accurate after

2000 , relative to pessimistic bold last revision forecasts.

to the forecast values, we also gather: the date and time of the forecast announcement, a code to
track each analyst, a code to identify the brokerage firm that employs the analyst, a CUSIP code
to identify the company analyzed, the fiscal year, and the actual reported earnings.

5.3 Potential issues
     We notice that there are four pairs of independent variables that have absolute correlations
above 0.25: Forecast horizon & Forecast frequency, Firm experience & General experience,
Companies & Industries, and Fiscal-year-end distance & Year-to-date distance (see Table 1
Panel E). The correlation among these pairs of independent variables could affect the results
of our regressions (section 5.2), through imperfect multicollinearity.
     We test the validity of our findings by repeating the four regressions that relate to our
hypotheses (Table 6, Columns 2 and 4 and Table 7 Columns 4 and 5) in Tables 8, 9, 10, and
11. In these tables, we exclude each of the eight, aforementioned, independent variables
(including each corresponding interaction term with Period in Tables 10 and 11) in turn.

6. Conclusions and implications

The purpose of this study has been to test whether a consensus that consists solely of optimistic
bold forecasts is more accurate than one that consists of pessimistic bold forecasts. Our findings
suggest that the former is, indeed, more accurate.
    We derive our findings from regressions that control for – and are therefore not biased by –
several analyst and forecast characteristics, such as the absolute distance of the forecast from the
consensus of the last 90 days (Year-to-date distance). The results pertain to last revision one-year
earnings per share forecasts for U.S. companies, issued by sell-side analysts within the time span
1987-2012. Forecast revisions are classified as bold if they are both above the analyst’s previous
forecast and above the consensus of the last 90 days, or below both. Bold forecast revisions that
are above both are classified as optimistic.
     We re-affirm Clement and Tse’s (2005) finding that bold forecast revisions are more
accurate than herding forecast revisions, for a longer and more recent time period, thereby
confirming our first hypothesis. This can be explained by private information: Clement and Tse
find that “[…] bold forecasts appear to reflect analysts’ relevant private information to a greater
extent than herding forecasts”, which explains the higher accuracy of bold forecasts that we
observe.
     We also find that optimistic bold revisions are more accurate than those that are pessimistic,
which confirms our second hypothesis. This is explained by Chen and Jiang’s (2006) finding that
optimistic forecasts tend to give a greater weight to private information. The systematic optimism
that Easterwood and Nutt (1999) describe should dilute the informational value of optimistic
forecasts; however, this effect is mitigated by the fact that we use Clement and Tse’s method of
analyzing only an analyst’s last forecast revision per firm-year. The optimism/pessimism pattern
(optimistic forecasts early on in the fiscal year, followed by pessimistic forecasts closer to the
earnings announcement) that Ke and Yu (2006) and Libby et al. (2008) find should thus lean
toward pessimistic for our dataset of last forecast revisions, reducing the effect of systematic
optimism that Easterwood and Nutt observed.
     We find support for our third hypothesis that optimistic bold forecast revisions have become
less accurate after Regulation Fair Disclosure, compared to pessimistic bold forecast revisions.
However, the finding appears to be affected by imperfect multicollinearity between Forecast
horizon and Forecast frequency. Furthermore, the support from the regression is weaker

                                      22
(significant at the 5% level, whereas our other three findings are significant at the 1% level), and
there is no support from the descriptive statistics. Ultimately, there is no clear-cut finding with
regards to our third hypothesis. This ambiguity is reflected in prior literature, as well:
Mohanram and Sunder (2006) find that while analysts receive less private information from
company management after Regulation Fair Disclosure, analysts compensate by looking harder
for private information; the net effect is uncertain. Furthermore, there were only three years
between Regulation Fair Disclosure and the Global Settlement, which makes it difficult to isolate
the impact of the former. Moreover, the 36 month period featured an eight month long recession
in 2001 (NBER, 2010). In addition, Rule 2711 and Rule 472 were adopted in July 2002, which
might have countered the impact of Regulation Fair Disclosure. We encourage future studies to
address these issues by (i) adjusting for the effect of business cycles (e.g. by using a dummy
variable, based on NBER’s classification) and (ii) excluding the effect of Rule 2711 and Rule
472, by examining the time period between the adoption of Regulation Fair Disclosure and the
implementation of the rules.
     Finally, we find that optimistic bold forecast revisions have become more accurate after the
Global Settlement, as well as Rule 2711 and Rule 472, which confirms our fourth hypothesis.
Indeed, the positive effect that optimism has on accuracy among bold forecasts is more
pronounced in the most recent period, 2003-2012. The increased relative accuracy can be
explained by a decreased share of optimistic forecasts that are driven by career concerns, leaving
a greater proportion of optimistic forecasts that are based on private information. The explanation
above assumes that the Global Settlement, Rule 2711, and Rule 472 were effective at reducing
career concerns, which Kadan et al. (2009) find support for. We pick the year 2003 to separate
our second and third time periods (2000-2002 and 2003-2012) to focus on the effect of the Global
Settlement in particular. As Rule 2711 and Rule 472 were adopted in July 2002, forecasts issued
between July and December 2002 – which are affected by the two rules – fall within the second
time period. This does not affect our confirmation of the fourth hypothesis, however; if anything,
it should render the hypothesis more difficult to prove.
     We observe that the share of optimistic bold forecasts increased after 2003. This change
seems puzzling, as the Global Settlement, Rule 2711, and Rule 472 should have reduced career
concern incentives to issue optimistic forecasts. Furthermore, Kadan et al. report less frequent
issuing of optimistic recommendations after these changes. However, information asymmetry

                                      23
between investors and analysts provides a plausible explanation: Investors might not have been
able to distinguish between optimistic forecasts that were motivated by career concerns and
optimistic forecasts based on the analyst’s private information – driving the latter out of the
market, as their private information is mistaken for career concerns. This resembles Akerlof’s
(1970) famous example, where bad cars (“lemons”) compel good cars to exit the market. The
Global Settlement, Rule 2711, and Rule 472 then reduced the number of forecasts motivated by
career concerns. Consequently, the analysts who were optimistic because of private information
re-entered the market. Although we provide a plausible explanation above, we encourage future
studies to investigate this puzzling finding further.
     Clement and Tse write that their results “[…] suggest that consensus forecasts that are
restricted to bold forecasts may be more accurate than consensus forecasts that weight all
forecasts equally.” Our findings suggest that an investor could find an even more accurate
consensus by restricting it to only optimistic bold forecasts. This holds especially true today, after
the Global Settlement, Rule 2711 and Rule 472. Hence, it might be possible to develop a
profitable trading strategy, based upon our findings. We encourage future studies to investigate
whether such a strategy can be constructed.

This table reports the relationship between forecast accuracy and boldness, while controlling for scaled
analyst and forecast variables, through a multivariate linear ordinary least squares regression. The variables
are based on 257,917 forecasts issued between 1993 and 2012, and the data was gathered from I/B/E/S Detail
History and CRSP. The sample has been restricted to forecasts issued within one year of the end of the fiscal
year, and at least 30 days before it. Only an analyst’s last forecast revision per company and fiscal year is
included. Outlier forecast revisions that are greater than 10 percent in absolute terms and outlier forecast errors
that are greater than 40 percent in absolute terms have been excluded from our sample. The table looks at the
variables: Days Elapsed – the number of days that have elapsed since the most recent forecast by any analyst for
the same company, during the same fiscal year; Forecast Horizon – the number of days between the forecast
announcement and the end of the fiscal year; Broker Size – the size of the brokerage firm that employs an
analyst, calculated for each year and brokerage firm as the number of unique analysts that have made forecasts;
Forecast Frequency – the number of forecasts that an analyst has issued for the same company and fiscal year;
Firm Experience – the number of years that an analyst has reported forecasts for the particular company; General
Experience – the number of years an analyst has released forecasts for any company; Companies – the number of
companies that an analyst have made forecasts for during the year; Industries – the number of industries that an
analyst have made forecasts for during the year, where an industry is defined as having the same two-digit
Standard Industrial Classification (SIC) code; Fiscal-Year-End Distance – the absolute distance between an
analyst’s forecast and the consensus (mean) forecast at the end of the fiscal year; and Year-To-Date Distance –
the absolute distance between an analyst’s forecast and the consensus when the forecast was announced, divided

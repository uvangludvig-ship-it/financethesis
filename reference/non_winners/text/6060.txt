Household Response to Fiscal Stimulus  :  A Study on Japan’s 2020 Special Cash
Payments Scheme Abstract:

       This thesis investigates the effectiveness of Japan's 2020 Special Cash Payments
        Scheme, a fiscal response to mitigate the economic downturn caused by the COVID-
       19 pandemic. Implemented as part of broader emergency economic measures, the
       government distributed ¥100,000 to every resident to  maintain  economic stability.
       Using data from the Family Income and Expenditure Survey, this study employs an
       Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression and an Instrumental Variable (IV) approach
       to analyse the short-term and  long-term impacts of these payments on household
       financial behaviour. The findings reveal that while there was a significant immediate
       increase in household financial assets and a reduction in  loans, the consumption
       response  varied  significantly  depending  on  liquidity  constraints  and  was  more
       pronounced   in   non-liquidity   constrained   households.   Additionally,   the   analysis
       suggests that the timing of the payments could have been optimised, as the payments
        were not fully utilised until economic conditions worsened. This study contributes to
       understanding the dynamics of fiscal stimulus on household behaviour during economic
        crises and provides insights for future policy formulation.

Keywords:

   Fiscal  policy,  Stimulus  payments,  Consumption,  Saving,  Debt,  Investment, Household
   behaviour

1   Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic plunged global economies into distress and forced
leaders worldwide to act quickly in an unprecedented situation, balancing the
avoidance of recession with the reduction of deaths.  The G7 countries expe-
rienced a sharp decline in GDP growth rate during the COVID-19 pandemic,
ranging from -2.8% in the United States to -11% in the United Kingdom, which
was subsequently followed by a spike in growth rates in 2021. Amongst these
countries, Japan was able to maintain the most stable GDP growth rates, experi-
encing a drop to -4.9% in 2020 followed by a return to more normal growth rates
at 2.2% in 2021. One potential explanation behind this range in GDP growth
rates resulting from the pandemic could be the differences in fiscal stimulus
policies imposed by the different governments, and their effect on households.
The UK, Germany, France, Italy, and Canada opted to only provide stimulus
checks to those who had been rendered unemployed as a direct result of the
pandemic, and all witnessed a  ‘mirroring’effect in 2021 when GDP growth be-
came positive once again. The United States, on the other hand, implemented
the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act (CARES Act) which
granted households stimulus checks depending on income levels and number of
children.
   Similarly, Japan also opted to pay stimulus checks to households, yet they
were far less selective when deciding on what basis to distribute these payments.
Moreover, Japan was the only G7 nation that did not adopt strict lockdowns,
and therefore the effects of the pandemic on individuals’lifestyles and businesses
were potentially weakened. As shown by figure 1, Japan witnessed the fastest
return to target growth levels out of the G7 nations, therefore implying that their
response to the economic decline was effective in maintaining economic stability.
In an uncertain situation such as the COVID-19 pandemic, government policy
responses are vital in deciding future repercussions that could have long-lasting
effects for years following a crisis. Examining whether or not a policy response
to a crisis was effective, and whether or not it could have been optimised, is
therefore crucial in guiding governments in how to act when future crises arise.
   This paper specifically examines the impacts of Special Cash Payments
(SCP), a part of the  ‘Emergency Economic Measures to Cope with COVID-19’
(新型コロナウイルス感染症緊急対策), during which the Japanese government
distributed ¥100,000, or approximately $930 at the time, to residents across
Japan. More specifically, I investigate the net increase in financial assets, sav-
ings, and securities, and the net decrease in land and housing loans and other
loans as a result of the SCP. I look both at initial responses to the payment
and on long-term behaviour up to 5 months after SCP receipt. In addition, I
comment on the effects of liquidity constraints on the net increase in financial
assets, savings, and securities, and the net decrease in land and housing loans
and other loans.
   For this analysis, I use data from the Family Income and Expenditure Sur-
vey, a nationwide household-level database to explore the effects of the SCP on
all items of households’balance sheets using an Ordinary Least Squares (OLS)

   I begin by running an OLS regression and two IV regressions, one with
monthly dummies and one without. I run these models on the effect of the SCP
on the change in household consumption expenditure, financial assets, savings,
purchase of securities, land and housing loans, and other loans.  I estimate a
marginal propensity to consume (MPC) of 7%  –9%, a net increase in financial
assets of 95%  –99% and a net increase in savings of 96%  –100%.  I also find
that households use 10% –34% of the SCP towards paying off land and housing
loans. I then run an IV regression with time lags of up to 5 months after the
receipt of the SCP to look at the delayed effects of the SCP. I find that after an

initial increase, financial assets and savings become negative, with withdrawals
between 19% - 26% of the SCP. This, coupled with the fact that consumption
increased by 11% - 12% 3  –5 months following the receipt of the SCP, suggests
that the stimulus payments were received at a time when they were not needed.
After initial receipt, I do not find any delayed effects on land and housing loan
repayment behaviour amongst households.
    Finally, I look at the effect of liquidity constraints on SCP usage by splitting
the cities in the dataset into two groups, based on whether or not they own more
than the median amount of liquid assets.  In contrast to previous literature, I
find that non-liquidity constrained households increased consumption by 15% -
20%, and do not find a statistically significant effect on consumption expendi-
ture amongst liquidity constrained households. Both groups put a large portion
of the SCP into financial assets and savings, with constrained households saving
approximately 88% of the SCP, and non-constrained households saving between
100% - 113%. Conversely, I find that constrained households use 15% - 16% to-
wards paying off land and housing loans, whilst the estimate for non-constrained
households is only significant when the model includes monthly dummies and
could therefore be explained by seasonal variation in debt repayment behaviour.
Across all specifications, I do not find a statistically significant effect on the net
purchase of securities or on the repayment of other loans.
   I argue therefore that the Japanese government was too early in its imple-
mentation of the payments, and that the effect on consumption in households
would have been greater had the pay outs occurred 3 to 5 months later. When
compared to previous packages, I find that this policy was relatively more suc-
cessful in boosting long-term consumption, however, argue that this may be due
to the subsequent exogenous spikes in cases. Despite the fact that the payments
were made before they were truly needed, households were sensible in that they
placed the excess in savings, creating a  ‘rainy day fund’that they later used
to maintain their lifestyles when they were in jeopardy, providing evidence of
consumption smoothing behaviour in households.
   Existing literature has focused primarily on household consumption in re-
sponse to stimulus packages and tax rebates. Rafiq (2012) argues that counter-
cyclical fiscal policies, in line with Keynesian accelerator principle, hold in
Japan. Therefore, the aim of this paper is not to explore whether the Japanese
government was justified in implementing fiscal measures during the COVID-19
recession, but rather whether or not the specific policy was optimal.  Looking
closer at previous fiscal measures in Japan, Shimizutani (2006) explores the ef-
fects of the 1998 tax rebate on household consumption and estimates an MPC
of 6% that declines over time.  They find that a tax cut was not sufficient to
stimulate consumption, and therefore other policy responses to financial slow-
down must be considered in Japan’s case. In addition, Hsieh, Shimizutani, and
Hori (2010) explore the effects of Japan’s 1999 shopping coupon program, which
was more similar in nature to the SCP, albeit more selective, more constrained,
and on a smaller scale. They find that the coupons led to an increase in spend-
ing on semi-durable goods, but there was no significant effect on non-durable
spending.  The shopping coupon program was viewed as one of the Japanese

                                  3
Government’s more successful policies, and therefore, from a policy perspective,
it is relevant to explore a policy that affected a far greater proportion of the
population whilst also imposing far fewer restrictions.
    Beyond Japan, Agarwal and Qian (2014) look at the effects of Singapore’s
2011 Growth Dividend Programme. Specifically within the G7 nations, Agar-
wal, Liu, and Souleles (2007) and Kaplan and Violante (2014) study the 2001
tax rebates in the US. Parker et al. (2013) explore consumption responses to
the 2008 Economic Stimulus Payments in the US, and just like Agarwal et al.
(2014) they treat the payment of stimulus checks as an event study. Acconcia,
Corsetti, and Simonelli (2020) look at three different post-earthquake recon-
struction programmes in Italy and study the impact on consumption and the
interplay between fiscal stimulus and liquidity on household responses.
   Michihito Ando et al. (2020) provide an overview of the Japanese govern-
ment’s fiscal policy response to COVID-19.   Looking specifically at the 2020
Special Cash Payments, Hattori, Komura, and Unayama (2021) find that house-
holds’baseline MPC in response to this payment was 7.6%.   Hattori et al.
(2021), Shimizutani (2006), and Hsieh et al. (2010) use the same dataset as us,
namely the Family Income and Expenditure Survey (FIES). Kubota, Onishi,
and Toyama (2021) use data from Mizuho Bank, one of Japan’s three largest
banks, and obtain a baseline MPC of 50%. Kaneda, Kubota, and Tanaka (2021)
also use proprietary transaction-level data and estimate a baseline MPC of 16%.
This vast difference in results is due to different specifications, specifically how
ATM withdrawals are treated.
   Japan was not the only country to implement such measures, and as such
there have been many studies across the globe that have also estimated the MPC
in response to such measures.  In particular, Baker et al. (2023) explore the
effects of liquidity on household consumption responses to the CARES Act and
find that liquidity-constrained households increase their spending to a greater
extent than non-liquidity constrained households.   This opposes my findings
that constrained households, as defined by liquid assets held, spend less than
non-constrained households and provides an interesting opportunity for future
research into why this difference arises.
   Beyond consumption, Greenwood, Laarits, and Wurgler (2023) explore the
effect of the CARES Act on retail investor activity and document an increase in
retail trading in retail-preferred stocks following the payment of stimulus checks.
In general, however, existing literature has predominantly focused on exploring
the relationship between fiscal stimuli and consumption, and I therefore aim
to provide a more nuanced overview of how households use stimulus payments.
Da, Warachka, and Yun (2018) explore the effect of fiscal policy on consumption
risk and find that counter cyclical policy causes a reduction in consumption risk,
which leads to a reduction in the equity premium demanded by investors. Just
like Greenwood et al. (2023), their study looks at the US, where they separate
firms on a state level based on the political party of the governor in that state.
My results show that the SCP did not have a significant effect on household
net purchase of securities, which is in line with Yamori and Ueyama (2022) who
explore the correlation between financial literacy and stock market participa-

                                  4
tion of Japanese households and find that the low levels of financial literacy
in Japanese households explains the historically low stock market participation
the country faces. Furthermore, Sekita (2011) uses the SLPS survey to explore
financial literacy rates and finds that only 49% of respondents were able to
answer questions on interest rates and inflation, and over 60% were unable to
answer questions on risk diversification, providing further explanation for my
findings on household purchase of securities.
    My contribution to existing literature is therefore twofold. I provide a more
nuanced image of how households in Japan used the SCP by going beyond con-
sumption, an area which previous literature has overlooked.  This then allows
us to make more informed conclusions on household consumption and income
smoothing behaviour, specifically during a time of unprecedented crisis.  This
paper also adds to existing literature on the Japanese government’s fiscal re-
sponses to crises and allows for comparisons to be drawn and for policies to be
further optimised. I provide clear paths for future research on both these topics.
    The rest of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2 provides background
information on the stimulus payments and the empirical strategy.  Section 3
describes the data and Section 4 presents the main results.  Section 5 shows
delayed responses to SCP receipt and Section 6 shows the impact of liquidity
constraints on household behaviour. Section 7 discusses the reults and compares
this policy with previous Japanese policies, and finally Section 8 concludes.

2.1   2020 Special Cash Payments
The COVID-19 pandemic was widespread, with the majority of countries ex-
periencing several explosions in cases.  It presented a novel threat as doctors
did not know how badly individuals would be affected by the infection due to
the fact that it presented itself with a broad range in symptoms, which ranged
from mild cold symptoms to death.  Japan experienced its first reported case
of COVID-19 in January 2020.   In mid-February Japan had experienced its
first COVID-related death, and by the end of February Hokkaido prefecture
had announced a state of emergency, which was extended to cover the whole
country in mid-April.  The virus spread at a rapid rate, and as such munic-
ipalities responded in various ways, however, unlike in the other G7 nations,
a mandatory lockdown was never enforced. Instead, the Japanese government
chose to rely on recommendations such as stay-at-home requests, which meant
that businesses were not forced to close, however many ended up having to do
so due to a decrease in demand and fear amongst customers.
    On the 20th of April 2020, Prime Minister Shinzo Abe’s Cabinet passed the
‘Emergency Economic Measures to Cope with COVID-19’act, which provided
households and businesses across Japan with economic support.  The act was
estimated to cost 21% of real GDP, or ¥117.1 trillion.  This study focuses on

                                  5
the ¥100,000 cash payments made to Japanese residents, a payment that was
substantially larger than the 1999 shopping coupon program and the 2009 cash
handout. For example, a married couple with 2 children would receive ¥400,000
in total, which was 33% more than the average monthly salary for a full time
worker at the time. 76% of the act was aimed at individuals and business, with
the direct payments to households representing 15% of this section, and there-
fore they are one of the main elements affecting individuals in Japan. Additional
support to households was also provided in the form of a doubling in the child
benefit allowance from ¥10,000 to ¥20,000 per month. For the most distressed
households, interest-free loans of up to ¥200,000 were offered in order to pro-
vide them with a temporary injection of capital, and an additional 3-month
interest-free loan of ¥450,000 - ¥600,000 with the specific objective of helping
households rebuild their lives was also offered. These loans could be forgiven in
extreme cases.  Finally, rent support could be provided to those struggling to
meet payments and certain social security contributions were either reduced to
removed entirely.
   All individuals that had been registered as residents in the basic registra-
tion system as of the 27th of April 2020 were eligible to receive the payment,
regardless of nationality, age, income level, employment status, etc.   Initially
Prime Minister Abe’s cabinet discussed paying ¥300,000 only to those directly
impacted by the pandemic, however this was not accepted by the coalition gov-
ernment as they deemed it insufficient to combat the adverse economic effects
on households caused by the pandemic.  Following a U-turn on previous an-
nouncements, the ¥100,000 handouts were officially announced on the 20th of
April 2020 by the Cabinet Office. Whilst there were in essence no restrictions
on who was eligible to receive the payments, household heads still had to send
in applications in order to receive the payment.  These applications were pro-
cessed on the municipality level. As such, municipalities had complete freedom
in determining when to begin accepting applications, which was determined by
their administrative capacity.  Therefore, households across Japan did not all
receive the payments at the same time, as this depended on when their mu-
nicipality started accepting applications, when the household head sent in the
application, and when the application was processed.   Moreover, individuals
could either apply online or by post, which also had an impact on the timing of
receipt.
   In the dataset, Morioka Shi was the first to start accepting applications on
the 11th of May 2020. Figure 7 in the appendix shows the cumulative pay out
of the SCP, and by the end of August 98% of the estimated ¥12.9 trillion had
been paid out, with 95% having been paid out by the end of July.

paid out for a given city. As I could not find reliable information on the starting

assumption that municipalities took about two weeks to process an application
and transfer the SCP, I look at the 28-day window from the 2nd week to the

3   Data

I use data on monthly household flows into consumption, financial assets, and
debt from the Japanese Government Statistics’FIES survey. The database is re-
garded as one of the most reliable for statistics on Japanese households (Hattori
et al. 2021). I looked specifically at data on households with two or more mem-
bers and chose the  “For City Groups, Districts and Cities with Prefectural Gov-
ernments”table to gain access to a dataset representative of households across
Japan.
   The dataset ranges from January 2015 to December 2020 and allows us to
establish a base level with which to compare household behaviour during 2020.
I look at the net positive flows of capital into financial assets (金融資産純増),
which is comprised of savings (貯蓄純増) and securities (有価証券純購入), and
the net negative flows into land and housing loans (土地家屋借金純減) and
other debt (他の借金純減).  In the replication, I also consider consumption
expenditure (消費支出).
   The FIES dataset is made up of 10,000 randomly selected households across
the whole of Japan, spread across the 52 cities with prefectural governments,
and therefore provide us with statistics that represent the nation as a whole.
These 10,000 households are aggregated by city in order to create the average
household for that city.  Table 1 shows summary statistics for key household
characteristics, calculated for the differences between 2020 and the average of
2015-2019 values. Income tax uses the average of 2016-2019 values due to the
aforementioned 2015 income tax hike.   I have data points from each month
during the specified window for all 52 cities and therefore have a total of 624
observations.
   More specifically, I look at the  ‘Other Special Income’(他の特別収入) vari-
able in the FIES database, which is a subcategory of  ‘Special Income’(特別収
入), together with  ‘Gifts’(受贈金). However, the variable  ‘Other Special Income’
is still subject to measurement bias as it is not solely comprised of the SCP.
Thus, to ensure that the results are unbiased, Hattori et al.   (2021) run an
instrumental variable regression, which tries to exploit the exogenous nature
of the application window for each city.  This instrument is uncorrelated with
Other Special Income, and as I make use of the same dataset and a similar
economic model, I incorporate their instrumental variable approach to address
the potential endogeneity issues that occur as a result of the bias.

5   Long-Term Effects

One key aspect of any government policy is the timing, as this is crucial in
determining whether or not the policy will achieve its intended outcome.  As
such, I introduce lags up to 5 months after the receipt of the SCP and explore the
long-term effects on household behaviour. Figure 3 shows the  β1  estimate when
I run the IV regression without monthly dummies for consumption expenditure,
financial assets, savings, and land and housing loans. At the time of payment,
the MPC was 15.3%, with 66.3% (68.1%) being placed into financial assets
(savings).  Finally, when t = 0, 13.3% was used towards paying off land and
housing loans. The results for financial assets and savings is significant at the
99% confidence level, the result for consumption expenditure at the 95% level
and the result for land and housing loans at the 90% level. There are no more
significant effects on land and housing loans during the studied period.

   Looking at household behaviour one month following the receipt, I no longer
see a significant effect on consumption expenditure, but start to see that there
was a net decrease in both financial assets and savings. Households withdrew
26.3% (26.9%) of the SCP from financial assets (savings) during this period,
and these effects are significant at the 99% level.   This behaviour amongst
households continues when t = 2, namely that there is still an insignificant
effect on consumption expenditure and that withdrawals are being made from
financial assets (savings) at similar levels.  When t = 3, 4, and 5, households
are still withdrawing from their financial assets and savings at a statistically
significant rate, however now I also witness a statistically significant increase in
consumption. During this period, households increase consumption expenditure
by between 6% - 12% of their SCP, whilst simultaneously withdrawing 19% -
25% (19% - 24%) of the SCP payment from their financial assets (savings).
   This change in household behaviour could imply that at the time of receipt,
households were not experiencing any lifestyle changes caused by the pandemic
and therefore simply placed the majority of the payment into financial assets
(mainly savings). The payment remained here until households started to expe-
rience economic hardship, potentially brought about the spike in daily COVID-
19 that occurred around the new year, or 5 months after the end of the receipt
window.

7   Discussion and Comparison to Previous Eco-
   nomic Stimulus Program

My estimates, across all specifications, imply that over 100% of the SCP was
spent or saved by Japanese households.   Whilst it is not reasonable to sug-
gest that households spent more than they received, there are two potential
explanations for this outcome. The first is omitted variable bias, namely that
the specifications do not control for other changes in households’economies that
could cause changes in spending behaviour, and the second is that I do not
look at all potential uses of the SCP. I do not run models on the effect of SCP
receipt on other elements of non-consumption expenditure in the FIES, which
could explain an aggregate effect of over 100%.
    I find that the majority, if not all, of the change in financial assets can be
attributed to a change in savings. Figure 4 shows the household currency deposit
reserves as a % of total financial assets of the G7 nations and demonstrates that
over 50% of financial reserves are held in savings in Japan.   Therefore, it is
unsurprising that I do not find a significant effect on household purchase of
securities.
   The fact that households started to withdraw from their financial assets and
savings after an initially large positive increase suggests that the economic boost
was not needed until 3  –5 months after the payments were made. As the win-
dow of receipt begins in May and ends in July, this means that the payments
were actually needed between August 2020 and January 2021. Figure 5 shows
the number of daily cases between May 2020 and January 2021, and shows a

                                  26
Figure 5: Daily number of cases in Japan from January 2020 to January 2021.
Source: WHO.

huge spike in cases between November 2020 and January 2021, with a peak of
approximately 43,000 cases in early January.  Looking at policy changes dur-
ing the same period, the Japanese government announced the suspension of its
‘Go to Travel’initiative, which subsidised domestic tourism, in an attempt to
disincentivise citizens from making unnecessary journeys. This measure would
have exacerbated the already vulnerable tourism sector. Furthermore, in Jan-
uary 2021 Prime Minister Yoshihide Suga declared a new State of Emergency
in Tokyo, and introduced limits on crowds at public events and requested that
bars and restaurants stop serving alcohol at 7pm, and close at 8pm. These addi-
tional measures, even though they only affected 30% of the Japanese population,
would have had a direct impact on households’economic situations.
   Culturally, Japanese households display more conservative reactions to the
SCP than some other countries. The excess payments were placed in savings,
creating a  ‘rainy day fund’that was later used towards consumption when the
situation became more dire.  This may not have been the case had the pay-
ments occurred in another country where households may be more inclined to
use this excess money towards non-essential consumption. Figure 6 shows the
Consumer Confidence Index during 2020. In April 2020, when the SCP was first
announced, households had the most pessimistic outlook on their future finan-
cial situation, and in June 2020, when the SCP was being paid out, households

                                  27
Figure 6: Consumer Confidence Index from January 2020 to December 2020.
The long-term average = 100, with values below 100 indicating a pessimistic

experienced an increase in real income. This provides evidence for consumption
smoothing, as households still had a negative outlook on the future but expe-
rienced a positive income shock that they saved in order to maintain the same
level of consumption in the future (Morduch, 1995). However, towards the end
of 2020, consumer outlook on consumption improved and there were no further
positive income shocks.  It was in this same period that households withdrew
from their savings in order to increase consumption, and, in other words, they
were able to  ‘maintain their lifestyles’.
    My results also imply that liquidity constraints had the inverse effect of pre-
vious studies.  I find that receiving the SCP had a far stronger positive effect
on the MPC of non-constrained households, and that it did not have a statis-
tically significant effect on consumption amongst constrained households.  As
the COVID-19 pandemic affected all households regardless of their economic
situation through illness, lockdowns, and uncertainty, it is possible that in the
dataset, non-constrained households were hit harder by the pandemic and subse-
quent measures than constrained households. Moreover, measures aimed specif-
ically at non-constrained households may have been sufficient in supporting
these households. In terms of household saving behaviour, my results are in line

                                  28
with those of Dynan, Skinner, and Zeldes (2004), who show that the rich do
have a higher propensity to save, but that all income groups exhibit a tendency
to save.
   Whilst the nature of the COVID-19 crisis was unlike any previous crisis, it
is still useful for governments to reflect on previous policy measures in other
crises in order to be better equipped in designing optimal policy responses in
the future. In response to the Asian financial crisis in 1997, the Japanese gov-
ernment introduced two separate temporary income tax cuts in 1997 and in
1998. Similarly to the SCP payments, tax cuts were not dependent on income,
but rather on the number of members in a given household. Shimizutani (2006)
explores the effect that these tax cuts had on consumption and find an MPC of
6%, which is broadly in line with my findings. They do not find any statistically
significant long-term effects on consumption and conclude that tax cuts are not
sufficient in stimulating consumption.
   In an attempt to stimulate consumption, in 1999 the Japanese government
handed out shopping coupons worth ¥20,000 to the elderly and families with
children under 18 as an alternative to a tax cut. These coupons could only be
used at local businesses and had a strict lifetime of 6 months. The scheme was
widely viewed as a success, and as such the government repeated the shopping
coupon programme in 2009. The key difference between the programmes is that
the 2009 programme offered coupons of ¥12,000 to all Japanese residents, with
the elderly and those under the age of 18 being eligible for an additional ¥8,000.
Hsieh et al. (2010) argue that the  ‘spend it or lose it’element of the policy would
only impact short-term household consumption, and their estimates show an
MPC on semi-durable consumption of 10% - 20% in the month of receipt of the
coupons.
   It is evident that the Japanese government has developed its policies based
on lessons learnt from previous responses to measures. The 2009 cash handouts
were widely viewed as insufficient in stimulating consumption as the majority
was placed into savings. I see the same phenomenon with the 2020 cash hand-
outs, however I also note a sustained increase in consumption in the months
following the handouts and argue that this is evidence of consumption smooth-
ing behaviour in Japanese households. The question then arises whether or not
the 2020 handouts would have had the same effect on consumption, had it not
been for the subsequent wave of cases that the government had been unable
to foresee. An interesting extension of this study would be to look further into
household economic outlook and explore the evidence provided for consumption
smoothing further. Moreover, Japan remains a patriarchal society, with many
households still following the  ‘Ie’set of values (North, 2009), and as such the
majority of household heads are the men of the household. However, it is be-
coming increasingly common that women become designated household heads,
and therefore exploring differences in consumption and saving behaviour be-
tween these two groups of households would be an interesting avenue for future
research.

                                  29
8   Conclusion

Japan, like many other countries, opted to implement fiscal stimulus in an at-
tempt to mitigate the negative effects of the COVID-19 pandemic and reduce
the likelihood of a recession. I find evidence that the SCP had a positive and
significant effect on long-term consumption expenditure, however I argue that
payments were made a few months too early. Despite this, Japanese households
were rational in their consumption behaviour and demonstrated consumption
smoothing. They saved the payments initially before using them towards con-
sumption in subsequent months when their situation worsened. I used the FIES
database and looked at changes in consumption expenditure, financial assets,
savings, securities, land and housing loans, and other loans. The SCP had the
greatest effect on financial assets and savings when initially paid out but was
also used towards paying off land and housing loans. I also looked at the effects
of liquidity constraints on household behaviour and find that the SCP had a
significant effect on consumption on non-liquidity constrained households, but
not on liquidity constrained households, when initially paid out. This study pro-
vides a detailed analysis of this policy and offers some suggestions for improved
future policy design in Japan.

Taking the common currency out of the common market: Redenomination risk in
the eurozone

Abstract:
This paper examines whether political and macroeconomic variables can explain and
predict redenomination risk spreads, defined as the residual CR14-CR CDS premium
derived through a synthetic-control method for France, Italy, and Germany between
2015 and 2024. Using daily CDS data combined with monthly political and
macroeconomic indicators, we construct country-specific redenomination spreads that
seeks to isolate the market-implied risk of a euro exit. We then run OLS regressions
linking these spreads to inflation, output gaps, ECB policy rates, exchange rates,
government debt, trade measures, and political variables. Our findings show that
redenomination risk is highly country-specific and event-driven. Italy exhibits
sensitivity to political variables such as parliamentary EU skepticism and election
timing. Germany shows minimal redenomination risk, driven only weakly by
government EU position, consistent with a safe-haven status and the possibility of
negative redenomination premia. France demonstrates persistent positive
redenomination spreads, but these are less well explained by observable political or
macroeconomic determinants. Across countries, selected macroeconomic variables,
particularly inflation and the EUR/USD exchange rate, provide additional explanatory
power, though their effects vary by national context. Our results suggest that
redenomination risk functions as a political tail-risk premium as it is dormant for long
periods but prone to repricing around elections, coalition uncertainty, or institutional
credibility shocks. These findings have practical implications for investors in sovereign
debt, indicating that traditional economic credit models may miss relevant forward-
looking risks unless they incorporate euro-membership credibility.

Keywords:

   Redenomination risk, eurozone, sovereign debt, currency, CDS

I. Introduction
A. Background
Since its inception in 1999, the euro has symbolized one of the most ambitious steps
toward European integration. By eliminating exchange rate fluctuations and reducing
transaction costs, it was designed to strengthen trade, investment, and financial stability
within the European Union. Over time, the eurozone has expanded to encompass 20
member states with diverse economic structures, fiscal positions, and political priorities.
However, this diversity has also exposed limitations of a shared monetary policy
without a corresponding fiscal union. The European sovereign debt crisis in the 2010s
exposed this tension and triggered widespread concern about the possibility of member
states abandoning the common euro. Such an event would expose bondholders and
other investors to currency redenomination risk, the risk that a state’s eurodenominated
debt could be repaid in a newly introduced national currency of a lower value relative to
the euro. There is also a rising theme of euro-skepticism in Europe, where parties who
have taken a lesser and greater anti-EU approach have seen electoral success in many
countries (European Commission, 2023).
Redenomination risk is distinct from traditional sovereign default risk as a country may
still continue to honor its bond obligations while altering the currency of a repayment.
This effectively imposes a loss unto the investor via simple devaluation. Conversely, a
country with a strong shadow national currency could experience negative
redenomination risk, as a eurozone exit could imply an appreciation of its new national
currency and thus a gain for investors. Understanding this distinction is crucial for both
policymakers and market participants, as redenomination risk tangibly affects the
pricing of sovereign bonds.

The economic logic behind redenomination risk also aligns with the twin Ds framework
as used by Augustin, Chernov, and Song (2020). They argue that sovereign credit
spreads in the euro area incorporate not only default risk but also the possibility of a
currency devaluation if a default were to occur. Their quanto-CDS evidence shows that
investors assign a sizable conditional probability to currency depreciation following
sovereign distress. This framework can be said to provide a theoretical foundation for
interpreting the CR14-CR difference as compensation for a default-linked currency
event, namely a euro exit or forced redenomination. They furthermore group euro
countries into two different groups, a core group and a periphery group. Core countries
refer to fiscally stronger and systemically important euro-area members such as
Germany and France, and periphery countries like Italy exhibit weaker fiscal positions
and greater exposure to regional financial stress.

To understand the relevance of their contribution, it is important to note that a CDS
quanto spread reflects the difference between a CDS contract written in different
currencies on the same underlying sovereign obligation. This difference isolates the
market’s expectations about an exchange-rate jump conditional on a credit event. In
other words, quanto spreads capture how much depreciation investors anticipate if
sovereign distress occurs. This mechanism is conceptually quite similar to the CR14-CR
distinction. Both settings allow the market to price a default-linked currency jump,
whether via depreciation of the euro against the USD (in the quanto case) or through a
switch from the euro to a newly introduced currency (in the redenomination case).

                                   2
Given the nature of the euro and the eurozone, the European Central Bank (ECB)
conducts a uniform monetary policy for the entire euro area and is mandated to maintain
price stability. Its institutional design originally limited its ability to act as a direct
lender of last resort to sovereigns, reflecting concerns about fiscal discipline and
monetary financing (ECB, n.d.). These constraints became salient during the euro area
sovereign debt crisis, when several member states faced surging bond yields driven not
only by credit concerns but also possibly by redenomination risk. To address this and
restore market confidence, the ECB introduced a sequence of interventions.

The first was the Securities Markets Programme (SMP) and consisted of targeted
purchases of stressed sovereign bonds to stabilize dysfunctional market segments and
reduce liquidity-driven spikes in yields. According to Krishnamurthy, Nagel, and
Vissing-Jorgensen (2018) SMP announcements are associated with noticeable short-
term declines in sovereign spreads. Later came the Long-Term Refinancing Operations
(LTROs), large-scale, low-cost loans to euro area banks. Although not aimed directly at
sovereign debt, LTRO liquidity eased funding pressures and indirectly supported
sovereign bond markets by reducing tail risks in the banking system. Last came the
Outright Monetary Transactions (OMT) in 2012, a conditional program allowing
potentially unlimited purchases of short-term sovereign bonds of countries.
Krishnamurthy et al. (2018) argues that the OMT was one of the pivotal measures that
quelled redenomination fears during the debt crisis, despite never being activated. The
inherent nature of the interventions are useful when trying to grasp the dynamics of
redenomination risk, as existing research shows that convertibility premia tended to
spike around moments of political and financial stress but fell sharply following
credible ECB actions, highlighting that redenomination risk behaved as an event-driven
rather than persistent component of sovereign yields.

In the years following the euro debt crisis, redenomination risk again became salient
during several major political events, such as the 2017 French presidential election and
the 2018 formation of Italy’s populist government. During these episodes, financial
markets priced measurable increases in exit risk premia, reflecting fears of either a
eurozone breakup or isolated exits. According to Kremens (2024), redenomination risk
accounted for up to 40% of France’s sovereign credit spread in 2017 and roughly one-
third of Italy’s in 2018, strongly indicating that investors differentiate between political
and debt-driven risks.
Building on existing literature, this present study focuses on the three largest economies
within the eurozone: France, Italy, and Germany. These countries collectively represent
the political and economic core of the monetary union, while also differing substantially
in fiscal capacity, market perception, and political climate. By analyzing the period
from 1 January 2015 to 31 December 2024, this thesis captures a period marked by
post-crisis stabilization, the COVID-19 pandemic, and renewed geopolitical and
inflationary shocks.
B. Research Question
To what degree can political and macroeconomic variables help explain and predict
redenomination risk spreads for France, Italy, and Germany? A natural sub-question
then becomes, what effect does that have on investors?

                                   3
C. Literature Review
This thesis aims to build on a growing literature examining how financial markets price
the risk of a eurozone breakup or the exit of individual member states. The most direct
reference is Kremens (2024), who develops a market-based measure of redenomination
risk using differences between CDS contracts governed by two sets of ISDA definitions
(see Methodology). His findings show that redenomination risk contributes substantially
to sovereign yields around political events such as the 2017 French and 2018 Italian
elections, highlighting its political rather than debt-driven nature.

While Kremens (2024) introduced a novel method to extract redenomination risk from
CDS markets, his analysis primarily focuses on identifying the risk premium rather than
explaining its political drivers. In practice however, redenomination risk is intertwined
with the domestic political environment. Factors such as euroscepticism, government
ideology, and electoral uncertainty directly influence market perceptions of euro
membership credibility. Building on this observation, our present study investigates the
role of political variables in shaping redenomination risk.

As mentioned, Krishnamurthy et al. (2018) decompose euro-area sovereign yields into
default, redenomination, and market-segmentation components. They show that the
European Central Bank’s (ECB) bond-purchase programs compressed yields partly by
lowering redenomination premia. This work underscores that redenomination risk is not
purely a financial-market artifact but also responds to institutional and policy
credibility, which is something this thesis aims to expand upon.
Furthermore, several related studies have analyzed the interaction between sovereign
credit risk and exchange rate risk. Augustin et al. (2020) use quanto CDS contracts to
decompose sovereign risk into distinct default and devaluation components. They find
that these two risks are tightly linked yet able to be separable, implying that sovereign
credit spreads incorporate both credit and currency dimensions. Their results
complement the notion of redenomination risk.
Our thesis contributes to this literature in two ways. First, it provides an independent
replication of Kremens’ redenomination spread estimates using data from LSEG
Workspace, validating the robustness of the synthetic-control approach and its
sensitivity to updated data sources by adding data from more recent years. Second, it
also extends the analysis by linking redenomination risk to political variables from the
Chapel Hill Expert Survey (CHES), testing whether changes in, for example,
government composition or euroscepticism systematically influence redenomination
risk. This second part is thus our original idea, as Kremens never tries to find a concrete
statistical connection between redenomination risk and interesting variables. We believe
that by trying to do so, a number of interesting conclusions can hopefully be drawn that
have both theoretical and practical uses.

II. Methodology
Following Kremens (2024), CDS markets distinguishes between three types of credit
events: (i) default without redenomination, (ii) default with redenomination, and (iii)
redenomination without default. Both the 2003 ISDA (CR) and the 2014 ISDA (CR14)
CDS contracts insure against events (i) and (ii), but only CR14 pays out in case (iii).
Therefore, the difference in CDS premia between CR14 and CR, known as the ISDA
basis, theoretically captures compensation for the risk of a stand-alone redenomination.
While redenomination is technically included in event (ii), Kremens argues that defaults
and redenomination do not usually coincide with each other and mentions examples
such as Greece not leaving the eurozone after its default and Czechoslovakia not
defaulting after its redenomination in the 1990s. We can thus infer that looking solely at
the ISDA basis, and variations of it, gives us some idea of redenomination risk.
Furthermore, when a country redenominates its euro-denominated debt into a new
national currency, the new currency typically depreciates once it begins trading freely.
Even if the government sets a one-to-one conversion rate at the exit, the market value of
the new currency can still fall when it starts trading on exchange markets. This
depreciation reduces the euro value of bondholders claims, generating an economic loss
even in the absence of a conventional default. CR14 contracts insure against this loss,
while CR contracts do not for G7 eurozone members. In practice, this means that if the
new currency trades at, for example, 20% below the initial conversion rate, a CR14
contract compensates for that loss whereas a CR contract remains silent. This difference
in payouts is what allows the ISDA basis to reflect the expected depreciation
conditional on a stand-alone redenomination (Kremens, 2024).
Since these contracts specify that only bonds issued by a G7 country are insured against
event (iii), it becomes natural to look at the three G7 countries that use the euro,
Germany, Italy, and France. As mentioned, the difference between the two CDS
contracts captures a market-implied redenomination risk. However, this raw difference
is affected by other factors. These include liquidity, contract design, and pricing
frictions. To isolate the pure redenomination component, Kremens constructs a
synthetic control for each treated G7 country (France, Italy, Germany) using other
eurozone countries unaffected by redenomination risk. The synthetic control reproduces
the behavior of the treated country’s ISDA basis using the observable covariates CDS
liquidity (measured as number of contributors), sovereign bond yields, and CR14 but
excludes the redenomination clause itself. Note that Kremens (2024) also uses CR14
bid-ask spread as a matching variable, but due to lack of data from LSEG we do not
include bid-ask spread. We argue that the number of contributors should sufficiently
capture liquidity and that bid-ask spread is not necessary for our analysis since it is in
this context largely a secondary measure of liquidity.
The donor pool consists of the following countries: Belgium, Finland, the Netherlands,
Portugal, Austria, Spain, and Ireland. We use these countries since they are all in the
eurozone, which avoids the relevant exchange rates interfering in the data, and taken
together we believe these countries can make for a sufficiently fitting portfolio of our
treated countries. Kremens (2024) uses in addition to the mentioned countries both
Cyprus and Greece, but due to a lack of data from LSEG we could not include these
countries. Note that for each treated country, the other two G7 countries are also used
for the synthetic control. For example, Germany’s donor pool also consists of Italy and

                                   5
France. The important assumption here is that redenomination risk is country specific
and does not propagate across the G7 treated countries.
For each of the countries for the synthetic control, we find daily optimal weights. This is
essentially a daily optimization problem that aims to minimize the loss function, which
is defined as the squared difference in predictors, for each day, by optimizing the
weights of the donor countries. This is done so that the weights sum up to 1, and no
weight can be less than 0. We add a small ridge penalty to the loss function to
discourage extreme weights. The predictors CR14, number of contributors and 5-year
bond yield are given an equal weight within one donor country, following Kremens
(2024).
 The synthetic control method is particularly appropriate in this setting because it
isolates the effect of redenomination clauses from other determinants of CDS spreads.
By creating a no-redenomination benchmark for each treated country, it allows for a
cleaner interpretation of the residual as the market-implied redenomination premium.
The difference between the treated country’s ISDA basis and that of its synthetic control
yields the redenomination spread (RS);

It is also worth mentioning that while the underlying CDS series is available at a daily
frequency, our method requires combining daily financial market data with political and
macroeconomic variables that are observed monthly or even quarterly. Using daily CDS
data alongside political indicators that are reported with much larger time-gaps would
inflate the number of observations without meaningfully increasing the information one
can conclude from this. Aggregating the CDS-based redenomination spread, after the
synthetic control, to a monthly frequency therefore ensures better consistency across
variables. A second motivation concerns the statistical properties of the synthetic-
control residual. At the daily level, the CR14-CR difference contains noise driven by
liquidity frictions and day-to-day sentiment shifts. Monthly averaging smooths these
fluctuations and produces a cleaner series that better captures the underlying risk

                                   6
premium. This approach is also conceptually aligned with Kremens (2024), who
emphasizes the importance of filtering away short-run noise when interpreting the ISDA
basis.

Furthermore, political developments, such as coalition negotiations, changes in cabinet
composition, shifts in party support, or adjustments in EU-related policy positions,
typically unfold over weeks or months rather than days. A monthly frequency therefore
matches the more natural horizon at which political uncertainty is reflected in markets,
allowing us to capture how political signals translate into sovereign credit risk without
overstating the precision of the political data.

III. Data
A. Data Description

The data used for the dependent variables includes both the CDS data and the data used
for the synthetic control, all of which are daily data taken from LSEG Workspace
(LSEG, 2025). The CDS data consists of CR14 and CR premiums for Germany, Italy
and France, measured as mid yields. The CDS contracts are denominated in U.S. dollars
and correspond to 5-year maturities, consistent with the most liquid tenor in the
sovereign CDS market. For the synthetic control we also use CR14 and CR data from
other eurozone countries, specifically Ireland, Portugal, Finland, Belgium, Austria,
Spain and the Netherlands. For all these countries, including Germany, Italy and France,
we further collect liquidity data for the CR14 contract, measured as number of
contributors and data on 5-year government bond yields, measured as mid yield, which
are used in the synthetic control.

In addition to the CDS data, which is used for the dependent variable, we include a
large variety of data used for the independent variables. Political variables include
government composition, euroscepticism, and time to election, allowing a dynamic link
between market prices and political developments. The inclusion of these can all be
motivated, and these motivations and the sources for the data hereby follow.

The inflation data is taken from OECD (2025b) and is measured as a harmonized index
of consumption, HICP, which is a measure of inflation that is comparable across
countries in the EU. If a country has unusually high or low inflation there is an incentive
to leave the euro for the country to be able to set the interest rate that is optimal for the
country itself, should the ECB interest rate not be the optimal rate for that country.
Eichengreen (2010) discusses scenarios in which inflation provides incentives for a
country to leave the Euro. One scenario is a hypothetical country, similar to Portugal,
suffering from high labor costs and chronic slow growth that might be incentivized to
leave the Euro in order to engineer a sharp real depreciation in their background
currency to export its way back to full employment. A second scenario provided by
Eichengreen is a hypothetical country, similar to Germany, with a strong economic
growth that is upset that the ECB has come under pressure to relax its commitment to
price stability and therefore reintroduces its background currency to avoid high
inflation. Including inflation therefore captures such occurrences.

                                   7
Our regression also uses output gap as a percentage of GDP. The data comes from
OECDs Economic Outlook 117 (OECD, 2025a) which uses forecasted data for 2025
and 2026, but since our observation period is 2015 to 2024 inclusive, the dataset only
contains observed data. Output gap measures GDP in relation to potential GDP and is
connected to inflation but inflation also depends on expected inflation and cost shock
and therefore does not follow output gap directly. Including both inflation and output
gap therefore enables us to test separately for the business cycle and price dynamics.
The theoretical effect of output gap on redenomination risk is similar to the above
reasoning regarding inflation. A country with a positive output gap is incentivized to
leave the euro to be able to increase the interest rate to the optimum, and vice versa for a
negative output gap.

The interest rate that ECB sets is highly relevant to our analysis and is therefore
included. We use the deposit facility rate, abbreviated as DFR, obtained directly from
the ECB (European Central Bank, 2025). The DFR is the interest rate banks receive
when they deposit money with the central bank overnight and is the key rate through
which the Governing Council steers its monetary policy stance (European Central Bank,
2024). The interest rate that the ECB sets is the interest rate of relevance in all eurozone
countries and that interest rate is therefore used for all countries in our analysis. An
interest rate that is not optimal is naturally captured by including the interest rate itself
in the regression. Since ECB sets the interest rate on specific dates, such dates involve
sudden movement in the interest rate, and since ECB does not disclose beforehand
which interest rate will be adopted, the level of optimality of the interest rate also
suddenly changes for each specific country. To further capture the relation between the
DFR and output gap, which largely determines if the interest rate is optimal or not for
each country in question, we create a moderating variable between output gap and DFR
based on the datasets already presented.

Exchange rate could also be motivated as relevant for redenomination risk. We use the
EUR/USD exchange rate with data from WRDS Compustat Global (WRDS, 2025). The
idea is to capture the strength of the euro, and comparing it to the U.S. dollar is a
reasonable approach to achieve this. The alternative would be to compare the euro to a
basket of other currencies, but this adds little value for our purpose. Since appreciations
of the euro against the dollar generally coincide with appreciations against a broader
basket of currencies, the EUR/USD exchange rate provides sufficient and direct
measure of euro strength. The theoretical impact on redenomination risk is that there are
incentives for the country to redenominate to stimulate net exports if the euro becomes
too strong. Redenominating and devaluing the currency makes exports cheaper and
imports more expensive, which stimulates GDP.

The EUR/USD exchange rate is connected to net exports, but since net exports also
depends on variables such as capital flows and interest rate, we include net exports as a
percentage of GDP (OECD, 2025a) separately in our regression. We use this measure
since dividing by GDP provides for better comparability across countries. The idea of
including this variable is similar to the reasons for including the exchange rate. If a
country has low net exports it can be incentivized to redenominate in order for its
exports to become cheaper for the importing countries. To capture the relation between
exchange rate and net exports we create a moderating variable with a method described

                                   8
under the Defining Variables section. This moderating variable captures the case when
both the exchange rate is strong, and net exports are low.

Our final economic data that we include in our regression is government gross debt as a
percentage of GDP (OECD, 2025c). We use gross government debt since net debt might
include assets that differ in liquidity between the countries. Dividing by GDP is done
here for better comparability as well. The rationale for including this variable is that
high levels of debt increase the incentive to use redenomination as a tool to solve fiscal
stress. Redenomination would allow a eurozone country with high debt to print money
to repay its high debt and inflate away large parts of the real debt.

The political data are from Chapel Hill Expert Survey, abbreviated as CHES, whose
mission is to estimate party positioning and policy issues for national parties in various
countries across the world (Chapel Hill Expert Survey, 2019; Chapel Hill Expert
Survey, 2024). The data sets from CHES are large files that contain a large variety of
political variables, but the most relevant variable for our research is EU position. EU
position is measured on a scale from 1 to 7, where 1 means that the party strongly
opposes European integration and 7 means that the party is strongly in favor. Although
a country can be a member of EU without adopting the euro, it is legally unclear if a
country leaving the EU would also be forced to leave the euro (Eichengreen, 2010).
However, since skepticism towards the EU implies a preference for retaining national
control over key political decisions, it is likely that skepticism towards the EU involves
skepticism towards the euro. We also use data on seat shares in parliament for each of
the parties. We use the seat shares instead of the vote shares since seat shares reflect the
actual power that each of the parties obtain from the election and is therefore a better
predictor of parliamentary actions, and thus also of potential redenomination. Note that
the seat shares in the data sets do not always add to 100%, which is because for some
parties in certain years seat share data is not available. However, the seat shares always
sum up closely to 100% and since our method, as described under Defining Variables,
implies creating a weighted average of the EU position of the parliament this is not a
problem. The CHES data further shows the parties in government for each period
through a dummy variable where 1 indicates that the party was in the government, and
non-government parties receive the value 0. This is useful because we also create a
variable with weighted average EU position of the government.

A problem with the CHES data set is that the French legislative election 2022 is not
included in the data. This is because the survey instead includes the 2024 legislative
election which was snap election. To solve this, we find data for the election results of
2022 and assume that the EU position of the parties are the same as those found in
CHES (2024). The data from the election 2022 comes from the archives of election
results in France (Ministère de l’Intérieur, 2022). A problem with this is that the
election results for the constituents of the coalition Ensemble is not shown separately in
the archives of election results (Ministère de l’Intérieur, 2022) but in the CHES data the
results of constituent parties are shown separately. Due to lack of other sources, we use
Wikipedia (Wikipedia contributors, 2025) for the election results of the constituents of
Ensemble and cross-check it against the election archives (Ministère de l’Intérieur,
2022) so that the seats of the constituents add up to the seats of the Ensemble. We argue
that this is the best we can do due to the lack of other sources, and potential small errors
in the exact composition of seats of the Ensemble from the Wikipedia article will not

                                   9
severely damage our results since we use it simply to decompose the election results of
Ensemble.

The motivation for including the parliamentary EU position is that redenomination must
occur through a parliamentary decision and the weighted average EU position of the
parliament should therefore reflect the probability that a decision for redenomination
could occur. We also include the weighted average EU position of the government. The
idea for this is that governments often propose laws on which the parliament thereafter
votes. A second reason is that government parties often receive more media attention
and can therefore affect the public opinion to a higher degree in comparison to parties
which do not receive as much media attention.

The final political data used is simply the election dates from which two variables are
created. One of the variables is a variable with the remaining months to the next
election, and the second variable is a dummy variable where months that are within one
year of the next election receive the value 1, otherwise 0. The reasoning for this is that
the closer to an election, the sooner in time the results of the next election, which are
uncertain, will have an impact. The closer to an election, the more intense the political
discussion also becomes and parties might change their opinions to achieve as many
votes as possible in the upcoming election. The dummy variable is constructed to
specifically capture the last-mentioned aspect.

B. Defining Variables

Dependent Variable

Y: Residual ISDA spread. The dependent variable is created based on the daily data
from LSEG terminal of the spread between premia on CR14 CDS and CR CDS. We
perform a synthetic control to isolate the redenomination risk part of the spread, see
Methodology. The daily residual ISDA spread are transformed into monthly residual
ISDA spread by using the arithmetic average residual ISDA spread for each month as
the value for the residual ISDA spreads of the months.

Independent Variables

I: Inflation. The variable uses the monthly inflation data from OECD directly measured
as harmonized index of consumer prices, HICP.

OUT: Output gap. The variable uses quarterly data from OECD and is measured as
output gap as percentage of potential GDP. The quarterly data is converted to monthly
data by assuming that each of the months in the quarter have the same output gap. This
implies that each of the output gap data points from OECD is repeated for the three
months in the quarter.

IR: ECB deposit facility rate. The DFR data from ECB includes the dates when ECB
changes the DFR and the interest rate they change to. The variable is therefore naturally
constructed by extending a DFR rate over all days until the next date when the DFR is
changed. The variable is than transformed to monthly data by using the month end

                                  10
principle which is motivated by the fact that DFR has low volatility and has the same
values for long periods of time.

MODX: Moderating variable of DFR and output gap. The variable is constructed by
multiplying the monthly data from variable IR with the monthly data from variable
OUT. Before the multiplication, the IR variable is centered around the mean of the DFR
during the period 2015-01-01 to 2024-12-31. This is done to reflect that the risk of
redenomination should be higher when the interest rate is low and output gap is high,
and when the interest rate is high and output gap is low. The distance from the mean
interest rate defines if the value of the interest for a certain month is high or low. The
product is thereafter multiplied by negative 1 with the logic that more positive values
indicate higher risk cases of redenomination risk which is easier to understand in
contrast to if high risk cases should be negative.

EX: EUR/USD. The variable uses the American term exchange rate between the Euro
and the US Dollar. Due to the high volatility that is common in currency exchange rates,
we construct monthly variables by using the average of the daily exchange rate values
during each month.

D: Government gross debt as percentage of GDP. The variable uses yearly data from
OECD which is then transformed into monthly data by assuming that each month in the
year has the same value.

NETX: Net exports as percentage of GDP. The variable is constructed by converting the
quarterly data from OECD to monthly data by repeating the value of the quarter for the
three months in each quarter.

MODY: Moderating variable between net exports as percentage of GDP and EUR/USD
exchange rate. The variable is constructed by multiplying each monthly data point from
variable NETX and variable EX. Due to the higher volatility of the variables involved
here, in comparison to MODX, and because of different mechanisms, this moderating
variable is created with the use of a function. The function implies that the values of
NETX and EX are multiplied when both net exports are lower than the mean for the
period (2015-01-01 to 2024-12-31) and when the EUR/USD exchange rate is stronger
than the mean for the same period, otherwise the value of the specific month is set to
zero. This captures the fact that a weak Euro is always positive for net exports, while a
strong euro is only negative if net exports are historically low.

ME: Months until election. The variable is constructed with the relevant dates of past
and future elections and calculates the number of months until the next election. This is
done by finding the difference in years multiplied by 12 and the difference in the
numeric values of the months. Thusly, the difference in days is excluded from the
calculation. For the next Italian election, we used the latest possible date it must be held
(Constitution of the Italian Republic, n.d.).

DE: Dummy variable time until election. The variable uses relevant dates of past and
future elections and is equal to 1 if the next election is held within 1 year, and 0
otherwise.

SP: European Union skepticism in parliament. The variable uses data from CHES which
includes the EU skepticism of specific parties in each country and is updated so that for
every country except France, each of the parties involved in the survey for each
parliamentary term from 2015-01-01 to 2024-12-31 obtains a decimal value from 1-7 of
EU skepticism, where a lower value means higher EU skepticism. We assume that the
EU skepcitism within parties remain the same for one parliamentary term and therefore
extend the values of EU skepticism from CHES for the entire parliamentary terms. Due
to the unordinary election held in France in 2024, the period from the election in 2022
until the election in 2024 does not have values for EU skepticism since no survey is
made in that period. We therefore assume that the values of EU skepticism of the parties
obtained in 2024 is also the values of their EU skepticism in 2022. This is done because
2024 is the closest point in time where EU position is measured, and we argue that it is
reasonable to assume that the EU positions of the companies do not change significantly
in 2 years. The variable is created as a weighted average of EU position based on the
share of seats from the CHES data. Since all parties in parliament are not always
included in the surveys, the shares of seats do not always add to 100%. However, since
only small parties are excluded, the sum of the seat shares is always relatively close to
100% and sufficiently represent the position of the parliament. Since the CHES data
does not include the outcome of the 2022 election, the weighted average parliament

is constructed based on number of seats in the lower house parliament out of 577

(Wikipedia contributors, 2025) and the EU position of those parties from the 2024
CHES survey which covers the 2024 election. This is done because 2024 is the closest
point in time where EU position is measured, and we argue that it is reasonable to
assume that the EU positions of the companies do not change significantly in 2 years.

IV. Main Empirical Findings
A. The Raw ISDA Basis
To fully motivate the construction of the redenomination spread used in the subsequent
analysis, this subsection begins by examining the raw ISDA basis, defined as the daily
difference between CR14 and CR CDS. The difference between the two theoretically
reflects the additional compensation investors demand for the possibility of
redenomination into a depreciated domestic currency. However, this raw difference also
incorporates several non-redenomination components, including liquidity differences,
contract-specific frictions, market-wide risk appetite, and idiosyncratic pricing
anomalies. Thus, the raw basis can be somewhat informative but importantly, it does
not isolate the pure redenomination component. We denote this collection of graphs as
Figure 0 as it’s not intended to carry serious informational weight on redenomination
risk and is only used to validate the post-synthetic control graphs.
Figure 0. Raw ISDA Basis for Germany-France-Italy (2015-2024)

Figure 0. The Raw ISDA Basis is mathematically denoted as the difference between the two CDS contracts CR14 and
CR (CR14-CR). Data: ISDA CDS quotes.

The raw basis reveals substantial cross-country variation. France exhibits a period of
quite low spreads early in the sample, followed by a clear shift to a higher level around
2017. After this break, the French basis fluctuates within a moderately elevated range
for the remainder of the sample, with some medium-term drift but no prolonged return
to pre-2017 levels. Germany by contrast, shows consistently low and relatively stable
values throughout. The German basis remains tightly clustered around a narrow band,
with only short-lived deviations that coincide with periods of market-wide volatility
rather than prolonged country-specific movements. Italy shows the opposite pattern as
its raw basis is both larger in magnitude and more volatile. Several pronounced episodes

                                  13
of widening are visible, and even outside these periods the Italian series remains clearly
above those of France and Germany.

The purpose of presenting the raw basis is not to interpret these levels as
redenomination premia, but rather to highlight two empirical features that motivate the
next step of the analysis. First, the raw series contains substantial high-frequency noise
and market-wide shocks that might obscure the underlying signals. Second, the overall
level and volatility differ markedly across countries, suggesting that contract-specific
and liquidity-related components actually vary substantially across issuers and over
time. These patterns make the raw basis an informative starting point but an imperfect
object for cross-country comparison or structural interpretation. The synthetic control
procedure addresses these limitations by constructing a benchmark that absorbs the non-
redenomination components embedded in the raw series.

Overall, the raw ISDA basis provides a useful descriptive overview of market pricing
patterns and highlights substantial cross-country variation. However, as mentioned the
CR14-CR spread also reflects non-redenomination factors that vary across time and
across issuers, it does not isolate the pure redenomination component. To address this
limitation, the next section presents a synthetically controlled version for each country.
By matching treated countries to a weighted combination of control units that replicate
the non-redenomination pricing components, the synthetic control method allows the
residual difference to be interpreted as a more accurate measure of redenomination risk.

V. Discussion

A. Main Discussion

Our empirical results support that redenomination risk within the eurozone is not a
homogeneous phenomenon but is shaped by country-specific political structures,
institutional credibility, and macro-financial sensitivities. Although macroeconomic
fundamentals play a role, much explanatory power arises from political variables,
particularly in countries with unstable coalition governments and a history of
Eurosceptic political movements.

Italy’s results demonstrate that redenomination risk is to an extent political. Multiple
political variables, including EU position, months to election, and election year are
statistically significant, and the model explains almost 80% of the variation in Italy’s
residual CDS spread. This aligns with the synthetic-control evidence showing large

                                  21
positive deviations around political transitions such as the 2018 formation of the first
Conte government and the 2022 shift to a more Eurosceptic (SIEPS, 2022) coalition led
by Giorgia Meloni. These episodes illustrate that markets rapidly price in changes in
expected policy direction when credibility towards the euro becomes uncertain. The
Italian case thus strongly supports the view that redenomination risk is closely tied to
political fragmentation and governing coalition instability. It is also worth noticing that
the residual spreads are much larger for Italy, than for Germany and France, suggesting
that the risk for redenomination is priced in relatively high.

Germany on the other hand presents the opposite situation, as its redenomination spread
is close to zero or even negative throughout the sample. The regression also shows that
political drivers are limited. Only the government’s EU position carries some statistical
significance (close to 5%), and even this effect is small. This reflects Germany’s
comparatively stable political system, low electoral and political volatility, and central
role as a safe asset issuer in the eurozone. If hypothetically redenominated, German
sovereign debt would likely be repaid in an appreciating currency, generating negative
redenomination risk (Kremens, 2024). The German results therefore highlight the role
of institutional strength and market confidence as stabilizing forces.

France lies between these two extremes. The synthetic-control results show sustained
positive redenomination premia after 2017, especially during the 2022 presidential
election. Yet the regression analysis reveals relatively weak explanatory power from
political variables. This may reflect the structure of the French semi-presidential system,
in which the president enjoys substantial executive autonomy even when parliamentary
fragmentation increases. Investors may therefore view French political volatility as less
likely to translate into changes in currency or fiscal policy, provided extreme shifts in
the presidential elections do not occur. The French case illustrates that redenomination
risk can remain elevated even when political variables are less predictive, which might
be suggesting a more structural or persistent premium unrelated to short-term political
cycles.

So far, a central insight emerging from this thesis is that redenomination risk in France,
Italy, and Germany does not behave as a persistent component of sovereign spreads.
Instead, it responds sharply to political shocks, while remaining more dormant during
periods of political calm. This pattern aligns with the findings of Krishnamurthy, Nagel,
and Vissing-Jorgensen (2018), who also document that redenomination risk behaves as
an episodic and event-driven premium rather than a stable or slow-moving factor. In
their analysis of ECB crisis interventions (namely the SMP, OMT, and LTROs), the
authors show that redenomination risk plays only a limited role during normal periods,
implying that redenomination risk was unlikely to be a major contributor to yields for
most of their sample period (2010-2012). They show that for the eurozone countries
Spain and Portugal, the redenomination premium is typically muted outside of major
ECB policy dates, and its contribution to overall yield changes is modest on average,
only accounting for only 13% of the total yield across the SMP and OMT
announcements. Yet this small average contribution masks a crucial feature of the
redenomination premium, which is that it responds to meaningful events.
Krishnamurthy et al. show that redenomination risk drops sharply in Spain around the
SMP and OMT announcements, with reductions of 20-56 bps observed in narrow two-
day windows. These large and sudden movements demonstrate that redenomination risk

                                  22
is not a smooth or continuous process. Instead, it is activated by specific developments
that change the perceived likelihood of euro exit, whether by stabilizing forces (ECB
intervention) or destabilizing ones (political instability or fiscal stress). The authors
emphasize this point when noting that redenomination risk was present at times but not
persistently so.

Our thesis finds a similar pattern in the political domain. Political shocks do not
uniformly elevate redenomination risk, instead their effects appear more punctuated,
emerging only when political developments meaningfully alter expectations about
policy continuity. This mirrors the event-driven dynamics in Krishnamurthy et al. but
with the relevant shocks coming from domestic political processes rather than ECB
policy actions. In both settings, redenomination risk represents a latent risk premium
that remains suppressed until an event triggers a repricing of euro-area breakup
probabilities.

Redenomination risk could be said to be a tail-risk premium, characterized not by
continuous movement but instead by concentrated jumps around certain relevant events
that change the market’s fundamental perception of the euro. The ECB policy events
examined by Krishnamurthy et al. and the political shocks analyzed in this thesis can
therefore both be said to operate through this mechanism. Where their analysis
demonstrates that ECB interventions can reduce redenomination risk in compressed
time windows, the results here show that political instability can increase it with a
similarly abrupt pattern.

Taken together, these insights again support the view that redenomination risk is best
seen as an event-sensitive component of sovereign spreads. Its episodic nature helps
explain why average levels remain low even when the theoretical potential for euro
fragmentation exists, while its sharp reactions to political shocks underscore the
importance of institutional credibility and political stability in sustaining market
confidence in the euro.

The twin-Ds framework also helps explain some cross-country asymmetries we
observe. According to Augustin et al. (2020), the conditional devaluation probability is
far higher for core countries (where they include Germany and France) than for
periphery ones (Italy), meaning that even fiscally strong countries price a substantial
currency-jump component when default risk rises. This aligns with our finding that
France, despite its fundamentals, exhibits persistent positive residual spreads after 2017.
Conversely, Italy’s elevated and volatile residual spreads are more consistent with the
periphery factor structure described in their model, where regional stress and political
fragmentation amplify the interaction. Germany’s near-zero or negative residuals reflect
its position as a safe-asset issuer, for which redenomination would likely imply currency
appreciation rather than depreciation, a mechanism they highlight in their contribution
quanto-CDS literature.

Another relevant insight from Augustin et al. (2020) is the distinction between true and
risk-adjusted probabilities. Their model shows that markets require substantial
compensation for bearing currency-jump risk, implying that the risk-neutral probability
of devaluation conditional on default is higher than the true underlying probability. This
to an extent parallels our findings that redenomination risks tend to spike in response to

                                  23
political shocks even when fundamental fiscal conditions remain unchanged, with the
latter being a reflection of unchanged true probability. The market prices the risk of
such events, reinforcing the event-driven nature of redenomination risk in the euro area.

An important pattern in our results is that the parliamentary EU-position variable and
the government EU-position variable does not have a uniform explanatory power across
all three countries. This asymmetry is not random as it can be argued that it reflects
structural differences in how political authority is exercised in Italy, France, and
Germany, and how investors interpret the institutional feasibility of redenomination. As
noted earlier, redenomination is formally a legislative act, but investors may not treat all
political actors as equally relevant signals of redenomination likelihood.

Italy is the clearest example of this logic. The Italian political system is characterized by
fragile coalitions, frequent government turnover, and high legislative fragmentation
(The Economist, 2021). Because Italian governments rarely survive a full term, markets
could regard individual cabinets as unreliable predictors of long-run policy direction.
What ultimately matters for a decision as drastic as redenomination is also broad
parliamentary support, not the stance of a particular cabinet that may fall within months.
Germany presents the opposite institutional environment. Its parliamentary system is
marked by comparatively stable governing coalitions and an executive that typically
steers the legislative agenda with consistent majorities. An example of these would be
the grand-coalition Merkel cabinets. France occupies an intermediate position. As a
semi-presidential system, executive authority is shared between a directly elected
president and a government that generally sets policy priorities. While the National
Assembly plays an important legislative role, macroeconomic and European policy
signals tend to carry the most weight when coming from the executive branch rather
than parliament. Ertaş (2023) mentions how the French president can, and have, passed
legislative acts without going through a round of voting in parliament first. This helps
explain why the parliamentary EU-position variable does not explain redenomination
risk for France, while the government measure shows only marginal significance.

A common theme across regressions is that the macroeconomic variables also explain a
good portion of redenomination risk. Italy, however, remains predominantly driven by
political variables, again suggesting that markets view Italian redenomination risk as
domestic and political rather than macroeconomic in nature. Germany’s role as a
structurally export-oriented economy provides a plausible explanation for why net
exports appear to correlate with lower redenomination risk in our results. Germany has
maintained one of the world’s largest and most persistent current-account surpluses
throughout the euro era, driven by strong manufacturing competitiveness and external
demand. As documented by the Bertelsmann Stiftung (2018), this surplus is a long-
standing structural feature rather than a cyclical fluctuation. Kremens (2024) also
frequently mentions that the euro is undervalued relative to what a standalone Deutsche
Mark would likely be, reinforcing the idea that Germany’s exports gains from euro
membership. Against this background, rising net exports can be interpreted as
reinforcing Germany’s structural advantages within the monetary union, thereby
reducing the perceived likelihood of any currency change. While the literature does not
directly link fluctuations in trade balances to redenomination-risk pricing (Kremens,
2024; Krishnamurthy et al., 2018), the mechanism can be argued to be consistent with
Germany’s position as the eurozone’s anchor economy.

                                  24
In Italy’s case, the inflation variable being significant and with a positive coefficient is
logical due to the country’s nature as a high-risk country, where political debates have
raised Eurosceptic concerns. High inflation often brings a desire to set one own’s
interest rate, which would only be possible if Italy left the eurozone. Thus, high
inflation can directly correlate to increased redenomination risk if the ECB interest rate
is not the optimal rate for the country in question, which seems to be the case for Italy.

Germany shows a negative and significant coefficient for inflation which is reasonable
if the interest rate that the ECB sets is optimal for Germany, which likely is the case
most of the time since Germany is by far the largest economy in the EU. The negative
coefficient is explained further by the argument that Germany having high inflation can
be interpreted in terms of the eurozone as a whole having an economic upturn,
something that would hardly have Germany considering leaving the euro due to
economic concerns. This can be because of a potential high correlation between
economic upturn in Germany and economic upturn in the rest of the euro countries due
to the large size of the German economy.

However, since the residual spread for Germany is negative for a large period, a
negative coefficient for inflation might instead imply a larger risk for redenomination. A
more negative spread implies that the CR14 premium is lower in comparison to CR
premium, which happens if a redenomination would benefit the bondholder due to a
stronger shadow currency. If the negative coefficient of inflation mainly stems from the
negative residual spreads, the effect of inflation on redenomination risk would be
similar to the case of Italy, where inflation increases redenomination due to a will the
country to control their own interest rate. For Germany’s case, the higher inflation
would then imply that the shadow currency appreciates, or more accurately that the
redenomination to the shadow currency would be made possible at a stronger initial
currency rate, which explains the more negative residual spread.

We cannot fully prove which of the cases for Germany is the true case, but by running
the regression using the absolute value for each residual spread data point, see Appendix
B, we find that the coefficient for inflation is positive. When using absolute values, a
higher value implies higher redenomination risk. Therefore it is likely that the second
case holds true, where inflation increases redenomination risk, but we cannot prove this
entirely using absolute values because it is not certain that a distance to the x-axis from
the negative side corresponds to the same risk for redenomination as the same distance
to the x-axis from the positive side.

For France, inflation is not significant. It could be the case that the ECB interest rate for
the period closely matches the optimal interest rate for France, and the inflation does
therefore not become significant since it is handled by ECB with a suitable interest rate.

France obtains a significant negative coefficient for the EUR/USD exchange rate while
the exchange rate is significant with a negative coefficient for Germany at the 10% level
with a p-value of 0.065. Italy does not obtain a significant p-value, although the p-value
is relatively close to being significant at the 10% level (p-value of 0.129), also with a
negative coefficient. The negative coefficient implies that as the value for the exchange
rate increases, meaning that the euro appreciates against the U.S. dollar, the
redenomination risk decreases. In our motivation for including the variable we argued

                                  25
that a higher exchange rate should increase the redenomination risk as countries are
incentivized to leave the euro in order to redenominate to a weaker currency to stimulate
net exports. Our results instead show the opposite. This can be explained by the fact that
using currency devaluations as a tool for stimulating net exports is a short-term solution.
A strong euro instead ignites competition among the domestic companies, but also
between domestic companies and companies abroad, to provide better products or
services. This benefits the economy in the long run which makes redenomination an
inappropriate tool for achieving economic growth. The negative coefficient of the
exchange rate can instead be explained by the strength of the economy captured through
the exchange rate. A stronger euro is a result of high exports and/or high financial
inflows in the forms of investments. The high exports means that the economy is likely
doing good. In this situation there are no incentives to stimulate net exports.
Interestingly, the exact value of the coefficient might reflect the volume of trade and
investments between the USA and the country in question where more trade and
investments should imply a more important relationship.

If the strong euro is caused by high financial inflows, this signals a strong belief in the
future of the economy since many investors believe they have found positive NPV
investments, or at least investments that have returns equal to the discount rates. If
investors would not believe that they would receive at least a return equal to the
discount rate, capital would not flow into the country to such a large extent to influence
the exchange rate until the market prices such investments cheap enough to expect a
return at least equal to the discount rate.

Furthermore, our data supports that government debt significantly affects
redenomination risk and has a negative coefficient for Italy. The negative coefficient
does not align with our hypothesis on how the variable would affect redenomination
risk. We motivated the inclusion of the variable by reasoning that a higher government
debt incentivizes the country to redenominate to be able to print money and inflate away
the real value of the debt. The interpretation of the negative coefficient is instead the
opposite, that higher debt reduces redenomination. This result is counterintuitive, but it
becomes reasonable when interpreted in terms of trust in the Italian economy. The
government gross debt as a percentage of GDP is in our data set always much higher for
Italy than for Germany and France. To illustrate this, Italy had a government gross debt
as percentage of GPD in 2024 of 148.41% while Germany and France had 63.288% and
116.457% respectively (OECD, 2025c). When the gross debt of the Italian government
increases even further when it is already at high levels, this signals a trust from debt
investors in the Italian government. Debt investors, whether private or institutional,
would not want to lend to Italy if they did not expect that Italy would be able to repay
the debt in the future. For this expectation to hold, investors would need a positive
forecast on the Italian economy. Therefore, potential uncertainty in the market about the
potential of Italy to repay its debt is reduced when institutional investors and other large
investors show trust in the Italian economy and government, which helps explain the
inverse relationship between redenomination risk and government gross debt. This can
be further explained by understanding that the Italian government would not seek loans
without a purpose, and the increased debt can therefore be used for growing the Italian
economy which strengthens the economic forecast of Italy. As the economic forecast of
Italy is strengthened, the need to redenominate to stimulate the economy through

                                  26
multiple channels diminishes. Government gross debt in Germany and France does not
significantly affect redenomination risk according to our result. This is reasonable since
both countries have much lower government gross debts as percentage of GDP.
Investors might therefore never doubt that these countries will be able to repay their
debt, and changes in the level of government debt thereby has no significant impact on
redenomination risk.

As the exchange rate can be regarded as significant for Germany and France, and almost
significant for Italy, while the net export variable shows significance for Italy only, their
interaction term, moderator Y, does not provide additional explanatory power for
redenomination risk in Germany and France where Moderator Y is statistically
insignificant. Moderator Y was constructed to only include extreme cases where net
exports are below the mean while the exchange rate is above the mean. These cases do
not occur often in our dataset for Germany and France, and in those cases they do occur
they do not significantly affect redenomination. This might be because the magnitude of
deviations from the respective means are not large enough for redenomination to
become relevant. There might also be a problem of multicollinearity. However,
Moderator Y is nearly significant at the 5% level (p-value of 0.060) for Italy. This
suggests that the extreme cases discussed earlier do occur for Italy. When net exports
fall below their average and the euro is simultaneously stronger than its mean value,
markets appear to perceive a higher incentive for Italy to exit the euro to stimulate net
exports. The negative coefficient of -191.95 is large and negative, reinforcing this
interpretation.

Moderator X, the interaction term between the ECB DFR and output gap, did not show
significance. DFR and output gap did not show significance by themselves for any of
the countries either. A likely explanation is the low frequency of the output gap
measure, which is only available annually. Because the annual values are repeated
across all months in the year, the variable exhibits very limited variation, reducing its
ability to explain monthly fluctuations in redenomination risk. Because output gap
updates infrequently, a large part of the variation in the moderating variable stems from
the variation of the DFR. Therefore, there might be problems with multicollinearity
which we will examine further in our robustness check. DFR and output gap might both
be insignificant because of possible multicollinearity with Moderator X. For output gap,
it might also be the case that inflation, which depends on output gap, expected inflation
and price shocks, already capture the important aspects of the economy and that
inflation is the main aspect a country wants to control, not primarily output gap. The
DFR might also be reflected in the exchange rate which immediately changes as the
DFR changes. These complex macroeconomic links in the data cannot be fully proven,
but the possibility of potential links reduces the robustness of our method.

Taken together, an important implication of the results is how they portray investor
behavior in pricing redenomination risk. In Italy, the pronounced spikes around major
political transitions illustrate how investors react not to slow-moving fundamentals but
to sudden changes in expected policy direction, suggesting that market-implied
redenomination probabilities rise abruptly when coalition uncertainty increases. For
Germany, the near-zero or negative residual spreads highlight that investors do not
assign meaningful probability to a depreciating exit scenario. Instead, markets appear to
anticipate that any hypothetical redenomination would strengthen the currency. For

                                  27
France, although redenomination premia remain elevated after 2017, the weaker
explanatory power of political variables suggests a more persistent, structural premium
that investors maintain even in the absence of direct political shocks. All in all, these
findings reinforce the view that investors should pay close attention to political climates
as macroeconomic fundamentals alone do not fully capture the nature of
redenomination risk.

B. Robustness Test

In the empirical analysis, OLS is used as the primary estimation method because it
provides consistent and interpretable point estimates for linear models and represents a
very standard approach in literature. However, the classical OLS standard errors rely on
assumptions such as homoskedasticity and no autocorrelation, which are often violated
in monthly economic and financial time series. Inflation, output gaps, and interest rates,
for example, typically exhibit strong autocorrelation.

To assess the robustness of the OLS inference under weaker assumptions, we
complement the baseline results with a moving-block bootstrap (MBB). The MBB is
specifically designed for dependent time series data. Instead of resampling individual
observations independently, the method resamples overlapping 12-month blocks,
thereby preserving the autocorrelation structure of the original series. This makes it a
more appropriate choice than the standard i.i.d. bootstrap for data with autocorrelation
(Bühlmann, 2002).

The bootstrap procedure is repeated 10,000 times for each country. Importantly, the
bootstrap does not replace the OLS coefficient estimates. Instead, the coefficient
estimates remain those from the original OLS regression. What the bootstrap provides
are robust standard errors, z-values, and p-values based on the empirical distribution of
the resampled estimates. This allows us to evaluate whether the statistical significance
of the OLS results is robust when relaxing the classical error assumptions.

Generally, from the tables above, one can reasonably suspect that there is likely
multicollinearity between the variables which reduces the robustness of our research.
There might also be other problems with the data that causes additional assumptions of
the OLS to break. This can be seen through the decrease in the number of significant
variables in the bootstrap results when compared to the OLS results. However, a few
variables remain significant when using bootstrapping. For Germany, inflation remains
highly significant. For Italy, debt GDP is significant at the 10% level and for France the
exchange rate is highly significant.

The political variables lose their significance after implementing bootstrapping. From
our data we therefore cannot conclude that our political variables influence the
redenomination risk. However, since previous research has documented that
redenomination risk is largely event driven (Krishnamurthy et al., 2018), we have strong
reasons to believe that the loss of significance is a data problem rather than proof
against political variables affecting redenomination risk. For parliamentary EU position
and government EU position this might also be because the data from CHES is
infrequent, updated only every 4 years. Although we have argued that political opinions
develop slowly, it is possible that regularly drawn yearly data would better capture the
changes in EU position of political parties.

Note also that we must be careful about the interpretations of the coefficients for the
significant variables for Germany. When discussing the inflation variable, we referred
to Appendix B to gain a clue about if the coefficient is positively or negatively affecting
redenomination risk. For the rest of the significant variables in the regular OLS, these
variables are not significant in the OLS using absolute values and we cannot gain such
clues about the coefficients. The interpretations of the coefficients for Germany in the
discussion assume that a positive coefficient increases redenomination risk as the
variable increases, and vice versa. However, this cannot be proven to be the case from
our analysis.

VI. Conclusion

This thesis set out to examine whether political and macroeconomic variables can help
explain and predict redenomination risk spreads for France, Italy, and Germany. By
combining existing synthetic-control methods with the extension of an OLS framework,
and using updated CDS and political datasets for 2015-2024, we provide new evidence
on how markets price the risk of a euro exit across the eurozone’s three largest
economies. Our main finding shows that redenomination risk is highly country-specific
and largely event-driven rather than persistent. Italy displays the strongest and most
systematic link between political dynamics and redenomination premia, with variables
such as parliamentary EU position and election timing emerging as significant
predictors. Germany, in contrast, shows minimal redenomination risk and only limited
sensitivity to political developments, reflecting its institutional stability and safe-haven
status. France occupies an intermediate position. While its redenomination premium
rises meaningfully after 2017, especially around presidential elections, political and
macroeconomic variables explain this variation to a lesser extent.

Across all three cases, macroeconomic variables provide additional explanatory power.
Specific variables such as inflation and the EUR/USD exchange rate contribute much to
this, but their influence is neither uniform for all countries, or dominant for a specific
one. Instead, our chosen macroeconomic variables seem to be inherently related to
underlying national economic fundamentals. As such, they can amplify redenomination
concerns when the underlying economic environment is fragile or dampen them when
fundamentals are strong.

As for the implications of investors, our findings point to the conclusion that
redenomination risk should be viewed as a political risk rather than a constant
economically fundamental premium. Market-implied redenomination probabilities can
remain dormant for long periods, only to rise sharply in response to election outcomes
or coalition instability. This suggests that a given investor should ideally monitor
political developments in relevant countries, especially those with volatile party systems
or rising Eurosceptic sentiment. This becomes especially salient with the found
implication that macroeconomic fundamentals, although informative, do not fully
capture the dynamics of redenomination risk. This suggests that traditional sovereign-
risk frameworks that prioritize macro fundamentals could theoretically miss the most
important forward-looking variables. A comprehensive financial assessment should
therefore incorporate political risk indicators and institutional credibility in some
capacity.

                                  32
A final implication is that redenomination risk does not always operate in the one-way
direction investors might expect. For countries viewed as safe assets (Germany) within
the euro area, markets may price the possibility that a hypothetical redenomination
would result in a stronger, not weaker, national currency. In such cases, the
redenomination component of the CDS premium can turn negative, reflecting
anticipated appreciation rather than depreciation. As such, investors may use CDS not
only to guard against the risk of default, but also to express views on the relative
valuation of the euro versus an implied shadow currency.

The analysis, however, comes with limitations. Synthetic-control estimates depend on
the traits and behavior of the donor pool and may still contain noise from liquidity
frictions and market microstructure. The MBB reveals that political variables are not
robustly significant, since they do not retain statistical significance after bootstrapping.
The political indicators are measured at relatively low frequency, which restricts their
ability to capture short-term developments. Potential multicollinearity among variables
also constrains the precision of some estimates, and the linear OLS framework may not
fully accommodate the nonlinear, jump-like nature of redenomination risk.

We suggest that future research incorporate higher-frequency political data and perform
statistical diagnostics to mitigate multicollinearity and ensure that the OLS assumptions
hold more closely.

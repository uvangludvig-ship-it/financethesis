Do CEO stock options induce risk-taking?
-  A  study on Swedish companies

Fanny Bsenko* and Simon Karlsson**

ABSTRACT

The use of option-based compensation is a common measure to mitigate agency problems. However,
due to the convex payoff structure  of an option,  concerns have been raised that managers may be
induced  to take on excessive risk. This paper explores the relationship between CEO option grants and
risk-taking in Swedish companies. By using annualized stock volatility and the debt-to-equity ratio as
proxies for firm risk, we can conclude that option grants do not  lead to  any significant increase in  risk-
taking behavior amongst Swedish CEOs. Instead we find that most of the variations can be attributed
to market factors, suggesting either that stock price volatility lies out of the CEO’s control or that
options do not provide sufficient incentives to increase firm risk. We also provide evidence that
options have a negative effect on the firm’s dividend policy as the dividend per share decreases  when
the value of options granted increases. Even though this is has no effect on firm risk it suggest that the
CEO  to some extent  responds to the incentives created by stock options, and strives to maximize
personal wealth.

Keywords:  CEO stock options,  equity  risk, stock volatility, debt-to-equity  ratio, dividend per share

1.  INTRODUCTION
The  use of equity-based compensation  –  and options in particular  –  has  virtually exploded
during the  past decades. Granting key employees a larger equity stake is considered an
effective retention strategy,  but  the  main  cause  for  this development can be attributed to  a
desire to mitigate agency problems.  The idea is to align manager and shareholder incentives
by linking the executives’ personal wealth to the value of equity.  In the US, stock options
grants  grew  to become the largest single component of executive pay  during the late 90’s
(Frydman and Jenter, 2010)  and a similar development could later  be seen in the rest of the
world  –  also to some extent in Sweden. However, in addition to the positive implications of
managerial equity stakes, concerns  have been raised  that  option programs might give rise to
adverse  incentives.

These concerns stems from  the convex payoff structures of employee  stock options. If the
stock price is below the strike price at the time of expiration, the option will be left
unexercised, yielding  a  payoff  of zero.  The upside potential is on the other hand unlimited,
implying that the value of the option will increase with share price volatility.  This might
therefore induce excessive risk taking amongst executives looking to maximize personal
wealth  (Berk and Demarzo, 2014,  p.747-750).

Important to take into account though, is the fact that a chief executive typically also will own
a large equity stake  in the firm  that is not comprised by options. Furthermore,  the longer  his
tenure, the  higher the probability that  the CEO  will be  deep-rooted and  have developed skills
unique to  the firm  (May,  1995).  This implies that a  high amount of his  human capital  also will
be tied to the company. As a result, managers might be reluctant to take on too much risk.

Our  assumption  is that the effect will to a large extent depend on risk-aversion and  the
proportion of stock options  to  total compensation. A risk-averse CEO will  most likely  try to
keep stock volatility at  a minimum, especially if option grants  only represent a small fraction
of his pay  and he has a large equity stake in the firm. If the opposite conditions prevail,
however, we expect to see a significant increase in firm risk.

There are of course other factors that might affect the option-risk-taking relationship and that
potentially could bias the results. Risk-loving CEOs may for  example have a preference for
both high-risk investments and being paid in options. Moreover, the board might introduce a
stock option program with the intention to encourage the CEO to take on riskier projects since
they tend to yield higher returns  when  successful. The decision may also be a result of
changes in company strategy, directly affecting firm risk.

Whether increased firm risk creates or destroys shareholder value is a very disputed argument.
Since the conventional risk-return relationship suggests that increased risk yields higher
payoff in the “good state”, shareholders may benefit more from high risk than managers, who
in addition to their financial wealth also have their human capital invested in the company.
Undiversified, risk-averse managers may therefore turn down projects with a positive NPV if
the negative effect on their expected utility from the increased idiosyncratic risk is negative.

implies that the employee does not receive a benefit  until the stock options  are  actually
utilized to acquire securities  or transferred. In addition, the value of the benefit  will be
unknown  up  until the  point when  the  options are  exercised.

At a good value development in the company, taxes will be high for employee stock option
grants. The higher payroll taxes constitute a risk for the firm that issues the options,  in
particular  for smaller companies that may encounter liquidity problems and  for  private
companies that are unable to purchase its own stock to hedge the future transaction.

The Swedish  income tax rate is dependent on the subject’s annual salary. The upper limit is
616 100 SEK, resulting in a  tax rate of 58-68%,  depending on municipality. Payroll taxes are
31.42%. In addition to this, any dividends and gains or losses  at a future exit are  as earlier
mentioned  taxed as capital gains  at a rate of 30%.  This can be compared to the US option tax
of 15%  (Svenskt  Näringsliv, 2013).

Needless to say, the  unfavorable tax environment can explain why Swedish companies are
more reluctant to make use of option-based  compensation.  The  smaller potential option gain
may also affect  CEO  risk aversion.

This paper aims to explore the link between CEO stock option compensation and risk-taking
in  Swedish  companies.  The  theory  implies  that  the  larger  the  share  of  total  CEO
compensation comprised by options, the greater his incentives to take on more risk.  We would
therefore like to investigate if this theory is applicable to Swedish firms or if factors such as
the high tax rate on options are sufficient to undermine CEO incentives.

We will use volatility as our main measure of risk  –  defined as the annualized standard
deviation of  daily returns of each fiscal year.  We will also look  at increases in leverage, by
exploring the link between stock options and the  debt-to-equity

ratio. As a final test, we will also study the effect on  dividend payouts. Even though cutting
dividends do not directly affect firm risk, it might be an indication that the CEO is trying to
maximize personal wealth. The retained cash is normally used for investments, which could
potentially lead to an increase  in equity volatility.

Our study will be based on two hypotheses, which will help us determine if CEO stock option
grants  gives rise  to increased risk-taking:

Hypothesis 1:  CEO  stock option grants  will have a positive correlation with  stock volatility.

Hypothesis 2:  CEO stock option grants  will  have a positive correlation with  the company’s
debt-to-equity ratio.

To investigate  how the  CEO  responds to the  risk-taking incentives created  by  options, a
number of variables with a proven correlation to firm risk  and risk aversion were included as
controls.  Fixed effects were also incorporated in the regressions  to  help  mitigate the risk of
endogeneity.

                                                                                   5
Our  findings suggest that there is no  significant correlation between  CEO option grants and
risk taking  in Swedish companies. Most of the variations in the dependent variables can be
explained by firm or industry characteristics, time specific effects or factors not captured by
our model.

One plausible explanation  is that the  high option taxation in Sweden  effectively undermines
risk-taking incentives, since the gain from  increased  equity volatility is small compared to the
possible adverse consequences.  Furthermore  –  assuming  that the efficient market hypothesis
does not hold  and all investors are not perfectly rational  –  taking on additional  leverage or
riskier projects does not necessarily lead to higher stock price volatility. The power of the
CEO is in other words limited.

But there  is another way  through  which the CEO can affect the value of his option holdings,
without putting  his human capital and equity stake in the company at risk. As an additional
test, we will therefore explore the relationship between option grants and dividend policy.

Supplementary hypothesis: CEO stock option grants will have a negative impact on
dividend payouts.

Here we find that firms employing CEO stock option programs will  experience  a significant
decrease in dividend  payouts per share  the following year. This relationship holds even after
including  careful controls, such as  additional independent variables  and adjusting for time and
firm fixed effects. The  response to  option grants in  terms  of a more  restrictive  payout policy
can be regarded as an indication that the president  seeks  to maximize personal wealth. Since
the stock price in theory will decrease by the exact amount of a dividend payout, total
shareholder wealth will remain unaffected (solely redistributed). The value of a call option
will on the other hand decrease, providing  a clear incentive to  executives with large option
holdings to cut dividends.

The rest of the paper will be structured as follows.  In the next section, the economic theory on
which the hypotheses are based on is presented, followed by a literature review. A more
detailed description of the dataset  and the reasoning behind the selected control variables can
be found in section 4. The methodology of the study is accounted for in part 5, and the results
are presented in section 6. In part 7 we analyze and interpret our findings  in further detail,  as
well as  explore some possible explanations.  Potential biases and limitations of our study  are
discussed in section 8, followed by a summary of the most important conclusions implied by
this paper.

2. THEORETICAL FRAMEWORK

2.1 BLACK-SCHOLES VALUATION MODEL
The Black-Scholes model  is the most commonly  used formula in option pricing. According  to
the model, as modified by Merton (1973),  the value of an option is contingent on six factors:
The risk-free interest rate (r), the price of the underlying security (S), the exercise price of the

                                                                                   6
option (X), the time to expiration (T-t), the rate of dividend payment of the underlying
security  (d) and the implied volatility (σ).

An  option gives the  holder  the right  –  but not the obligation  –  to purchase or sell a stock  at  a
predetermined price  (the strike/exercise price). Since employee  stock  options are call options,
their value is increasing with the value of the underlying security  and  thus create  an additional
incentive for the manager to increase the company’s stock price  and thereby maximize
shareholder wealth.

In addition to the stock price, option  value is also  contingent  on the so-called risk-free interest
rate,  which is the return an investor would expect from an absolutely risk-free investment
over a specified period of time. In practice, there is of course no such thing as a completely
risk-free  investment, and government treasury bonds are therefore often used as a benchmark
for the risk-free rate.

Two other important determinants of option value are the strike price and the expiration date.
These two factors are defined when the option is  written, do not change over time and are
therefore easily observable. Just as in the case of the risk-free rate, executives cannot one-
sidedly affect these features.

The management do however exercise some control over the two remaining inputs in the
model, dividend payments and equity volatility.  Since options do not carry the rights to
dividends, and the stock price in theory will decrease by the amount of the dividend, it lies  in
the interest of managers with option holdings to reduce the  payout rate. Important to note
though, is that the exercise price may have been adjusted for dividends or that executives
sometimes are compensated for the value reduction caused by dividends.

The call option value also  increases with volatility. Since the holder of the option is under no
obligation to exercise, he will only chose to do so if the strike price is below the market price.
This convex payoff structure creates a clear incentive to increase stock volatility.

2.2 THE PRINCIPAL-AGENT THEORY
This  theory  describes the situation  in which  one party (agent) is expected to act in the  best
interest of another  party  (principal),  but due to a conflict of interest might have incentives to
do otherwise. In finance, the Principal-Agent theory  is most commonly applied  on  the
relationship between managers and shareholders  –  managers taking the role as agents and
shareholders  acting  as  the  principal. The problem arises  due to the separation of control and
ownership. Executives are supposed to  act on behalf of the shareholders  with the goal of
maximizing shareholder  wealth. However, decisions that would  benefit shareholders may not
necessarily lie in the manager’s best interest. In addition to running a successful business,
executives are naturally driven by other self-interests, such as perks and power  (Berk and
Demarzo, 2014, p. 11).

As of today, the Principal-Agent theory is widely recognized, and companies usually
undertake a range of measures to help mitigate the agency problem.  Performance-based
compensation, direct intervention of shareholders and threat  of firing or takeovers are

                                                                                   7
common examples.  Share and option based compensation was introduced as a method to tie
human capital to the organization as well as to reduce the gap between ownership and control.

While options  certainly  will fill the function of closer  aligning managerial and shareholder
incentives, they may also give rise to other concerns relating to the principal-agency problem.
Since the value of options as earlier mentioned will increase with volatility, this will provide
managers with the incentive of taking on more risk to maximize personal wealth. Whether
this increased risk will benefit or harm shareholders is better left unsaid, but in the bad states
of the economy, companies with high leverage and riskier projects are at greater risk of
encountering financial difficulties.

2.3 EQUITY RISK FACTORS
A CEO can affect the equity risk mainly through two measures: taking on riskier projects and
increasing firm leverage.

When in need of new capital it may be  easier for a firm to  take on more debt rather than
requesting  shareholders  to  invest even more in the firm. According to basic economic theory,
an increase in leverage will, holding assets and other factors constant, automatically lead to an
increase in equity volatility. This is due to the fact that more debt constitutes a larger  financial
risk as equity holders’ return will become more sensitive to fluctuations in firm performance.
At a good value development in the company,  the shareholders’ return  will be higher in a
levered  firm than  in an unlevered firm. However, when the opposite situation prevails, e.g. in
a market downturn,  the  shareholders  of  a firm with levered equity  only have a claim on the
earnings residual.  They will, in other words,  receive what is left (if any) after debt holders
have  obtained  their share.  Thus, by increasing the amount of leverage  held by the firm,
shareholders are exposed to more risk  and  will demand a higher  return on their investment. As
a result, the required  equity  risk premium  will increase  (Berk and Demarzo,  2014, p. 481-
482).

3. LITERATURE REVIEW
Numerous studies exploring the relationship between executive stock option programs and
managerial behavior have emerged during the past 30 years. A common theory is that
executives who have been granted stock options take on more risk. This is due to the fact that
the value of an option, according to the Black-Sholes model, increases with the volatility of
the underlying stock (Haugen and Senbet,1981; Smith Jr. and Watts,  1982; Smith and Stulz,
1985). Since a call (put) option will have a positive payoff whenever the share price is above
(below) the strike price and a payoff of zero if the opposite condition prevails, the option
holder faces a limited downside risk and an unlimited upside potential. Consequently, this
convex payoff structure implies that an executive who has been granted employee stock
options  will have incentive  to increase equity volatility  –  and  as a result  the option value  –
since he will not  be at  risk  of  reducing his exiting personal wealth (Tufano,1996).

                                                                                   8
The connection between  executive stock option grants and increased risk-taking behavior has
                                         1
been confirmed in several studies .  For this reason,  stock option grants are commonly used as
a measure to mitigate agency problems. If a manager’s optimal degree of risk-taking is below
what is desirable  to  shareholders, he may choose not to invest in positive net present value
projects even though  shareholder wealth  would increase. By introducing a convex payoff
scheme  –  like that of stock options  –  manager wealth will increase with volatility and thus
induce  them  to  take  on  more  risk,  effectively  aligning  their  interests  with  those  of
shareholders  (Amihud and Lev, 1981, and Smith and Stulz, 1985).

However, the empirical  literature covering this subject  remains  inconclusive. Kane (1985) and
Benston et al. (1986) argues that this theory  is  not applicable  on  all managers,  due to the  large
amount of human capital  they have  tied  to  the  company. The authors  claim that as the
prominence of this  factor  increases, so does also the risk-aversion of  the executive.

Furthermore,  Ross (2004) finds that managers may not always consider an increase  in stock
volatility to be  value maximizing, even if they hold  a large amount of  stock options. This  is
also  in line with the findings of Larraza-Kintana et al. (2007) and Sawers et al. (2011)  who
state  that if the value of an option at grant date is high, executive risk taking decreases. The
reasoning  behind  this  theory  is that  executives may become more loss-averse if the present
value of their options is high,  and  thus  less willing to take on  more  risk since  this  may
adversely  impact  their wealth.  Moreover,  Martin et al. (2013)  has  presented  evidence
confirming  that prospective (current) wealth comprised by stock option can have a positive
(negative) effect on  the risk of the firm.

Another  factor  affecting  equity volatility is the level of debt held by the firm.  Managers with
large option holdings  may  therefore  be  tempted  to take on more leverage to  boost their
personal wealth. There  is  a  great  amount of empirical literature  pointing  towards a positive
correlation between executive  stock options  and level of debt. Galai and Masuis  (1976) as
well as  Leverage Cole, Daniel, Naveen (2004) find  that the  amount of leverage in the firm
increases with the sensitivity of  the  executive’s  wealth  to changes in stock price (vega).
However,  the results are inconsistent  also  in  this area. Lewellen (2006) finds  that most
managers  with  stock option holdings actually become  less motivated to take on  additional
leverage.  She argues  that this  is  due to  the fact that managers, in contrast to shareholders,
often are undiversified with  large  equity  stakes in the  firm, leaving them exposed to
idiosyncratic risk. This can in turn make them reluctant to increase the amount  of leverage
held by the company  as this to a high  extent puts  their  personal wealth  at risk.  The  question of
whether or not stock option grants actually induce risk-taking behavior amongst executives
thus  remains  unanswered.

4. DATA AND COLLECTION PROCESS

4.1 OPTION DATA
The dataset  used  in this study  contains information  on  54  Large Cap  companies listed on the
OMX Stockholm exchange  for  the years 2005-2014.  As discussed previously, stock option
programs are heavily taxed in Sweden and at a good value development  of the stock they  can
be very costly  too  the company. Consequently, the number of  Swedish  firms that employ
stock option programs is low  as  compared to  the rest of the world, and the  fraction decreases
with  firm  size.  By focusing our  research on Large Cap companies we  therefore  target the
section of Swedish business that  most frequently makes use of stock options. Furthermore, by
both including  firms that uses  stock options  grants as a part of CEO compensation  and  firms
with no  stock options  outstanding, differences  in risk-taking  between the two  groups will be
more easily observable.

The  specified  time frame was selected with the intention  to include as many years as possible
with   comparable   information   on   CEO   option   grants.   Our   dataset   therefore   contains
observations from the year  2005  –  which was the first year when Swedish companies where
obliged to report more detailed information on  options granted  –  up until  2014. Even though
several  companies engaged in stock option  programs long before 2005,  few actually reported
this  information  before  the requirement was introduced.  The relatively short timeframe and
the fact that far from all companies approved CEO option grants in all 9 years of course
reduces the quality of  our  dataset  and may produce a less reliable result regarding the impact
of  stock option  programs on  risk taking.

Since there is no database available with information on the value of options  granted to
executives in Swedish listed firms, all option data  –  together  with information on total CEO
compensation  –  have  been collected manually from  the  annual reports and proxy statements.
Most companies provided sufficient information to  obtain  a value  the flows,  i.e. the fair value
of CEO options at grant date,  but the details of the option programs (e.g. grant date, exercise
date and strike price) were harder to obtain. Due to the inconsistencies in the reporting
between different companies,  as well as  between  different years, we  ended up with too many
missing  values relating to  the stock of options outstanding, and therefore decided to exclude
the  variable  from our dataset.  However, having options outstanding  can provide  an incentive
to take on more risk  even if no new options were granted during the previous year. Not taking
this effect into account in our model  could  therefore  potentially  bias the results.

The inventiveness  and creativity  in the Swedish  market is great when it comes to equity-
related incentive programs. Hence, we encountered  instruments with  certain features similar
to those of options. The option classification has been made on the criteria of call option
payoff structure,  since this is the characteristic that would induce less risk-averse behaviour.
In those cases were the delimitation proved problematic, the company was excluded from the
sample to provide a more reliable  outcome.  Financial companies, or banks, were also
excluded from the dataset since they would not be comparable in terms of the level of debt.

                                                                                 10
To construct a more comparable measure of the option flows, we will use the value of options
granted as a percentage of total compensation. Furthermore, it is  reasonable to assume that
CEO risk-taking will be induced by the relative value of options rather  than the absolute
value.

4.2 DEPENDENT VARIABLES
The CEO of a company can affect the equity risk by making  risky investments and decisions
or by increasing the amount of leverage held by the firm.  Our measures of risk and dependent
variables will therefore be stock volatility and debt-to-equity ratio.

As  the  base  for the calculation  of stock  volatility  we made use of the  daily closing prices,
adjusted for dividends and splits. The stock data  was retrieved from Finbas,  a database known
for its trustworthy  and accurate  historical price data.  An annualized volatility measure was
generated by (1) calculating the daily percentage changes in option price, (2) calculating the
annual standard deviation and (3)  applying the square  root rule to obtain the annualized
values.

Our second  proxy for  the level of risk taking is  company capital structure.  Information on
company debt-to-equity ratios was  therefore  collected from Retriever and annual reports.  As
earlier mentioned,  a CEO with large stock option holdings may  be induced to engage in more
risky firm  strategies  like increasing the amount of leverage  held by the company. Thus, the
idea is to observe  the changes over time in the  debt-to-equity ratio as  the  firms are granting
stock options.

4.3 INDEPENDENT VARIABLES AND CONTROLS
To improve the accuracy and explanatory value of our model, control variables with a proven
correlation to  equity risk and risk-aversion will be  incorporated.

4.3.1  INDUSTRY
Industry characteristics have a big impact on firm risk.  Parameters such as demand/supply
outlook, cost structures, competition, required capital investments and business cycles varies
from  industry to industry,  and this  needs to  be  taken  into account when looking at firm
strategies or capital structure.  For instance,  capital-intensive  industries usually exhibit high
debt-to-equity ratios whilst  cyclical businesses tend to keep debt at a much lower level. The
optimal amount of debt held by a company also strongly depends on the certainty and stability
of the cash  flows. The operations of a utility company are for example usually considered
stable, with predictable inflows. A technological company, on the other hand, operates in a
more uncertain environment  (Berk and Demarzo, 2014, p. 417-418).

To control for these differences,  a letter  was assigned  to each company, based on the main
industry classifications provided by Statistiska Centralbyrån (see Table 1, appendix). These
particular industry codes were  selected  due to the fact that  they enabled us to make broad but
accurate  classifications.  Consequently, we were able to group several companies together,
despite the  relatively small  sample size,  enabling  us to see  a  trend more clearly. However, by
using  broader  classifications we may reduce the  accuracy of  differences in specific industries.

                                                                                 11
4.3.2 VALUE OF STOCK HOLDINGS
Increased equity volatility will yield a higher value of options, but also put the value of
current stock holdings at risk. The CEO commonly has a significant ownership stake in  the
firm,  and thus might prefer a more stable value development of the company stock. It is
therefore reasonable to assume that CEO risk-aversion will increase with the value of his
stock holdings, offsetting the risk-inducing effect of option grants.

To control for this in our model, the value of CEO stock holdings, as per 31 December, will
be calculated for each year. The number of securities will be gathered manually from the
annual reports and  multiplied  by the closing price at the end of each year.  To make the figure
more comparable, the  log value will be utilized in the regression.

4.3.3 DAYS AS CEO
As suggested by empirical literature, the number of days since appointed CEO could be used
as a proxy of risk-aversion. For instance, Berger et al. (1997) argues that CEOs with longer
tenures are more likely to be entrenched and will seek to avoid risk. Furthermore, May (1995)
argues that CEOs with more tenure develop skills unique to a specific firm, also making them
more risk averse.  This implies that we might expect to see a tendency towards stability the
longer the president has maintained his position.

To  capture this effect,  a variable containing the number of days since assigned to the CEO
                      st
position at the  31  of  December  of each year  will be introduced. These figures are based on
the  dates  of appointment and  termination  of  each CEO during  our  selected time frame.

4.3.4 FIRM SIZE
Firm size has also been proven to correlate with volatility and firm risk.  However, since  the
study only includes  large cap companies, the  size factor  may not  be as decisive as in more
wide-ranging samples.  To test this hypothesis we collected info on market capitalization,
defined as the share price multiplied by the number of shares outstanding at the end of each
fiscal year, from Retriever and Orbis. After running some preliminary regressions and finding
that the size variable did not contribute any explanatory value to our models, we decided to
exclude it from the dataset.

4.4 DIVIDEND POLICY
Earnings can either  be retained and reinvested in the company, or distributed to shareholders
as earnings.  Even though dividends do not directly affect firm risk, it may still be relevant to
examine the relationship between dividends and CEO option grants  in this study.  Since
options do not carry the right to dividends, and the stock price in theory is expected to
decrease with the amount of the dividend, a higher payout ratio  will translate  into a lower
option value. The total wealth of the stockholders will not be affected  by payout  policy (Berk
and Demarzo, 2014, p. 725).  Consequently, lower dividends in companies with executive
stock option programs, may be an indication of that the CEO is trying to maximize his
personal wealth. Furthermore, retaining  a larger fraction of  earnings increases the investment
capability of the company. CEOs with an incentive to increase equity volatility may therefore
be given more freedom to also engage in riskier projects.

                                                                                 12
To investigate if there is in fact a correlation between option grants and a more restrictive
dividend payout policy, annual data on dividend per share for each company  has been
collected from Orbis. For  years with missing data  the information was retrieved from  annual
reports. The dividend per share will be used as the outcome variable in a model similar to the
main regressions to be able to determine if  option programs affect payout policy in Swedish
companies.

5. METHODOLOGY
This section describes how this  study on the relationship of option grants and CEO risk taking
was conducted. In the  primary  stage, we  tested for multicolliniarity between the independent
variables and the distribution of our variables. Secondly, we test for fixed and random effects
to establish how to best model the regressions.

5.1 PRIMARY TESTS

5.1.1 MULTICOLLINIARITY
The problem of  multicolliniarity  arises when two variables in a regression are so highly
correlated that it becomes difficult to determine which factor in the model is actually
producing the effect on dependent variable.  Higher multicolliniarity also causes higher
standard errors, smaller t-statistics and wider confidence intervals. This implies that  rejecting
the null hypothesis  will  require larger coefficients in  the prevalence of multicolliniarity.

To be able to  single  out  the effect from  value of option grants to total compensation on  the
risk measure,  we  therefore need to establish that the correlation with other variables is not too
high. As can be seen in  Table  2,  the problem of multicolliniarity is not of a concern  in our
case. Our main independent variable is only correlated with number of days since appointed
CEO when disregarding results with a significance level below 95%. This slight negative
correlation is most likely due to the fact that newly appointed CEOs  in  companies making use
of stock option programs often receive the most substantial amount  of options  in their first
year.

5.1.2 DISTRIBUTION OF VARIABLES
When the distribution of a variable is skewed, using the natural logarithm of the variable in
the regression can drastically improve its explanatory value. Furthermore, when the variations
of the dependent variable is  subject  to  percentage changes  in an independent variable (and
vice versa), the correlation will be better explained by taking the log value of one or both of
the variables.

To determine whether log or normal values would yield the better fit in our model, the
distribution of the quantiles of  the variable were plotted against the quantiles of a normal
distribution. As can be seen  from  Graph 1 (appendix), there are significant deviations from
the diagonal line, the “normal”, in the tails for  all 4 variables.  The distribution becomes less
skewed after taking the log value,  which makes them  it more appropriate to  use in the
regression  (see Graph 2, appendix). The same procedure was preformed for the variable
dividend per share and as can be observed in Graph 3  (appendix)  also here  the distribution of
the variable becomes less skewed when the log value is used.

5.2 MODELING THE REGRESSION

5.2.1 PREPARING AND MATCHING THE DATA
An  essential part in modeling the regression is the matching of the data. We seek to explain
how (1) the volatility of the stock price and  how  (2) the level of debt relates to the flow of
option grants, while controlling for other variables that might explain  some of  the variations
in the  dependent  variable. Volatility is  measured as the annualized standard deviation of daily
returns  during year t  and the amount of leverage  is  measured as the debt-to-equity ratio as per
31/12 of  year t.  The explanatory variables are as earlier mentioned also  measured at the year-
end, with the exception of industry, which  is assumed to be constant.  By using the values of
all variables at year t we would inevitably make the assumption that the actions of the CEO is
dependent on  future events and conditions.  To properly model the relationship of the change
in risk-taking behavior with respect to option grants, we therefore need to incorporate lagging
variables in the regression.

To examine if there is a correlation between our measures of risk and option grants, we will
use the percentage share of total compensation at year  t-1 as our predictor. The same will be
true for CEO tenure, i.e. the measure will be defined as the number of days since appointed
CEO at the 1st  of January of year  t.  The stock of options  outstanding will be kept as its value
at t.

Our dataset consists of panel data on 54  companies with observations from the year 2005 to
2014. To enable us to both lag variables and set Stata to handle longitudinal  data, the xtset
command was utilized.

5.2.2 TESTING FOR FIXED AND RANDOM EFFECTS
Before specifying our final regression we conducted a series of tests  to determine which
effects to control for in our model.

                                                                                 14
5.2.2.1  Hausman test
The Hausman test allows us to determine whether to control for fixed or random effects.
Using fixed effects enable us to control for factors unique to each entity that might bias the
regression results. By removing the effect of time-invariant characteristics we can better
assess the net effect of the predictors on the dependent variable. In the random effects model,
we assume that  the variations between the identifiers  are  due to chance and the error terms are
uncorrelated with the predictors in the model. If these conditions prevail, it is in other words
not suitable to incorporate fixed effects.

The results from the Hausman test can be seen in Table 3  in the appendix. In  the case of
                     !
volatility, the Prob>χ  is 0.02. Thus, we can conclude that the fixed effects model  is suitable
in this case.

By deploying a fixed effects model, we will adjust for all time invariant characteristics unique
to a particular firm. The use of industry dummies as an additional control will therefore be
superfluous.

As illustrated in Table 4 (appendix), the Prob>χ  is not significant, implying that random
effects may be more appropriate to explain changes in  the debt-to-equity  ratio.

5.2.2.2 The Breusch-Pagan Lagrange Multiplier test
After establishing that the fixed effects model is not appropriate  for  predicting  the relationship
between the debt-to-equity  ratio and the independent variables, we use the  Breusch-Pagan
Lagrange Multiplier test to  decide  between a random effects regression  and a simple OLS
regression. The  null hypothesis  in the test  is that  there are no significant  variances across
entities, i.e. no panel effect.

Here,  we reject the null  since there is evidence of significant differences across companies
(see Table 5, appendix). Consequently,  the OLS will be biased and  the random effect model  is
more  appropriate to use.

5.2.2.3 Testing for time-fixed effects
Finally, we wanted to test  whether  or not  we would need to control for time-fixed  effects in
our model.  It is reasonable to assume that differences in stock volatility to a large extent also
depend on external macroeconomic factors, such as for instance the state of the economy.
Furthermore, companies may chose to  reduce  their  level of debt in recessions due to the
increased financial risk and higher interest rates  (Berk and Demarzo 2014, p. 550-551).  By
using the command “testparm” in Stata it is possible to perform a joint test of significance on
the time parameters.

In the case of Volatility,  the Prob>F is smaller than  our selected alpha of 5%. We can
therefore safely reject the null hypothesis that the coefficients for all years are jointly equal to
zero,  confirming  that time fixed-effects should  be incorporated into the model  (see Table 6,
appendix).

The opposite is true for  the debt-to-equity  ratio. As can be seen from Table 7 (appendix), we
failed to reject the null and thus no time-fixed effects are needed  in this case.

firm   and   time   fixed   effects   were   included   in   the   model.   Clustering   through   the

                                                                                 24
Huber/White/sandwich VCE estimator also allows us to obtain a heteroskedasticity-robust
result.

The clustering of standard errors allows for correlation between observations within clusters,
which will increase the confidence intervals substantially.  This poses quite a stringent test of
the null hypothesis, implying that the effect of  the independent variable has to be rather
substantial to be deemed significant. Nevertheless, in our case we are able to reject the null on
a significance level of 5%, confirming that stock option grants will induce the CEO to reduce
the following year’s dividend payments to maximize his personal wealth.

Finally, an adjustment of the residuals was preformed as an additional robustness check. The
two observations with the most extreme negative residual values as well as the two with the
most   extreme   positive   residual   values   were   excluded   to   mitigate   issues   related   to
heteroskedasticity. Graph 9 (appendix) displays the residuals versus fitted values plot for the
initial and adjusted values. What becomes evident from the unadjusted graph is that there are
three dots that substantially increase the spread between minimum and maximum values.
Eliminating those outliners will therefore help improve the fit of our model.

Performing the regression on the adjusted dataset yields a similar result as in the initial
outcome  (Table 23, appendix). However, excluding the extreme values allows us to obtain an
even stronger negative correlation as well as a higher R-squared, confirming that the model
now provides a better fit for our data as previously stated.

7. DISCUSSION
In this section the results of our study will be analyzed in greater detail and interpreted.
Furthermore, we explore possible explanations for the outcome, and seek to understand why it
might differ from previous research.

As became evident from the regression results, most of the variation in equity risk can be
attributed to market or industry factors. This can either indicate that option grants in Swedish
companies does not induce the CEOs  to take on more risk, or simply that the determinants of
equity  volatility lies out of  their  control. Most plausible is perhaps a combination of the two.
Option grants usually only constitute a small fraction of total compensation in Sweden, and
the gains are heavily taxed. Taking into account that a CEO generally have  most of his human
capital invested in the company, along with a substantial equity stake, makes the prospective
benefit from increasing firm risk appear small compared to the potential downsides.

According to economic theory, increasing the amount of debt and taking on riskier projects
will have a direct impact on equity volatility. The reality, however, is  a bit  more complicated.
The stock market is a game of expectations. The investors are the players, looking to score a
big win without exposing themselves to unnecessary risk. In an efficient market made up by
rational players, share prices should reflect all the relevant information available. The
problem is that far from all players are rational, and the efficiency of the market can definitely
be questioned. This game of expectations is therefore highly subjective, and since the
investors can diversify their holdings, they only have to base their investment decisions on

                                                                                 25
their anticipations for the market. An increased debt-to-equity ratio or a riskier business may
therefore not translate into a more volatile share price, even though both idiosyncratic risk and
market sensitivity would be higher.

Moreover, it is important not to disregard possible influences by board members and other
key executives. Significant changes in capital structure and taking on very high-risk projects
are most likely a mutual decision and not something the CEO can decide upon alone. Thus, it
reasonable to expect stock option  grants to only have a limited effect on the firm’s overall risk
if the CEO is the only one holding stock options.

The fact that the risk-taking incentives created by options appear to have a greater influence
on the behavior of American CEOs is not very surprising. One plausible explanation is the
differences in compensation policy. As previously mentioned, option grants are regarded as
the rule rather than as the exception in US companies. In contrast, only about 19% of Swedish
Large Cap firms employ executive option programs at a given year, and this fraction
decreases with firm size. The mean value of options, measured as fair value at grant date,
constitutes 17.5% of total compensation, whilst option grants in the US represent the largest
single component of executive pay.

Perhaps even more important are the differences in option taxation. In the US, the benefit is
taxed at a rate of 15%, while Swedish CEOs will have to pay up to 68% (+ an additional 30%
on possible capital gains).

Another characteristic unique to Swedish firms is that option grants  appear to be  quite  a
transient part of the compensation plan. Moreover, we see that the value of option grants in
many cases is very low as compared to total compensation, which raises the question of why
these companies chose to grant options in the first place. It seems unlikely that such a small
fraction of  total compensation made up by  option pay would induce the CEO to take on more
risk, or offer him any economic advantages over cash-  or stock-based compensation. An
investigation on  this topic lies out of the scope of this paper, but the observation in itself
contributes with an interesting aspect to Swedish compensation policy.

As a supplementary test to our main investigation, we examined the relationship between
option grants and  dividend policy. The regression results pointed to a significant negative
correlation between CEO option grants and the dividend per share distributed during the
following year. More specifically, we observe  a  2.36  SEK  decrease  when  the value of options
divided by total compensation increases with 1 percentage unit.

This result indicates that CEOs to some extent respond to the incentives created by stock
options. However, as previously mentioned, most of the value gain from increasing equity
volatility will be devoured by taxes, yielding a negative risk-return  relationship. Cutting the
dividend rate, on the other hand, will increase the value of  outstanding options, leaving the
remainder of personal wealth unaffected. This can therefore be regarded as a far more
attractive approach to boost option value from the perspective of the CEO.

                                                                                 26
8. PROBLEMATIZATION
This essay explores how option  compensation  will affect CEO risk taking  during  the
following year. However, few options vest earlier than 12 months after  the grant, implying
that the typical executive also holds previously granted options. The value of the stock of
options outstanding held by the CEO will needless to say also increase with volatility, which
translates into similar risk-taking incentives.

Simply looking at the effect from the flows might be sufficient to establish  whether or not
there is a link between option grants and increased equity risk, but to properly assess the
magnitude of this effect the value of option holdings should be taken into consideration.

Another concern is the limited dataset. Our sample consists of 54 companies, of which only
29 have approved CEO stock option grants at some point in time between the years 2005 to
2014; a total number of 526  observations. A larger dataset could significantly improve the
reliability of the findings from our study.

9. CONCLUSION
This paper explores how CEOs in Swedish companies respond to the risk-taking incentives
created by stock option grants, where risk is measured as the stock volatility and the debt-to-
equity level in the firm.

We find that differences in stock volatility between firms that grant stock options and firms
that do not are very slight and that changes in stock volatility mainly can be attributed to
market factors. In our  regression model we show that there are no significant correlation
between stock options and volatility after adjusting for fixed effects and time fixe effects.

Running the regression modelled on the relationship between debt-to-equity ratio and option
grants yields a similar result. No significant correlation prevails after controlling for firm and
industry specific factors. Consequently, taking both of these fallouts into account, suggests
that stock option grants in Swedish companies do not induce the CEO to notably increase firm
risk. These findings have several possible explanations, many of them discussed previously in
this paper. Most likely is that the added value to stock options from an increased equity risk is
not sufficient to predominate the possible adverse consequences. This conclusion contradicts
most evidence from previous research preformed on US firms, leading us to conclude that the
high Swedish  option taxation effectively undermines risk-taking incentives.

As a preliminary test to our main hypotheses, the connection between dividend policy and
option programs was explored. Here we find a clear negative correlation between CEO option
grants and dividend per share the following year. This result suggests that the CEO  to some
extent responds  to the incentives created by stock options, and seeks to maximize his personal
wealth.

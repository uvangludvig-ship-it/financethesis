Does a high tax relax income inequality?

Abstract:

This  study examines  the relationship between top marginal state income tax rates and
income inequality across 35 U.S. states from 1990 to 2020.  It employs  a panel dataset and
fixed effects regression models, the study tests whether higher top marginal tax rates are
associated with lower Gini coefficients, controlling for  state-wise  factors such as union
density, unemployment, and state revenue. The results show that progressive taxation is
modestly but consistently linked to reduced income inequality, particularly when labour
institutions and fiscal capacity are  considered. These  findings highlight that while tax
progressivity remains an important redistributive tool, its consequences depend on broader
institutional and structural dynamics.

   State-wise

Introduction
Income inequality in the United States has emerged as a defining economic and political
challenge  of the twenty-first century. It remains front and centre in high-level political
debates as well as in everyday conversations. The widening gap in income distribution,
exacerbated by events such as the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic, has
attracted widespread scholarly and policy attention. Income inequality,  widely  measured
by the Gini coefficient, captures  income  disparities across individuals  or households and
influences a broad range of social and economic outcomes, including consumption
patterns,  economic  opportunity,  and  intergenerational  mobility.  Understanding  and
addressing inequality is  extremely  essential for promoting economic  equity.
Over the past four decades, income inequality in the United States has risen markedly. In
2021, the richest 1% of households earned, on average, almost  139 times more than the
bottom 20%,  a  disparity that has widened  substantially  since the late 1970s  (Economic
Policy Institute, 2022). Between 1979 and 2021, the average income of the top 0.01% of
households grew nearly 27 times faster than that of the bottom 20% (Saez & Zucman, 2016;
Auten & Splinter, 2023). Moreover, geographic income disparities have intensified; from
1980 to 2021, income inequality across U.S. regions increased by over 40%, with per-
capita incomes in the highest-earning areas now more than double those in the lowest
(Frank, 2009;  Chetty et al., 2014).
Figure  1  shows state-wise scatter plots of the  Gini  coefficient over the years 1990-2020
and it clearly shows how inequality is rising in  all  the states.  These  trends not only
emphasize the magnitude of inequality but also  highlight  the  need  to  urgently  examine  the
fiscal and policy tools available to address it, particularly at the state level where significant
fiscal autonomy exists.  A recurring trend seen in  most  states is a sharp decrease in the Gini
coefficient in the early 2000s followed by an increasing trend up to date.  This temporary
dip, from  2001 to 2003, coincides  with the 2001 recession, which reduced high-end labour
and capital income, narrowing the  income distribution. However, the recovery that
followed favoured top earners through rising capital gains and corporate profits, while
median wages stagnated. As the Centre on Budget and Policy Priorities notes, federal tax
cuts in 2001 and 2003 significantly boosted after-tax incomes at the top, contributing to
the resurgence of inequality by the mid-2000s  (CBPP, 2023). However,  individual states
vary a lot in the level of inequality and the taxes imposed at the state level and this
relationship has been largely underexplored.
Fiscal policy,  particularly  in the  U.S. tax system  is  a complex blend of federal, state, and
local policies,  which  plays a critical role in shaping income distribution. States differ
widely in their tax structures, ranging from those with highly progressive income taxes to
those that levy no personal income tax at all. As  argued by  Hacker and Pierson (2010)  tax
policies often reflect broader ideological shifts and  have the ability to   sway elections,
making taxation both a fiscal and a political issue. State-level variation provides a unique
opportunity to assess how progressive taxation affects income distribution within a federal
framework. This study thus focuses specifically on the role of top marginal state income
tax rates as a policy tool to influence inequality

While national inequality trends are well-documented (Piketty & Saez, 2003;  Auten &
Splinter, 2023), the heterogeneity of inequality across individual U.S. states remains less
explored. A federal system like that of the United States allows for considerable fiscal
discretion, creating significant differences in the way states approach redistribution. States
such as California and New York implement steeply progressive income tax systems,
whereas Florida and Texas levy no personal income taxes at all. Understanding the
implications of these structural variations is both empirically valuable and normatively
urgent, as it reveals how different tax designs can either mitigate or exacerbate inequality
within the same national context.
In addition to the variation in personal income tax structures, many U.S. states have
undergone notable shifts in their broader fiscal strategies since the 1990s. Between 1991
and 2001, 41 out of 42 states with corporate income taxes experienced significant declines
in effective corporate tax rates, resulting in an estimated $11 billion reduction in state

revenue by  (  Braunstein, E, 2004).  At the same  time,  an  increasing  number of states have
started relying on  sales and excise taxes, which are inherently regressive, placing a heavier
burden on low  and middle-income earners. This structural shift away from progressive
income and corporate taxation and toward regressive consumption-based models has not
only narrowed states' redistributive capacity but also amplified existing income disparities.
These  developments  highlight  the importance of scrutinizing the role of progressive
income tax instruments  especially,  top marginal rates,  in state-level  efforts to address
inequality.
Against this backdrop, this thesis investigates whether and how top marginal state income
tax rates affect income inequality across U.S. states from 1990 to 2020.  I  constructed a
panel dataset at the U.S. state level, covering the years 1990 to 2020. The goal was to
investigate the relationship between top marginal income tax rates and the Gini coefficient,
controlling for a range of economic and institutional factors across states. There are two
main questions this study aims to answer

Is there any  relationship between progressive taxation in the United States and income
inequality  from 1990?

If such a relationship exists, do higher top marginal income tax rates tend to increase or
decrease income inequality at the state level

look at fiscal policy on a state level as an important tool to curb income inequality.

The focus on top marginal rates is deliberate  as  they target high-income households, which
have disproportionately benefited from recent economic growth  Saez & Zucman (2016).
In contrast to regressive forms of taxation like sales and property taxes, marginal income
taxes serve as more direct and potentially progressive tools for redistribution (Altig &
Carlstrom, 1995; Akhand & Liu, 2002). By analysing the effects of changes in top marginal
tax rates using a state-level panel dataset and fixed effects models, this study aims to isolate
the redistributive impacts of tax policy from broader economic and demographic trends.
The choice of the Gini coefficient as the primary measure of inequality is also intentional.
The Gini coefficient captures disparities across the entire income distribution and is widely
used in both academic research and policy evaluation. Its sensitivity  to distributional
changes makes it a fitting instrument for assessing the nuanced effects of state-level tax
policies on income inequality.
One of the initial findings of this study is that the average Gini coefficient across U.S. states
between 1990 and 2020 stands at  0.5878, highlighting a persistently high level of income
inequality for a developed country. This suggests that inequality is  not only entrenched but
widespread at the subnational level. However, the analysis also reveals that  top marginal
income tax rates exhibit a statistically significant and negative relationship with the Gini
coefficient. Specifically, a one percentage point  increase in a state's top marginal income
tax rate is associated with a  0.00207 reduction  in the Gini coefficient. This effect remains
robust even after controlling for factors such as unemployment, union density, total state
revenue, and expenditure. These findings underscore the continued redistributive relevance
of progressive taxation and suggest that state-level tax policy remains a potent tool for
mitigating income inequality in the United States.
This thesis makes three key contributions. First, it constructs a comprehensive panel dataset
combining economic, political, and institutional variables grounded in theory.  Second, it
estimates the  correlational  relationship  between marginal income taxation and income
inequality using within-state variation over time. Third, it situates its findings within
broader debates on fiscal federalism, redistribution, and political economy, drawing on a
range of foundational studies,  including  Troiano (2017),  Altig and Carlstrom (1995),
Mertens and Montiel Olea (2018), and Saez and Zucman (2014), that are discussed in detail
in the following literature review.
By building on this prior research, this study not only seeks to empirically test the
redistributive  effects  of  top  marginal  tax  rates  but  also  contributes  to  a  deeper
understanding of the structural dynamics that mediate the relationship between taxation
and inequality in the contemporary United States.  This thesis goes through all the relevant
literature and the main issues within the current research which is followed by a detailed
methodology of how this research was conducted. This is followed by a discussion of the
results and then concluding results.
   2.Related  Works
This chapter reviews three major strands in the literature on taxation and income inequality
in the United States. In my view, the literature boils down to three main issues. First, the
historical and theoretical perspectives on how tax policy shapes income distribution.
Second, the empirical analyses that assess the redistributive capacity of tax progressivity,
especially at the state level. Third, institutional critiques that examine the limitations of tax
enforcement and design in achieving equitable outcomes. While each body of work has
yielded valuable insights, relatively few studies have assessed how top marginal income

                                                                       8
tax rates have impacted income inequality in the context of the diverse institutional and
fiscal environments across U.S. states. This thesis contributes to filling this gap by
analysing panel data at the state level from 1990 to 2020.

2.1 Historical and Theoretical Perspectives on Tax Policy and Income Distribution
Thomas Piketty and Emmanuel Saez (2003)  provide one of the most influential historical
accounts of the evolution of income inequality in the United States. Using detailed
individual tax return data from 1913 to 1998, they document a sharp decline in the income
share of the top one percent from  the 1930s to the 1970s. This period was marked by
extremely high marginal tax rates. They then show a reversal beginning in the 1980s, which
coincided with major tax cuts during the Reagan administration. Their core argument is
that tax policy does not merely correlate with inequality trends but plays a causal role in
shaping income distribution. This happens both through direct fiscal effects and through
behavioural responses from high-income earners. Their findings reignited academic and
policy interest in progressive taxation as a serious lever for equity.
Building on this framework,  Saez and Zucman (2016)  shift the focus from income to
wealth. They analyse capitalized income tax data and show that the bottom fifty percent of
Americans own a negligible share of national wealth, while the top 0.1 percent has
accumulated wealth at an exponential rate since the 1980s. One mechanism they identify
is that lightly taxed high incomes tend to compound over time into greater wealth
concentration. They argue that progressive taxation on both income and wealth is essential
to address this dynamic.
Further theoretical support for the centrality of tax policy in shaping inequality comes from
the dynamic general equilibrium model developed by  Hubmer, Krusell, and Smith (2021),
which investigates the drivers of wealth inequality in the United States from 1967 to the
present. Calibrated to match historical U.S. data, their model attributes the post-1980 surge
in wealth concentration primarily to declining progressivity in top income tax rates. The
authors find that reduced tax burdens on high earners increase after-tax savings and capital
returns, compounding long-term disparities. They also emphasize that portfolio return
heterogeneity—where wealthier households consistently earn higher returns—exacerbates
inequality over time. Their simulations show that without increases in tax progressivity,
the wealth share of the top one percent is likely to continue rising over the next several
decades. Although focused on national-level dynamics, their findings reinforce the
relevance of tax progressivity as a causal mechanism, supporting this thesis’s focus on
state-level top marginal income tax rates as a determinant of income inequality.
Brüggemann and Yoo (2015)  extend this line of theoretical inquiry using a general
equilibrium model to evaluate the aggregate and distributional effects of raising taxes on
top earners. Their results show that while taxing the richest one percent can reduce
inequality significantly, the macroeconomic output costs are relatively modest, especially
when revenues are redistributed through lump-sum transfers. This work adds nuance to the
equity-efficiency debate, suggesting that high top-end tax rates may achieve redistributive
aims without severely harming growth. These findings further support the theoretical
rationale for evaluating top marginal income tax policy in a state-level context.
While these studies clearly demonstrate the importance of federal tax policy in shaping
national inequality, they do not examine variation across states. States in the United States
have substantial autonomy to set income tax rates, especially for high earners. This thesis

                                                                       9
investigates whether the national-level theories proposed by Piketty, Saez, and Zucman
hold when tested against state-level data. By using a panel of U.S. states from 1990 to
2020, I examine whether higher top marginal income tax rates are associated with  changes
in state-level Gini coefficients. This empirical test brings these broad historical and
theoretical arguments into a more localized and varied institutional context.

2.2 Empirical Analyses of Tax Progressivity at the State Level
The second strand of the literature consists of empirical studies that use cross-sectional or
panel data to test whether progressive taxation reduces income inequality. Akhand and Liu
(2002)  analyse  data from all 50 states between 1977 and 1991. They estimate fixed effects
regressions that control for time-invariant state characteristics. Their key finding is that the
progressivity of marginal tax rates, rather than the average tax burden, has a statistically
significant and negative association with income inequality. They argue that tax systems
with steeper rate schedules compress income disparities more effectively. However, the
study  is  limited  to  an  earlier  policy  environment  and  does  not  reflect  post-1990
developments.  Similarly,  Leigh (2008)  uses a longer panel of U.S. states and finds that
more progressive state income tax structures are associated with reductions in income
inequality, further reinforcing the empirical consensus that marginal tax progressivity plays
a meaningful redistributive role.
Bishop, Formby, and Thistle (1992)  also use state-level data but focus on structural
economic and demographic factors rather than tax policy directly. They find that variables
like per capita property income, levels of educational attainment, and public education
expenditure  are  significant  predictors  of  state-level  income  inequality.  Their  work
highlights the importance of including broader structural controls in any empirical model
attempting to isolate the effect of tax policy.
Mertens and Montiel Olea (2018)  use national time-series data from 1946 to 2012. They
employ a narrative identification strategy to isolate exogenous tax shocks specifically
changes in marginal rates unrelated to current macroeconomic conditions. Their results
show that cuts in top marginal tax rates lead to disproportionately large increases in the
reported incomes of high earners. These income responses are largely attributed to tax
avoidance and income shifting rather than actual economic productivity gains. Their work
reinforces the  idea that reductions in top marginal tax rates disproportionately benefit the
wealthy and have limited trickle-down effects.
A more recent contribution by Zidar (2019) strengthens this empirical strand by examining
how the distribution of tax relief across income groups influences economic outcomes.
Using a shift-share design that exploits variation in income composition across U.S. states,
he finds that income tax cuts  for the bottom 90 percent of earners lead to significantly
higher employment growth, while tax cuts for the top ten percent, and especially the top
one percent, have little to no effect on job creation. These findings challenge the
assumption that top-end  tax cuts drive broad economic gains and instead suggest that
targeting lower-income groups yields more robust economic benefits. Although Zidar’s
analysis focuses on federal tax policy, the framework is relevant for understanding state-
level variation as  well. His evidence supports the idea that the effectiveness of tax policy
depends not only on the rate structure but also on who it benefits. This thesis builds on that
insight by investigating whether higher top marginal state income tax rates, which are

                                                                      10
designed to apply to high-income earners, are associated with reductions in pre-tax income
inequality across U.S. states.
Parsons and Naghshpour (2022)  take a comparable approach, estimating the effects of
income tax progressivity on Gini coefficients using fixed-effects panel data across states.
Their findings corroborate the hypothesis that progressive tax structures help compress the
income distribution, and their empirical strategy closely parallels the modelling framework
used in this thesis.
This thesis builds on these findings by combining the panel approach of Akhand and Liu
with the structural control framework of  Bishop et al  1992.  and the policy-specific focus
of Mertens and Montiel Olea.  A  state-level fixed effects model covering 1990 to 2020  is
constructed  and  focuses  specifically on  top marginal income tax rates. This choice isolates
the redistributive intent of state policy. In addition  to  the  core tax variable, I include
controls for union density, state expenditure, GDP/ capita and employment rates to account
for broader determinants of inequality. The central question is whether states with more
progressive   top-end   tax   policies   exhibit   systematically   different   inequality   trends
compared to those with flatter tax structures.

2.3 Institutional Critiques of Tax Design and Enforcement
The third and final strand of literature highlights institutional limitations in the enforcement
and design of state tax systems.  Troiano (2017)  presents a compelling argument that
increases in state income tax rates are not always associated with reductions in inequality.
Using panel data across states, he finds that sometimes inequality rises following tax hikes.
He identifies two mechanisms. First, enforcement disproportionately targets salaried
middle-income earners whose wages are reported by third parties, while higher-income
individuals can engage in legal tax avoidance and wealth shielding. Second, the allocation
of tax revenues may primarily benefit already advantaged groups, by focusing on investing
toward public universities thereby reducing the redistributive impact as these benefit the
rich most.
A prominent institutional critique of state-level taxation comes from the  Institute on
Taxation and Economic Policies  Who Pays?  (2024) report, which presents one of the most
comprehensive distributional analyses of state and local tax systems in the United States.
The report finds that in 44 states, the poorest 20 percent of residents pay a higher share of
their income in taxes than the  richest one percent  primarily due to regressive consumption-
based taxes such as sales and excise taxes. On average, low-income families pay 11.4
percent of their income in state and local taxes, compared to just 7.2 percent for the top one
percent. These disparities are most pronounced in states with no income tax or flat income
tax  systems  that  rely  heavily  on  consumption  taxes,  such  as  Texas,  Florida,  and
Washington. The report highlights how state tax policy design  especially  the balance
between income versus sales taxation can undermine progressivity, even when nominal top
marginal rates appear progressive. This evidence reinforces Troiano’s (2017) argument that
the redistributive capacity of tax policy hinges not only on statutory rates but also on the
institutional configuration of tax systems and their enforcement mechanisms.
Altig  and  Carlstrom  (1999)  approach  the  issue  from  a  macroeconomic  modelling
perspective. They simulate a life-cycle general equilibrium model and find that progressive
marginal tax rates can reduce lifetime income inequality. Their model suggests a trade-off:

                                                                      11
beyond a certain point, steeper tax rates may harm economic output more than they reduce
inequality.
This thesis incorporates these critiques by not assuming a simple linear relationship
between top tax rates and inequality. Instead,  it  controls  for enforcement-relevant variables
at the state level, including  state expenditure,  GDP  per capita, unemployment rates  and
union rates. These controls act as proxies for how tax policy is implemented, who bears
the real burden, and where tax revenues are channelled. By doing so, the empirical strategy
remains  sensitive  to  both  institutional  asymmetries  and  behavioural  responses,  as
highlighted by Troiano and Altig and Carlstrom.
Consequently, the existing literature on taxation and income inequality in the United States
can be grouped into three main strands: historical and theoretical arguments linking tax
policy  to  distributional  outcomes,  empirical  studies  that  assess  the  effect  of  tax
progressivity using state-  and national-level data, and institutional critiques that question
the real-world effectiveness of tax systems due to enforcement and design limitations. Each
strand contributes critical insights, but few studies have integrated these perspectives to
evaluate the role of top marginal income tax rates in shaping inequality at the state level
over an extended period. This thesis aims to bridge that gap. By leveraging panel data from
1990 to 2020 and incorporating both structural and institutional controls, it tests whether
more progressive top-end tax policies are consistently associated with lower income
inequality across U.S. states. The following chapter outlines the data sources and
methodological framework used to carry  out this analysis.

   3.  Data and Methodology

3.1 The aims of this study
This study constructs a panel dataset at the U.S. state level, covering the years 1990 to
2020. Data was retrieved from various official and publicly available sources and compiled
into a unified dataset  in a Microsoft  Excel spreadsheet in the format required to run panel
data regressions  using R.  Marginal Income taxes per state and Gini coefficient were
manually compiled. Web scrapping was employed to compile data for state GDP and
population while union density and state expenditure were manually entered.  The goal was
to investigate the correlation between top marginal income tax rates and the Gini
coefficient, controlling for a range of economic and institutional factors across states. There
are two main questions this study aims to answer

    1.   Is there a relationship between progressive taxation in the United States and
        income inequality over a 30-year period?

    2.   If such a relationship exists, do higher top marginal income tax rates tend to
        increase or decrease income inequality at the state level?

   Top Marginal Income Tax  Rates

                                                                                       1
State-level top marginal income tax rates were obtained from the OpenICPSR  Project.
This dataset provides harmonized, annual information on the highest statutory personal
income tax rate applicable in each U.S. state. The measure captures the top bracket in the
individual income tax system and reflects changes over time and differences across states.
The tax rate is expressed in  percentage points, allowing for standardized cross-state
comparisons. These values serve as the study’s main explanatory variable, enabling the
investigation of whether progressive tax policy reduces income inequality.

   Gini  Coefficient

                                                 2
Income inequality is measured using the Gini coefficient, sourced from Mark W. Frank’s
dataset (2009). These annual, state-level Gini coefficients are derived from gross income
reported on IRS tax returns, which include wages, business income, dividends, interest,
and rental income, but exclude government transfers and tax-exempt income. The Gini
coefficient ranges from  0 (perfect equality) to 1 (perfect inequality)  and reflects pre-tax,
pre-transfer income dispersion. Because the values are based on administrative rather than
survey data, this measure provides an accurate picture of top-end income concentration. It
is well-suited for longitudinal state-level analysis.

Total state expenditure figures were sourced from the Urban Institute’s State and Local
Finance Initiative. These figures capture annual government spending across functions like
education, health, and welfare, and are reported in  inflation-adjusted 2022 U.S. dollars.
Including this measure accounts for variation in the redistributive role of the state and helps

   Union Density

Union density is defined as the  percentage of wage and salary workers in each state who
are union members. This data helps control for institutional  labour  market dynamics.
Empirical research, including Hayes and Vidal (2015), has shown that higher union density
is associated with lower income inequality and greater wage compression. This variable
accounts for structural conditions that influence the earnings distribution across states.

   Unemployment Rate

Unemployment rates were also taken from FRED and are expressed as the  annual average
percentage of the state’s labour force that is unemployed. This variable controls for cyclical
economic conditions that directly impact household income and inequality. It follows the
approach of  Hubmer et al. (2021), who include unemployment to isolate the institutional
and fiscal effects on inequality.

3.5  Variable Construction and Panel Setup

This study constructs a novel panel dataset by compiling, cleaning, and organizing state-
level data from multiple public sources for the period 1990 to 2020.  The independent
variable was the marginal income tax, and the dependent variable was the Gini Coefficient.
Data for Union density, GDP/Capita, Employment Rates and  State expenditure was used
as control variables to isolate the relationship between the dependent and independent
variable.

Data were gathered using a mix of direct downloads, web queries, and manual formatting.
The dataset includes variables such as the GINI coefficient, top marginal income tax rates,
unemployment rates, union density, GDP  per capita, total revenue, and total expenditure.
Where numerical values were embedded in text formats, such as in union density and
financial indicators, they were extracted and standardized for analysis. Instances of zero
entries in revenue fields were treated as missing data, on the assumption that these reflected
incomplete or placeholder records.

To ensure meaningful empirical variation, fifteen states were excluded from the analysis
because they either did not levy a state-level marginal income tax or maintained a constant
top marginal rate throughout the study period. These states were identified  and filtered
based on the mean and variance of their top tax rates, ensuring that the regression results

                                                                      15
were not distorted by non-informative cases. The final dataset contains 1,116 state-year
observations across the remaining 35 states.

Panel data regression models were constructed in R, with both  state and year fixed
effects  to control for unobserved heterogeneity across states and national shocks that may
influence income inequality. Control variables  such as union density, unemployment rate,
GDP per capita, and total revenue  were progressively introduced to improve model
robustness and account for structural and economic factors that may confound the
relationship between tax rates and inequality. Table 3.1 presents descriptive statistics for
all variables used in the analysis, which demonstrate adequate variation across both states
and time, validating the use of fixed-effects panel regression as the primary empirical
strategy.

3.6 Acknowledging  Data Limitations:
While the datasets used in this study are well-established and publicly available, there are
some important limitations to consider. The Gini coefficient, although widely used to
measure inequality, tends to be more responsive to changes in the middle of the income
distribution than at the very top. This means it can miss rising inequality among the
wealthiest groups (Cowell and Flachaire 2007). In addition, as Troiano (2017) and Saez
and Zucman (2014) explain, high-income individuals often avoid taxes through methods
like income shifting or placing money in offshore accounts. As a result, income based on
tax return data may understate the actual level of inequality. Also, while total state
expenditure is included to account for overall public spending, it does not capture how that
money is used. For example, Troiano (2017) points out that higher education spending
often benefits higher-income groups more. The analysis is also limited to the years 1990 to
2020. While three decades is a meaningful period for observing long-term trends, the study
could not extend further back or forward due to data availability constraints. Finally, fifteen
states were excluded from the analysis because they either had no state-level marginal
income tax or showed no variation over time. This can be observed in the scatter plots for
top marginal tax rates for each  state  in  figure  1  in  the appendix.  These  include Alabama,
Alaska, Florida, Louisiana, Mississippi, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Carolina, South
Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, Washington, West Virginia, and Wyoming. Dropping
these states was necessary for the identification strategy but limits how much the findings
can be generalized to states without progressive income tax systems. These limitations help
contextualize the scope of the analysis and highlight the importance of interpreting the
results within the boundaries of the available data.

        4.1 Bivariate Regression: Tax Rate and Gini Coefficient

        The first model is a bivariate regression in which the top marginal income tax rate is the
        sole  explanatory variable. The estimated coefficient is  –0.002071 and is statistically
         significant at the 10 percent level. This result suggests that, on average, a one percentage
        point increase in the top marginal income tax rate is associated with a 0.00207-point
         reduction in the Gini coefficient. Although the magnitude of the effect is relatively small,
        the direction of the relationship is consistent with economic theory and prior empirical
        findings. The result closely aligns with the work of Piketty and Saez (2003), who document
         how reductions in top income tax rates throughout the late twentieth century contributed to
         a sharp increase in top income shares in the United States.

        This finding also resonates with the study by Akhand and Liu (2002), which emphasizes
         that the degree of tax progressivity, rather than the average tax burden, plays a central role
         in reducing income inequality. By focusing on the top marginal income tax rate, this study
         isolates a specific component of the tax code that is designed to target high-income earners,
        who have been the primary drivers of income concentration in recent decades.

        However, several important considerations should be noted. As a simple bivariate model,
         this regression does not control for other relevant economic, institutional, or demographic
        variables that may confound the observed relationship. The relatively modest  size of the
        coefficient may reflect behavioural responses to taxation, such as income shifting, legal tax
        avoidance, or changes in reporting behaviour among top earners. These behaviours are well
        documented in the empirical literature. For instance, Mertens and Montiel Olea (2018)
         show that reductions in top tax rates often lead to large increases in reported income among
         high earners, which may not reflect real productivity gains but rather strategic responses to
         tax incentives.

The statistical significance at the 10 percent threshold also warrants cautious interpretation.
While the result is suggestive, it lacks the robustness typically associated with stronger
significance levels. It is possible that state-specific anomalies or  omitted variable bias may
be influencing the estimate. Nonetheless, the negative sign of the coefficient is consistent
with international evidence. Piketty and Zucman (2016), for example, demonstrate that
countries   which   maintained   progressive   taxation   throughout   the   postwar   period
experienced lower levels of both income and wealth inequality. In contrast, the United
States, which significantly reduced top marginal tax rates beginning in the 1980s, saw a
substantial rise in top-end income concentration.

Overall, this preliminary model provides initial empirical support for the hypothesis that
higher top marginal income tax rates are associated with lower levels of income inequality.
It reinforces the theoretical claim that progressive taxation can play a  redistributive role in
shaping pre-tax income distributions. At the same time, the limitations of this model
highlight the importance of incorporating additional controls to produce more reliable and
nuanced estimates. The following models expand on this foundation by sequentially adding
labour  market, institutional, and fiscal variables.

4.2 Adding Unemployment Rate

The second model introduces the unemployment rate as a control variable in order to
account for cyclical  labour  market conditions that may influence income inequality. Once
unemployment is included in the regression, the coefficient for the top marginal income
tax rate becomes more negative, with an estimated value of  –0.002361. It is now
statistically significant  at the 1 percent level, suggesting a stronger and more robust inverse
relationship between top tax rates and income inequality after adjusting for  labour  market
fluctuations. This reinforces the earlier finding that progressive tax policy is associated
with lower levels of pre-tax income concentration.

The unemployment rate itself enters the model with a statistically significant coefficient of
–0.0018. This result indicates that a one percentage point increase in the unemployment
rate is associated with a 0.0018-point decline in the Gini coefficient. Although this
relationship appears counterintuitive at first glance, it is important to note that the Gini
measure used in this study captures  pre-tax, pre-transfer  income inequality. During
periods of economic downturn, high-income individuals often face substantial losses in
capital income, business earnings, or bonuses, which may lead to a narrowing of income
disparities in gross terms. This phenomenon has been observed in prior research, where
recessions temporarily reduce top income shares due to greater exposure to volatile income
sources among the wealthy.

Nevertheless, it is important to distinguish between gross income and disposable income
inequality. While pre-tax measures may exhibit compression during recessions, rising
unemployment is widely understood to increase inequality in after-tax or consumption-
based measures of well-being. Lower-income households tend to experience deeper and
more persistent income shocks, and they have fewer financial buffers to absorb economic

                                                                      19
losses. In this context, the negative relationship observed in this model may reflect a
statistical artifact of pre-tax measurement, rather than an indication of improved economic
equity.

The addition of the unemployment rate serves two key purposes. First, it controls
macroeconomic volatility that may otherwise bias the estimated effect of tax policy.
Second, it highlights the complex interaction between  labour  market dynamics and
inequality. While tax rates appear to maintain their negative relationship with the Gini
coefficient even after controlling for unemployment, the model also shows that  labour
market conditions themselves exert an independent influence on the income distribution.

This result underscores the importance of incorporating relevant economic controls when
estimating the determinants of inequality. It also sets the stage for further refinement in the
next specification, which introduces union density as an institutional variable reflecting the
bargaining power of workers.

4.3 Adding Union Density

The third specification introduces union density as a control variable to better account for
institutional  labour  market dynamics. Unionization plays a crucial role in shaping income
distribution through its impact on wage setting, labour protections, and the broader political
economy. By including union density, the model assesses whether the redistributive effects
attributed to progressive taxation remain statistically significant when institutional  labour
strength is  considered.

In this model, the coefficient for the top marginal income tax rate declines substantially in
magnitude, falling to  –0.000597, and becomes statistically insignificant. This attenuation
suggests that part of the variation previously attributed to tax policy  may, in fact, be due to
differences in union strength across states. States with stronger unions often also feature
more  progressive  political  environments,  which  jointly  influence  tax  policy,  wage
structures, and social spending. The correlation between unionization and tax progressivity
likely results in multicollinearity, diminishing the independent explanatory power of each
variable when included together.

Union density itself shows a large and statistically significant negative effect on income
inequality. The estimated coefficient is  –0.0071, and it is significant at the 1 percent level.
This implies that a one percentage point increase in union membership  among wage and
salary workers is associated with a 0.0071-point decline in the Gini coefficient. This is a
meaningful reduction, and the result is consistent with the findings of Western and
Rosenfeld (2011), who argue that unions not only raise wages for  their members but also
establish normative wage-setting institutions that reduce wage dispersion throughout the
labour  market. Their study finds that the decline of unions in the United States has
contributed substantially to the rise in wage inequality, particularly among non-college-
educated men.

                                                                      20
The continued statistical significance of the unemployment rate, with a positive coefficient
of 0.0012, underscores the persistence of cyclical  labour  market forces in shaping
inequality. Taken together, the results suggest that  labour  institutions and macroeconomic
conditions both exert strong and independent effects on income distribution. They also
reveal that tax policy alone may be insufficient to mitigate inequality if it is not
accompanied by institutional mechanisms like unions  that increase bargaining power and
compress the wage structure from below.

4.4 Full Model: Adding Total Revenue

The final model includes all key explanatory and control variables: top marginal income
tax rate, unemployment rate, union density, and total state revenue. The inclusion of total
revenue, expressed in real terms, serves as a proxy for the fiscal capacity  of state
governments  and  their  potential  to  redistribute  income  through  spending.  In  this
specification, the coefficient for the top marginal income tax rate regains statistical
significance at the 1% level (p < 0.01), with a value of  –0.0018. This reinforces the idea
that accounting for omitted fiscal variables helps isolate the true effect of tax progressivity
on inequality.

The unemployment rate remains statistically significant, with a coefficient of 0.0016,
indicating that a one percentage point increase in unemployment is associated with a rise
in the Gini index of approximately 0.0016 points. This further confirms unemployment as
a key macroeconomic determinant of inequality across all specifications.

Union density continues to have a statistically significant and negative effect (p < 0.01),
with a slightly smaller coefficient of  –0.0057 compared to the earlier model. This
attenuation likely reflects shared explanatory power with the total revenue variable.
Nonetheless, the result confirms the enduring influence of unionization on reducing
inequality.

Interestingly, the total revenue variable enters the model with a positive and statistically
significant coefficient, albeit with an extremely small magnitude. On the surface, this
appears counterintuitive, as higher state revenue is generally expected to be associated with
lower inequality due to increased fiscal capacity for redistribution. However, this result
may not indicate a failure of taxation  itself but  rather point to deeper reallocation issues
within states. That is, states with high revenues may  allocate a significant portion of their
budgets   toward   functions   such   as   higher   education,   infrastructure,   or   corporate
incentives—areas that disproportionately benefit upper-income groups. In such cases,
revenue generation may enhance state capacity without achieving redistributive outcomes.

This interpretation aligns with Troiano (2017), who finds that increases in state income tax
revenue do not always reduce inequality due to two key institutional constraints. First,
enforcement disproportionately targets middle-income salaried workers whose earnings
are automatically reported, while wealthier individuals are more likely to engage in legal
tax avoidance or income shielding. Second, public spending is often allocated in ways that
do not benefit lower-income populations, for instance  when state  governments invest in
flagship universities or property-based development programs. In other words, progressive

                                                                      21
tax rates must be matched by equitable spending practices to achieve redistributive goals.
This study's finding on total revenue thus reinforces the importance of not just how much
is collected, but how effectively that revenue is deployed to address inequality.

4.5  Summary of Results

The sequential regressions in this study yield several robust and theoretically grounded
insights. First, there is consistent evidence that higher top marginal income tax rates are
associated with lower income inequality, particularly when institutional and fiscal factors
are properly controlled for. Second, both union density and unemployment rate emerge as
key structural determinants of income distribution. The negative effect of unionization and
the positive effect of unemployment on inequality are stable across specifications and align
with broader economic theory.

Finally,   the   ambiguous   role   of   total   state   revenue   highlights   the   importance   of
distinguishing between revenue capacity and redistributive intent. High revenues alone do
not guarantee equitable outcomes; the composition of tax instruments and expenditure
patterns also matter. These findings confirm the multidimensional nature of inequality and
suggest that tax policy, while important, must be understood in conjunction with  labour
institutions and fiscal capacity.

Future research could extend this analysis by incorporating more granular spending data,
testing interactions between tax and institutional variables, or employing instrumental
variables to better identify causal effects. Nonetheless, the results of this study contribute
to the growing literature affirming the redistributive role of progressive taxation and labour
institutions in shaping the income distribution across U.S. states.

5.Conclusion

This thesis set out to examine the relationship between top marginal income tax rates and
income inequality across U.S. states from 1990 to 2020. Using a state-level panel dataset
and fixed effects regression models, the analysis found that higher top marginal tax rates
are generally associated with lower levels of income inequality, as measured by the Gini
coefficient. However, this relationship is modest in magnitude and sensitive to the
inclusion of institutional and economic control variables such as unemployment, union
density,  and  total  revenue.  These  findings  reaffirm  the  redistributive  potential  of
progressive taxation but also highlight that tax policy alone is not a panacea for the deeply
entrenched patterns of inequality observed in the United States.

One of the most important implications of this research is that addressing income inequality
in the U.S. requires a  multi-level governance approach. While the federal government sets
broad fiscal and  redistributive norms, states possess significant autonomy in determining
income tax structures. This autonomy has led to considerable divergence in tax policy,

                                                                      22
which in turn shapes the distribution of income across regions. Yet this decentralization
also introduces challenges for empirical analysis and policy design. For instance,  interstate
migration of high-income earners—a behavioural response to differences in state tax
regimes  cannot be fully captured in this dataset. If wealthy individuals relocate to low-tax
states, it could obscure or dilute the redistributive impact of high marginal rates in high-tax
jurisdictions. Future research should attempt to account for such mobility through
longitudinal tracking or micro-level data.

Moreover, this thesis stresses the  importance of interpreting state-level tax policy in
context.  Progressive taxation can reduce inequality, but its efficacy depends heavily on
complementary institutional factors, such as enforcement capacity, the strength of labour
unions, and how tax revenues are allocated. For example, a state may have high top
marginal rates, but if tax enforcement disproportionately targets middle-income earners or
if revenues are channelled toward programs that benefit higher-income groups, the net
redistributive effect may be limited. The modest size of the coefficients in the final
regression models suggests that  other structural forces  such as labour market dynamics,
capital accumulation, and demographic change—play a significant role in shaping income
inequality. These findings echo the institutional critiques in the literature and underscore
the limits of purely statutory reforms.

The results of this study contribute to a more nuanced understanding  of  fiscal federalism
and redistribution in the United States. They show that while state tax policy matters, its
influence must be evaluated considering broader economic, political, and institutional
ecosystems. A meaningful national strategy to reduce inequality must therefore involve
both federal oversight  and  state-level coordination,  especially in designing policies that are
both progressive and enforceable. Encouraging greater transparency, harmonization of tax
codes, and targeted social spending could amplify the redistributive effect of state-level
efforts.

In sum, this thesis provides empirical support for the idea that  progressive top marginal
income tax rates are associated with lower income inequality, but it also cautions against
over-reliance on taxation alone. Income inequality is a multifaceted problem, requiring
coordinated responses that span tax policy,  labour  regulation, education funding, and
institutional reform. Future research should explore these intersections more deeply,
particularly by integrating administrative data on migration, spending breakdowns, and tax
compliance. Such efforts would help build a  more holistic understanding of how policy
instruments interact to shape economic outcomes across America's federal landscape.

6.Policy Recommendations

At the national level, recent discussions have re-emerged around extending or renewing
components of prior tax reform legislation that reduced corporate tax rates and top marginal
income tax burdens. These reforms, originally intended to encourage investment and
economic growth, have yielded mixed outcomes, particularly regarding their long-term
effects on income distribution. In this context, the findings of this thesis suggest that it is
timely to evaluate how progressive taxation could serve as a more effective tool in

                                                                      23
addressing persistent income inequality. States may consider reassessing the structure of
their top marginal tax rates not only as a revenue mechanism but also as part of broader
equity objectives. As recently reported by  the Financial Times, current federal proposals
aim to raise the top income tax rate for individuals earning above two and a half million
dollars and direct the additional revenue toward middle income tax relief and healthcare
funding. This reflects a broader shift toward using tax policy not only to raise revenue but
also to reduce disparities across income groups.

To build on this effect, states should expand top income tax brackets for ultra-high earners
and implement enforcement tools such as third-party income reporting and state-level
minimum tax rules to limit avoidance. Equally important is the allocation of revenue. Tax
policies that are accompanied by targeted public investment in education, healthcare, and
infrastructure   may   enhance   economic   opportunity   and   reduce   disparities   more
meaningfully. In high population regions where inequality is often more visible and
concentrated, attention to income distribution in budget decisions is critical. Policies that
support unionization and expand collective bargaining can help raise wages and reduce
dispersion across industries. States should repeal right to work laws, promote sectoral
bargaining, and ensure minimum wages keep pace with inflation. Pay transparency laws
can also help close persistent wage gaps. On the fiscal side, states  should shift from
regressive sales taxes to more progressive models such as expanding earned income tax
credits and taxing luxury goods and high-end real estate. Public revenues should be
directed   toward   equity   focused   investments   like   universal   preschool,   tuition   free
community  college,  housing  support,  and  mass  transit  which  enhance  long  term
opportunity. Federal state coordination is essential to ensure consistency with conditional
funding tied to equitable tax and  labour  policies and multistate agreements to prevent tax
base erosion. Together these measures can amplify the redistributive effect of taxation
while addressing the structural foundations of inequality.

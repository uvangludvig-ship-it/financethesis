 International capital in Swedish businesses: A study of
  the relationship between foreign ownership and firm
                   performance

                       Erik Fogelström* and Martin Schönning**

                           Stockholm School of Economics

                                   May 14, 2018

                               Abstract

The purpose of this study is to examine if a relationship exists between foreign ownership
and the performance of Swedish companies and to explore the properties of this relationship.
The relationship is studied by measuring performance in terms of Return on Assets as well as
Approximate Q. Our study shows that a positive, contemporaneous, and significant
relationship exists between foreign ownership and Approximate Q, and that the relationship
between foreign ownership and Return on Assets is insignificant. The study further aims to
answer the question whether the relationship between foreign ownership and firm
performance in Swedish firms varies depending on what type of industry the firms under
investigation operates in. Our results indicate that the relationship differs for Approximate Q
depending on what industries the firms are active in, and shows an insignificant relationship
for all industries between foreign ownership and Return on Assets. We conduct the study by
looking at a comprehensive dataset of listed Swedish companies on the OMX Stockholm
between the years 2001 – 2016.

Keywords: Approximate Q, Firm performance, Foreign ownership, Ownership structure,
Return on assets.

1. Introduction

The share of foreign ownership on the Stockholm Stock Exchange has increased during the
last two decades and accounted for more than a third (c. 41.3%) of the total ownership at the
end of 2017 (Euroclear Sweden, 2018), compared to approximately a quarter (c. 25.5%) of
the total ownership in 2001 (Holdings, 2018). The development is displayed in Graph 1.
This trend has caused an ongoing debate of concern in Sweden, due to increased foreign
control over domestic businesses and exposure to foreign entities. It is a controversial subject
that raises the question whether or not this trend needs to be regulated to protect Swedish
interests, and what effects a free flow of capital between economies can have. Thus, a study
such as ours that examines if foreign ownership is associated with the performance of
Swedish firms, is relevant for the debate and its conclusions could contribute with research
that is objective and empirically rooted. Furthermore, the result is relevant both in the view of
Sweden’s competitiveness on a global scale and from the individual firm’s perspective, as it
could potentially suggest whether it is motivated to encourage the entrance of international
investors to maximize the value and competitiveness for Swedish businesses.
        In this thesis, annual data between 2001 and 2016 was collected to create regression
models explaining the relationship between foreign ownership and firm performance. Firm
performance was measured as Return on Assets (ROA), and Approximate Q. By using two
different metrics, we were able to make a more comprehensive and thorough interpretation of
the results. These two metrics served as complements to each other in the analysis, as ROA
presents an accounting perspective on performance, while Approximate Q represents
investors’ projection of performance. Besides including foreign ownership as a variable for
explaining performance, we included variables representing size, growth and risk to
strengthen the explanatory power of the model and to avoid omitted variable bias. The results
showed a significant, contemporaneous and positive relationship between foreign ownership
and Approximate Q, while ROA showed no significant relationship with the proportion of
foreign ownership.
        We further explored the relationship between firm performance and foreign
ownership by dividing the listed firms into different industry groups. We found that certain
domestic industries seem to benefit more than others from international capital. Once again,
the relationship was exclusively found between foreign ownership and Approximate Q for the
separate industries, and it was non-existent for ROA. The industries that showed a significant
positive relationship were Industry & Retail, Services, Property & Finance, and Other

                                         3
Industries, whereas Commodities and Technology showed an insignificant relationship. The
implication of these results suggests that the relationship between foreign ownership and
market based firm performance is dependent on firm- and industry characteristics. This
implies that for certain industries, foreign investors can increase firm performance and
subsequently firm value if it is measured based on market capitalisation. On a further note, it
supports the argument to encourage the entrance of foreign investors if value maximisation
for the company’s shareholders is the ultimate company goal. However, despite these
findings we cannot rule out the potential source of error where the results suffer from reverse
causality. Furthermore, this study has not investigated any potential change of risk that is
entailed with the increased proportion of foreign ownership. Such an examination might yield
results that can serve as an opposing view to the positive relationship we found between
Approximate Q and foreign ownership.

2. Previous Literature

Research in this field of study has previously been conducted and the results have been
ambiguous with contradictory conclusions. Some studies have found a positive relationship
between foreign ownership and firm performance while other studies have found a negative
relationship between the two. There have also been studies where no significant relationships
have been discovered. The relationship seems to vary depending on what countries are of
focus for the study. A majority of previous studies have been conducted outside of Sweden
and predominantly in developing countries. In developing economies, the distinction between
foreign and domestic capital is arguably larger than in developed economies and the potential
effects from foreign ownership on firm performance is thereby more prominent. This
characteristic of developing economies is the reason why much research has been directed
towards these types of countries.
       When considering research done outside of Sweden, Huang and Shiu (2009) explored
the local effects of foreign ownership in Taiwan. In their study, they examined if foreign
investors have an informational disadvantage compared to domestic investors due to limited
knowledge about the local environment and the domestic firms. However, the results showed
a positive relationship between the proportion of foreign ownership and stock performance
and the assertion could not be supported. Aitken and Harrison (1999) found mixed results
when looking into the Venezuelan market, studying whether domestic firms benefit from
foreign investments by looking at plant productivity. They studied both direct effects and
effects due to potential technological spillovers. Their results showed that direct foreign
ownership has a positive effect on productivity for smaller firms. Additionally, Barbosa and
Louri (2005) did a study on Greece and Portugal where they compared the corporate
performance of foreign- and domestically owned firms by looking at net and gross Return on
Assets. Once again, the results were somewhat mixed, with Portugal showing no significant
difference in performance between the two ownership categories. Neither Greece showed any
significant relationship between foreign ownership and performance except when looking at
firms with the highest gross profit, where a significant relationship was found. Furthermore,
an influential study was conducted in Korea by Baek et al (2004). They examined the effect
of foreign ownership concentration on share value of Korean firms during the Korean
financial crisis in 1997. The results showed that firms with a high foreign ownership
concentration in their ownership structure experienced less decline in share values due to the

                                         5
financial crisis than firms without these ownership characteristics. This result further argues
for the existence of a relationship between foreign ownership and firm performance.
       Regarding previous research on the topic in Sweden, there are mainly two prominent
studies that are relevant to our research question, both conducted around the millennium shift.
The study closest to our research is the one conducted by Oxelheim and Randøy (2003). They
looked into the relationship between market measures of firm performance and foreign board
membership by examining a sample of Swedish and Norwegian firms from 1996 to 1998.
Results showed that market measures of performance did have a significant relationship with
the presence of foreign board members. The second relevant study in Sweden was conducted
by Dahlquist and Robertsson (2001). In their study, they analysed what characteristics foreign
investors puts emphasis on when they invest in Swedish firms. In their paper, it is revealed
that foreign investors are primarily drawn towards larger firms, with a high presence in
international markets. They measure this international presence as the quantity of export sales
and the extent of listings abroad. The authors of this study also found results that indicate a
resemblance between foreign investors and institutional investors in Sweden.
        What this study aims to contribute with in relation to previous research is
predominantly to investigate and contrast the difference between looking at market- and
accounting measures of performance when studying the relationship with foreign ownership.
This contrast has not previously been explored in Sweden and could contribute with valuable
insights regarding the nature of the relationship. A deeper knowledge about the relationship
can also be gained by investigating if there are any differences based on industry
characteristics. Our study further aims to provide a current, relevant and trustworthy
understanding of the topic by examining an extensive time period leading up to present date.

3. Data

3.1 Data Sources

To analyse the relationship between firm performance and foreign ownership, we needed data
that reflected both these factors. Due to a lack of availability from one uniform source, the
data was gathered from two separate sources, Modular Finance and Thomson Reuters
Datastream. Data with annually updated historical figures on ownership was collected from
Holdings, a database supplied by Modular Finance. Their definition of foreign ownership is
based on the origin of the owner rather than on what country the account containing the
shares is based. By making this distinction, Holdings makes adjustment for the problem with
indirect ownership and the holding of shares through a foreign account directly or indirectly
through an asset management company. All remaining variables were collected from
Thomson Reuters Datastream.
        As a result of extracting the data from two different sources, some of the observations
were not identical in terms of measurement date. The difference in time was due to how the
data sources collect their data. For instance, the market value is collected each calendar year,
while accounting measures for some of the firms are reported and collected per fiscal year.
This caused the variables to be slightly different when comparing the market value of equity,
which could be extracted from both sources and serve as a way to make sure the merged data
was accurate. However, the differences seemed relatively small as the magnitude of this
potential error was investigated when going through the dataset. For those variables that
could be extracted from both sources, we chose the ones from Thomson Reuters Datastream
to create consistency in the data, as all data was gathered from this source except the variable
measuring the percentage of foreign ownership.

3.2 Sample & Adjustments

The data sample consisted of annual data for companies listed on OMX Stockholm for the
time period 2001-2016. The sample for this period in Holdings consisted of 278 publicly
traded firms. In Thomson Reuters Datastream the sample consisted of 313 publicly traded
firms. The intention was to use the most comprehensive time period possible leading up to
present date. Due to missing data from both Datastream and Holdings, data from 2017 was

                                         7
excluded from the sample. Ownership data in Holdings did only go back as far as 2001,
which is the reason why the sample consisted of data from 2001-2016.
       Data for companies that was not available in both datasets was dropped. When all
data had been merged together, the resulting dataset contained data on 278 unique companies.
The annual data for some specific years was missing in the merged dataset. The missing data
was likely due to IPO’s during the time period, delisting’s, or because of missing
observations from either of the data sources. The missing data had no indication of suffering
from attrition in any way and was assumed to be MCAR (missing completely at random).
Because of the MCAR assumption and to preserve as many observations as possible we
decided to keep the panel data unbalanced. The final dataset consisted of an unbalanced panel
with 247 companies and 2739 firm/year observations for the model explaining ROA, and 216
companies with 2367 firm/year observations for the model explaining Approximate Q. The
difference in number of observations between the two models was due to the elimination of
firms that did not have complete data for all the components needed to calculate either ROA
or Approximate Q.

When we examined the unadjusted variables in the two datasets that can be seen in Table 1
and 2, there were certain features that stood out. As mentioned in the previous section, the
number of observations differed between the two datasets. The Approximate Q dataset
contained slightly fewer observations than the ROA dataset due to more missing values.
Based on the descriptive statistics and after going through the datasets, the variables
representing ROA, Approximate Q and foreign ownership share were all judged to be
somewhat normally distributed, without any severe outliers or highly skewed distributions.
The leverage ratio and the revenue growth variables in the two datasets did however suffer

4. Scope of Study

4.1 Hypothesis 1

Research regarding corporate governance demonstrates that there is a conflict of interest
between firm principals and agents, commonly referred to as the “agency problem” (Andrei
and Vishny, 1997). The agency problem suggests that insider managers and owners have
incentives to perform value destroying activities in favour of themselves at the expense of the
firm value. Value destroying activities could include conglomerate building through
acquisitions and suboptimal investment decisions (Montgomery, 1994). Another example
could be that managers resist a takeover attempt to protect their own benefit rather than to
serve shareholders (Comment and Schwert, 1995). Furthermore, poorly structured incentive
programs are also a cause why managers perform activities that are value destroying for the
firm and its shareholders but that benefit the managers themselves (Andrei and Vishny,
1997). All these factors could lead to a worsened performance for firms due to the distance
between shareholders and managers. However, it has been argued that foreign investors have
similarities to institutional investors and are able to improve the monitoring of insider
managers and owners (Dahlquist and Robertsson, 2001). As a result, the presence of foreign
ownership could mitigate the agency problem in a firm and reduce the value destroying
activities. Thereby, a higher degree of foreign presence in a firm might lead to improved
performance and reinforce the value enhancing activities. This effect should be noticeable
when looking at accounting based performance measures as management becomes more
effective in managing the firm’s assets. The logic of this cause and effect relationship is the
reasoning behind the first hypothesis in this study.

Hypothesis 1a: There is a relationship between accounting measures of performance and the
proportion of foreign ownership in Swedish firms.

       Further research supporting the argument that foreign ownership could be positive for
performance is a study in Sweden that showed that firms with outsider Anglo-American
board members are more likely to fire a CEO if a firm is lacking in its performance
(Oxelheim and Randøy, 2003). This indicates that a higher presence of outsider Anglo-
American board members could lead to an increased focus on fulfilling the financial goals of
Swedish firms, supporting the assumption that the origin of owners might have an effect on

                                        10
firm performance. Another result from the same study proves that the presence of foreign
directors is perceived by the market as a signal of transparency and a willingness to improve
governance. This indicates that the presence of foreign ownership can cause higher firm
market values as it decreases the distance between shareholders and management. Thereby,
from a market perspective a high degree of foreign presence implies a positive effect on firm
performance as foreign owners are appreciated by investors, which in turn leads to a higher
valuation. Following this reasoning, the extension to the first hypothesis is formulated as
follows.

Hypothesis 1b: There is a relationship between market measures of performance and the
proportion of foreign ownership in Swedish firms.

       Another implication of an internationalised ownership structure is that foreign
investors can expand the firm’s potential investor base and its access to capital. This could
decrease the risk of equity due to diversification benefits for the shareholders, as risk will be
distributed among more investors with different portfolio exposures. Furthermore, investors
with a global portfolio rather than a national portfolio will have a globally determined cost of
capital, and risk that previously could not be diversified within a single national economy can
be erased (Stulz, 1999). As a result from a reduction in risk, the companies will decrease their
cost of capital which increases their market values. Other empirical evidence supporting this
causal relationship have been analysed in cross-border listings, where results seem to support
abnormal returns for firms with an international ownership structure (Sundaram and Logue,
1996), (Foerster and Karolyi, 1999). This argument is an explicit support to hypothesis 1b,
that questions a relationship between market measures and foreign ownership. Furthermore,
the argument can also be applied to hypothesis 1a, as other research shows a significant
relationship between a lower cost of capital and accounting measures of corporate
performance (Sattar, 2015).
       Other studies have recognised the notion that foreign ownership could have a negative
effect on firm performance. Arguments include that there could be a possibility that domestic
investors have an informational advantage compared to foreign investors, which creates
informational asymmetry that could expose firms to situations such as moral hazard and
adverse selection (Huang and Shiu, 2009). The informational asymmetry can subsequently
have a negative effect on firm value and its performance (Fosu et al, 2016). These arguments

                                        11
are applicable to both hypothesis 1a and 1b, but highlights that the relationship could be
negative rather than positive.
       Aforementioned arguments support the claim that firm performance is associated with
foreign ownership. Other research does however support that no significant relationship
exists between the two. Empirical research made in Portugal showed that ownership ties have
no significant difference in respect to the performance of firms (Barbosa and Louri, 2005).
Furthermore, research made in Bulgaria, Poland, and Romania showed mixed results where
Bulgaria and Romania showed no significant relationship, while Poland showed a positive
correlation between firms’ performance and foreign ownership (Jozef, 2001). This further
raises the question if there is in fact a relationship between the two in Sweden. As a result,
reflecting on hypothesis 1a and 1b, the null hypotheses being tested are the following:

HO1a: There exist no relationship between accounting measures of performance and the
proportion of foreign ownership in Swedish firms.

HO1b: There exist no relationship between market measures of performance and the
proportion of foreign ownership in Swedish firms.

4.2 Hypothesis 2

The extension of this study was to investigate if the relationship between the proportion of
foreign ownership and firm performance varied between industries. We did this by dividing
the OMXS into six different industries. In Sweden, there is a term commonly referred to as
“The traditional Swedish ownership model”. It refers to when the ownership structure of
companies is highly concentrated to only one or two investors, commonly consisting of a
family (Henrekson and Jakobsson, 2008). As a result of this ownership structure, the capital
base for listed companies in Sweden can become somewhat limited, and as the ownership
becomes globalised with increased influence of foreign capital, the traditional model is
challenged. Therefore, an increased proportion of foreign ownership could potentially
contribute more to industries that require substantial investments, as capital becomes
increasingly accessible. This indicates that industries that are characterised by large balance
sheets due to heavy investments in non-liquid assets, can gain more from the entrance of
foreign investors than industries without these kinds of investment requirements.

                                        12
       Another argument supporting a variation in the relationship between industries is that
some domestic industries in Sweden are more competitive than others on a global scale,
which attracts more foreign capital. It is widely recognised that Sweden is in the forefront of
digitalisation and many of the industries that are going through a digital transformation have
been the most attractive industries for foreign investments in Sweden, such as the service,
manufacturing and finance sector (EY, 2017). The skewed distribution of foreign investments
between sectors could imply that the magnitude of the effect from foreign ownership on firm
performance differs between industries. These arguments result in the next two hypotheses
that this study will analyse.

Hypothesis 2a: The relationship between accounting measures of performance and the
proportion of foreign ownership in Swedish firms varies depending on what industries the
firms are active in.

Hypothesis 2b: The relationship between market measures of performance and the
proportion of foreign ownership in Swedish firms varies depending on what industries the
firms are active in.

Where the null hypotheses are:

HO2a: The relationship between accounting measures of performance and the proportion of
foreign ownership in Swedish firms does not vary depending on what industries the firms are
active in.

HO2b: The relationship between market measures of performance and the proportion of
foreign ownership in Swedish firms does not vary depending on what industries the firms are
active in.

5. Methodology

5.1 Variables

The variable of interest for this study is foreign ownership. This variable is shown as the
share of foreign ownership of the firm’s total ownership. The variable is expressed in the
models as continuous rather than discrete, as this enables a measurement of the effect caused
by a changing ratio over time. Further, a categorical solution would demand cut-off points to
be selected without any clear approach on how to determine these. A categorical solution was
therefore discarded.

5.1.1 Performance Measures

Measuring firm performance is a complex task, as different measures takes various aspects of
performance into consideration. Therefore, to conduct a study measuring firm performance,
the first question to answer was what variables should be chosen to represent performance.
Previous research that studied the relationship between ownership and performance have
used different measures of firm performance. A study on foreign and domestic ownership in
Greece and Portugal used ROA as a measure of firm performance (Barbosa and Louri, 2005).
Studies done in India on foreign ownership and profitability has likewise focused on return
measures such as Return on Sales (ROS) and ROA. In research on Anglo-American board
membership’s effect on performance, a logarithmic Tobin’s Q was used as a measure of
performance (Oxelheim and Randøy, 2003). Tobin’s Q also appear as a performance measure
in research on foreign directors’ effect on board meeting frequency (Hahn and Lasfer, 2016).
       These measures can in general be classified as accounting based measures and market
based measures. Accounting based measures are based on reported numbers and reflect a
backward-looking measure of performance. Market based measures on the other hand, reflect
a forward-looking perspective and is based on the market’s valuation and expectations of the
firm rather than solely on the firms’ own reported earnings. In the models that tested the null
hypotheses, both ROA and a version of Tobin’s Q were used. Tobin’s Q was expressed in the
form of Approximate Q as defined by Chung and Pruitt (1994). The reason behind using both
measures was to establish a more exhaustive picture of the effect on performance from both
an accounting and market based perspective, and also to compare actual effects on
performance (ROA) with the perception of foreign ownership (Approximate Q). When
comparing the results from the models, the different dependent measures allows for a

                                        14
comparison between the effect of foreign ownership on market based and accounting based
measures.
       Tobin’s Q represents a ratio of the market value of a firm divided by the replacement
cost of its assets (Tobin, 1969). It thereby represents the inherent value and future
expectations on a company and is accordingly an appropriate measure of market based
performance. Throughout the years it has been used in a variety of shapes mainly due to the
fact that the original measure requires extensive financial data, which might be difficult to
obtain. One version of Tobin’s Q is Approximate Q which is an approximation of Tobin’s Q.
Approximate Q uses a sample of more easily accessible financial information and manages to
explain at least 96.6% of the variability in Tobin’s Q (Chung and Pruitt, 1994). As this
measure requires less financial information than Tobin’s Q, and still shows significant
correlation with the original measure, we decided to use Approximate Q for this study. When
dealing with the calculation of market value of equity used in Approximate Q, there was a
dilemma in how to handle different equity structures. For the companies with more than one
listed or unlisted equity security, which is common for Swedish stocks, we decided to use the
consolidated market value for all outstanding equity.
       An Approximate Q value of one can be seen as the equilibrium, and a value above
one suggests that value is added by the firm’s operations and the firm is worth more than the
replacement cost of its assets. On the other hand, a value less than one implies that the
replacement cost of the firm’s assets exceeds the market value of the firm. Therefore, a high
value implies better performance.

5.1.3 Control Variables

In order to avoid omitted variable bias, several control variables were included in the models
to represent other factors than foreign ownership that potentially could effect the performance
of a company. Factors were chosen based on how firm performance have predominantly been
measured in previous research and on financial theories regarding what factors have an effect
on firm performance.
        Firm size is a factor that have been extensively researched regarding its effect on firm
performance. Larger firms can in comparison to smaller firms be expected to exhibit superior
abilities to capture advantages from both economies of scale and scope (Penrose, 1959).
Additionally, larger firms may be argued to have the advantage of market power in terms of
their ability to influence prices on the market, in comparison to smaller firms (Bresnahan,
1989), (Shepherd, 1986). In contrast to these two views, it could be argued that smaller
companies can gain advantages on larger companies by employing different compensating
strategies, such as product innovation and providing employee benefits (Audretsch and
Yamawaki, 1992). The research on firm size and its effect on performance is thereby
ambiguous but indicates an existing relationship between the two. The natural logarithm of
total assets was used as an independent variable representing size. This adjustment was made

                                        16
in line with previous research, and to adjust for a seemingly skewed distribution of total
assets in the sample.
        Leverage ratio and its correlation with firm performance is a topic of constant debate,
with research based on perfect capital markets stating that capital structure should have no
effect on firm performance (Modigliani and Miller, 1958). On the other hand, empirical
research supports the argument of a relationship between the two (Le and Phan, 2017). Firms
can use a higher leverage ratio to improve the return to their shareholders. Meanwhile, with
higher leverage comes greater exposure to financial risk. Therefore, in theory the relationship
between the debt to equity variable and performance has an inverse U-shaped form, as
shareholders appreciate the exposure up to a certain extent (Myers, 1984). Besides these
factors, the leverage ratio can also be a measure of a firm’s ability to capture investment
opportunities through financial flexibility and bargaining power in financial markets
(Marchica and Mura, 2010). With these characteristics of the leverage ratio in mind, the
leverage ratio in the shape of debt divided by equity (debt-to-equity ratio), was included both
as a squared and non-squared variable in the models.
        The firm's growth rate is another variable generally considered to have an effect on
firm performance. Previous research has shown that there exists a positive relationship
between growth and profitability, although the relationship was demonstrated to be slightly
weaker within concentrated industries (Bradburd and Caves, 1982). A positive relationship
has further been demonstrated by Benefield and House (1995). On account of these factors,
the growth variable in the shape of yearly revenue growth was included in the models.

       In the descriptive statistics which can be seen in Table 1 and 2, the leverage ratio and
growth variable seemed to contain severe outliers. The outliers were deemed large enough to
potentially cause biased results. Due to this, we implemented a winsorization to reduce the

                                         th         th
problem and outliers beyond the 10  and 90  percentiles were replaced with the next value
counting inwards from the extremes. We performed this procedure to both the leverage ratio
and the revenue growth variable. The resulting winsorized variables as well as the
logarithmic total asset variable can be seen in Table 3 and 4.
       Unobserved heterogeneity is a potential problem when dealing with panel data, which
can cause endogeneity and a misleading model. To deal with this problem, we did a Hausman
test to decide if fixed- or random effect was appropriate for the models (Hausman, 1978). A
common approach for applying fixed effects to a model is to add firm fixed effects. This
approach will deal with heterogeneity caused by unobserved differences among individual
firms in the sample not represented by other independent variables in the model. The
Hausman test, which tests for no systematic difference among variables between firms, could
be rejected at a 1% significance level for both models. We thereby decided to use firm fixed
effects in the models. By concluding that a firm fixed effects model is needed, one is
implicitly stating that unobserved firm specific characteristics are correlated with the
independent variables, which we assumed to be the case. Additionally, besides the firm fixed
effects, we tested for time fixed effects in the models by using a Wald Chi-Squared Test.
When we tested for time fixed effects, which controls for aggregate differences between
years, the Wald Chi-Squared test showed that time fixed effects were needed at a 1%
significance level for Approximate Q and at a 5% significance level for ROA. A dummy
variable for each year in the sample was therefore added to the model.

                                        18
       The models were further developed by testing for heteroscedasticity (systematically
unequal variability of variables across observations). Additionally, the models were tested for
autocorrelation (correlation between observations with lagged versions of itself in time series
regressions). A modified Wald statistic for groupwise heteroscedasticity was used when
testing for heteroscedasticity (Greene, 2000), and a Portmanteau test was done when testing
for autocorrelation (Inoue and Solon, 2006). The tests showed that the models were suffering
from both heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation at a 1% significance level. In order to deal
with these issues, clustered standard errors were used in both models, clustered on the firm
level. Clustering can be used when common unobserved random shocks at the group level
will lead to correlation between all observation within each group (Hansen, 2007). This was
assumed to be the case in our models. Furthermore, using clustered standard errors on the
firm level was deemed appropriate since the sample contained a large number of firms to
cluster on.

5.3 Industry Group Comparison

In order to test HO2a and HO2b., firms were divided into groups based on their industry
belonging and separate regressions were run on each of the industries. Two main criteria
were considered when dividing the firms into industry groups. First, the classification into
industries had to reflect the operations of the firms within and be distinctly separated from
other industries. Second, industry groups had to contain a sample large enough to yield any
significant result. The chosen industries were; Commodities, Technology, Industry & Retail,
Services, Property & Finance, and Other Industries. The category labelled Other Industries
was used for companies belonging to an industry not containing enough observations or
companies without a clear industry belonging.
       The regression models for the industry groups were based on Model 1 and 2. We did
in these regression models, just as in previous ones, perform winsorization to the variables
representing D/E ratio and revenue growth to control for severe outliers in the data.
Furthermore, we once again used the natural logarithm of total assets as the variable
representing size due to the skewed distribution of total assets for firms in the sample.
Additionally, we used the same reasoning as in previous models for including a squared D/E
ratio.
       We found mixed results between the industries when we tested if fixed- or random
firm effects, and time fixed effect were to be applied to the models with the help of Hausman

                                        19
tests, and Wald Chi-Squared tests. The Hausman test could be rejected at a significant level
for both models explaining the industries Technology, Industry & Retail, and Property &
Finance. It could also be rejected for the ROA model in the Commodities industry as well as
for the Approximate Q model in Other Industries. For these industries, we used an OLS
model with firm fixed effects, and for the remaining industries we used a GLS model with
random firm effects. Using random effects in a model differs slightly from the fixed effects.
There are two different estimators being estimated in a fixed and random effects model for
panel data regressions. There is the within estimator which uses the variation within firms
during time. This is the estimator that the fixed effects model focuses on. However, there is
also the between estimator that use variation between firms. In a random effects model, a
weighted average of the within and between estimators is used.
        When we tested for time fixed effects with a Wald Chi-Squared test, the result was
that yearly dummies were to be included in the Technology, Industry & Retail and Service
industries. Furthermore, the test showed that yearly dummies were to be included in the
model for ROA in the Property & Finance industry as well as in the model for Approximate
Q in Other Industries. In the remaining industry models, there were no significant differences
over the years in the sample, and yearly dummies were not included.
       Due to the fact that the Model 1 and 2 suffered from heteroscedasticity and
autocorrelation in the main regressions, clustered standard errors on the firm level were used
when estimating the industry models as well. It was thereby assumed that random shocks at
the group level would lead to correlation between all observation within each group (Hansen,
2007). The sample of companies was on the verge of being too small to cluster standard
errors on the firm level in some cases, as the Commodity industry did only contain 15
companies. However, we decided that the benefits of adjusting for heteroscedasticity and
autocorrelation outweighed the disadvantages of a small sample size and clustered standard
errors were kept on the firm level.

6.5 Interpretation of the Results

Referring back to the main regression models, the results imply that foreign ownership has a
contemporaneous, significant and positive relationship with the performance measure
Approximate Q. Furthermore, the relationship between foreign ownership and ROA is
negative but insignificant. The discrepancy in the results indicates that an increase of the
foreign ownership seems to positively affect market measures, but have no relationship with
accounting measures. The contradictory result from the two models is further strengthened
when looking at their relationship in the Pearson correlation matrix in Table 12, which
indicates a weak relationship between Approximate Q and ROA.
       Considering the results when testing for accounting based measures of performance
(HO1a), it is evident that foreign ownership does not have a visible effect on ROA and does
not add extra value to how effective firms are at making use of their assets. Considering the
arguments regarding the monitoring abilities of foreign investors discussed in 4.1 Hypothesis
1, the result show that there seem to be no tangible benefits to gain from effective monitoring
caused by the presence of foreign investors. If any tangible benefits would exist, they are

                                        27
presumed to be reflected in ROA due to higher returns, as firms would improve their value
enhancing activities, but no such evidence was found.
       The results from the ROA model could also be interpreted and explained by
macroeconomic trends for firms over time. The world has changed during the time frame of
this study and has become increasingly globalised. Besides being providers of physical
capital, foreign investors can be providers of intellectual capital and contribute with valuable
global competence to the firms they invest in. It could however be the case that the
advantages of having foreign investors in the ownership structure is diminishing as the world
becomes increasingly interconnected. More advanced technological tools help information
travel faster and more effortlessly across borders. This might lead to a diminishing
importance of the origin of owners, since firms are in less need of the global competence
these investors possess. Although this might be the reality, it is not certain that the market has
completely adapted to this change in business environment. This factor might thereby be one
reason why no significant relationship was observed between ROA and foreign ownership
while it was found between Approximate Q and foreign ownership. The explanation above
can further find support when questioning why previous studies on the effects from foreign
ownership on performance have shown mixed results between countries. It might be the case
that effects on accounting measures of performance does not show any significant
relationship with foreign ownership in developed countries such as Sweden, while the results
from developing countries are more prone to show a significant relationship.
       Regarding the results from testing market based measures of performance (HO1b),
there are several possible explanations for the outcome. As discussed under 4.1 Hypothesis 1,
one explanation might be the signalling effect that an increased foreign ownership share
conveys to the market in terms of transparency and willingness to improve governance. This
effect could potentially increase firms’ market values, which in turn would have a positive
effect on Approximate Q. On the other hand, the explanation for the results can also be found
in the argument that foreign ownership reduces the cost of capital for firms. By enabling
access to a larger capital base, foreign investors may reduce firms’ costs of capital. In theory,
a decreased cost of capital can lower the discount factor used to value firms, which in turn
increases the present value of company market value. This effect could explain why firm
market value and thereby market measures of performance show a positive relationship with
foreign ownership in the results. However, as raised under 4.1 Hypothesis 1, a lower cost of
capital has in addition been associated to accounting measures of performance by Sattar
(2015). This indicates that a significant relationship should have been found between foreign

                                        28
ownership and ROA, but such a relationship could not be found. The explanation suggesting
that foreign investors can decrease firms’ cost of capital is thereby weakened due to the
contradictory results when taking both performance measures into consideration.
        Another important aspect from the results is to acknowledge the possibility of reverse
causality. Reverse causality is problematic to rule out, as the results might be a reflection of
foreign investors’ propensity to invest in companies of a particular nature, rather than
investors themselves having an impact on firms. The significant positive coefficient for
foreign ownership share in the Approximate Q model might, when considering reverse
causality, indicate that foreign investors are drawn to companies with high market valuations
in relation to their total assets. Furthermore, the insignificant relationship between ROA and
foreign ownership share, could be interpreted as an indication that foreign investors are not
attracted to firms that are profitable and properly managed in accordance with accounting
measures. When further reflecting on causes of reverse causality and why foreign investors
are attracted to Swedish firms, firm size seems to be a factor that these investors takes into
consideration. This argument is somewhat strengthened by looking at the correlation between
the share of foreign ownership and total assets displayed in Table 12, which shows a positive
relationship significant at a 5% level. The notion is further supported by Dahlquist and
Robertsson (2001). The propensity to invest in larger firms can be an approach to reduce the
informational disadvantage mentioned under 4.1 Hypothesis 1. By investing in large firms,
foreign investors may reduce disadvantages caused by language barriers, limits in
information communicated abroad, and firms’ limited global recognition.
       In addition to aforementioned results, the outcome from the lagged regressions under
6.4 Robustness Tests has important implications for this study. No significant relationship
was found between foreign ownership and any of the performance measures when using one,
two and five-year lags respectively for explanatory variables in the regression models. The
implication from these tests is that the relationship between Approximate Q and foreign
ownership represent a contemporaneous effect and not a lagged one. This result further
allows us to argue for the explanation of a positive signalling effect due to changes in foreign
ownership, since this effect is likely to be rather contemporaneous, as investors are assumed
to react instantaneously to the entrance of foreign investors. However, the result could also be
explained by the presence of reverse causality, which would show up as a contemporaneous
effect if foreign investors are drawn to Swedish companies at the same time as their
Approximate Q values are high.

                                        29
        Regarding the regressions testing Hypotheses 2a and 2b, the result is to a great extent
in line with the outcome of the main regression models. In fact, no industry shows a
significant relationship between foreign ownership and ROA and the majority of the
coefficients are negative, which is visible in Table 6. The models with Approximate Q as the
dependent variable does however show more variation between industries, as noted in the
result from 6.3 Industry Group Comparison. The question that undoubtedly arises is why
Industry & Retail, Services, Other Industries, and the Property & Finance industries show
highly significant, positive coefficients for foreign ownership when explaining Approximate
Q, while the Technology and Commodity industries do not. The explanation for this could be
that an industry such as Technology, where firms in many cases are global from the outset, is
unable to gain as much from foreign capital as other industries. The technology sector is
likely interconnected across the globe and competence is shared effortlessly across borders,
reducing the importance of location. The result from the Commodities sector is somewhat
more puzzling and does in fact point in the opposite direction of the argument that industries
with big, illiquid balance sheets might benefit more from foreign capital discussed under 4.2
Hypothesis 2. The result may however be biased due to the small sample size in the
Commodities industry of only 15 companies, which can be seen in Table 6.
       The most attractive industries for foreign capital, brought up under 4.2 Hypothesis 2,
were expected to be the Manufacturing, Services, and Finance industry (EY, 2017).
Interestingly, these were the same industries showing significant and positive relationships
between foreign ownership share and Approximate Q, namely Industry & Retail, Services,
Property & Finance as well as Other Industries. The reason for this might be, as mentioned in
4.2 Hypothesis 2, that the digital transformation in these industries might have caused an
increased interest abroad, attracting international capital and advantageous market valuations.
These advantageous market valuations might then be reflected in favourable Approximate Q
values. Furthermore, both Industry & Retail and Property & Finance are industries where
companies have large balance sheets and require substantial investments, which are both
factors making access to foreign capital favourable by lowering the cost of capital.
Additionally, both Industry & Retail and Property & Finance contain many traditional
Swedish firms. In these firms, a traditional Swedish ownership model might be expected,
where the ownership structure is highly concentrated to one or two investors, commonly
consisting of a family (Henrekson and Jakobsson, 2008). Thereby, the capital base might
further be limited, making access to foreign capital even more advantageous for firm
performance in certain industries.

                                        30
       An additional implication for the Property & Finance industry concerns the argument
of increased monitoring due to foreign ownership. The advantage of increased monitoring
might be more applicable to this particular industry due to the lack of trust for financial
institutions caused by historic events such as the financial crisis of 2008. Due to this lack of
trust, the signalling of a willingness to improve governance caused by increased foreign
ownership might be the reason why a significant relationship was found between foreign
ownership and Approximate Q in the Property & Finance industry.
        Overall, it is challenging to state any universal causes of the implications induced by
changes in foreign ownership and why they differ between industries when the relationship
with Approximate Q was tested. The different results between industries does somewhat also
argue that no universal explanation can be found. However, it is evident that the effect of
changes in foreign ownership on market based performance is dependent on firm and
industry characteristics. Furthermore, the underlying explanations this study has brought
attention to can be more applicable to some industries than others, and determining to what
extent each of the explanations applies to different industries becomes a topic for future
study.

                                        31
7. Conclusion

The intent of this paper was to evaluate if a relationship exists between foreign ownership
and the performance of Swedish companies listed on OMXS. Firm performance was
measured as Return on Assets, representing an accounting based perspective, and
Approximate Q, representing a market based perspective. Our study finds a positive,
contemporaneous, and significant relationship between foreign ownership and Approximate
Q and an insignificant relationship with Return on Assets. These findings are in agreement
with the results from Oxelheim and Randøy (2003), who found a positive relationship
between market measures of performance and the presence of Anglo-American board
members in their study with data from 1996 to 1998. Regarding our result between Return on
Assets and foreign ownership, it is aligned with the results from Barbosa and Louri (2005),
who found no significant effect of foreign ownership on accounting based measures in
Greece and Portugal. Furthermore, this study finds that the relationship between Approximate
Q and the proportion of foreign ownership varies between industries. The industries that
showed a positive relationship were Services, Industry & Retail, Property & Finance, and
Other Industries, whereas Technology and Commodities showed no significant relationship.
Because of the findings that the relationship differs between industries, it is evident that the
effect of changes in foreign ownership on market based performance is dependent on firm
and industry characteristics. These findings can therefore serve as support to the notion that
for some industries, foreign investors can contribute to firm performance and value
enhancement when looking at performance measures based on market value.

                                        32
8. Limitations and Suggestions for Further Research

The robustness tests showed that the models we used to test our hypotheses are rather solid
and the findings are similar when we used unadjusted variables in the models. This creates
validity and instils trust in the results. However, we made choices in the execution of the
methodology that could have yielded alternative results. The choice of including the foreign
ownership variable as a continuous variable was made in order to be able to look at a
changing percentage rate over time within firms. The method might have yielded another
outcome if foreign ownership had been made into a discrete variable representing a very high
share of foreign ownership and a very low share respectively. This could possibly have led to
a more extreme result, perhaps contradictory to the result that was found in our study.
        In terms of quantity of data for the analyses, the two main regression models both
have sufficient quantity of company observations. However, for the industry group analysis,
the data quantity for some of the industries are rather small. The small samples make the
results less trustworthy, and when interpreting the results, the limiting sample size was
therefore considered. In addition, the models used to test the hypotheses could have been
extended. For instance, there are other potential control variables that could have been used
or added to further control for endogeneity and omitted variable bias such as firm age,
advertising expenditure as well as investments in R&D. These are all factors found in
previous research to have an effect on firm performance. The reason why they were excluded
in this study was due to the lack of data, since too few observations would have led to a
smaller sample when running regressions. Based on this, we decided to favour a considerable
sample size over additional control variables. In addition to using supplementary control
variables, other measures of performance as dependent variables could have been used. When
the robustness tests were undertaken, ROE was tested as an additional measure of accounting
performance. Return on Sales (ROS) and Pre-tax Return on Assets could however also be
relevant measures for investigating accounting based performance.
       Another potential approach for studying the relationship between foreign ownership
and performance could be the introduction of instrumental variables. However, no suitable
instrumental variables were found, which resulted in a discardment of this method. If an
instrumental variable had been found, appropriate according to the nature of this study,
omitted variable bias as well as simultaneity bias could possibly have been further restricted
in the model. This would have resulted in more reliable results and reverse causality could
have been restricted.

                                        33
        In this study, it is implied that foreign ownership has a positive association with firm
performance measured as Approximate Q. We suggest that there are several possible
explanations for this result. Since the relationship varies between industries, some of the
explanations provided are more relevant to some industries than others. To give a more
nuanced picture of the causes behind the result, it would therefore be interesting to determine
to what extent each of the explanations mentioned in this study are contributing to firm
performance.
        Another area of investigation relevant for future research could be to explore if firms’
exposure to risk is affected by the extent of foreign investors. Potentially, it might be that
firms become more exposed to macroeconomic events as its owners are more dispersed
across the world. On the other hand, the relationship could be the opposite, and there might
be diversification benefits with foreign ownership, indicating reduced company risk.
        Lastly, instead of dividing the sample into subgroups based on industries it could have
been done based on market capitalisation. In Graph 1, we can distinguish that the level of
foreign ownership differs significantly between large cap, mid cap, and small cap. However,
there seem to be no clear explanation to why this is the case, and what the implications are.
Could it be that foreign ownership provides greater advantages to larger firms than smaller
firms or vice versa? Furthermore, such a study could also use both accounting and market
based measures for performance to see if any differences can be distinguished. Thus, the
result from a study of this kind could once again serve as a theoretical foundation revealing
how firms can maximise their value and performance, and whether or not foreign ownership
should be encouraged.

        Good  Will or Risky Business?

               -   The  required risk premium  for  stocks with goodwill

                         Helen Broman* and Nina Lundén**

                                     Abstract

This paper  studies  the  empirical  relationship between goodwill and  the cross-sectional
variation in  US  stock returns during different market  conditions. In order to better understand
how goodwill is priced in the market,  the correlation with  both absolute and risk-adjusted
future  stock  returns  are  investigated  using  a  Fama-MacBeth  regression  setting.  When
controlling for other variables known to  affect stock returns, the results indicate  a positive
relationship  between goodwill and  future absolute  stock returns.  This  result  provides  evidence
for the hypothesis  that investors perceive goodwill as  risky and requires  a risk premium  as
compensation  for holding  stocks  with  large  goodwill.  Furthermore,  our findings  show  that
firms with  goodwill have higher market betas,  earn lower  returns during market downturns
and higher  return  during market upturns.

Keywords:  Goodwill, risk  premium,  stock returns,  market  downturns, market upturns
JEL Classifications:  G32 (Financial risk and management, Value of firms, Goodwill)

2. Introduction
Performing  company valuation  based on accounting data  is common practice for investors. It
is therefore interesting to  investigate how specific  firm  characteristics  in the  company
accounts affect the  valuation and  thus  the stock returns.  The goodwill item  is one  of them.
Large amounts of this item  are  frequently found in financial reports  and  it  is  considered to be
surrounded by  uncertainty  of  what it is and how much it is worth.  This uncertainty  might be
reflected in the stock price of a security  with goodwill, which is what distinguish this item
from other firm characteristics.  White et al (2003)  describe how goodwill  appears as an asset
in the balance sheet  when the paying price  for the target  company  in an acquisition exceeds
the fair value of its net assets. The residual  is  called  goodwill  and  is classified as an intangible
asset shown in the consolidated accounts. Conceptually, goodwill can be seen as a
compensation for the abnormal returns that the acquirer expects to obtain from the
acquisition.  For example, this item might include  brand value or  research  and  development
resources.  Hence,  incorporated in large goodwill  items  are characteristics that are supposed  to
generate higher performance and profitability  in the future.
             Companies with a mergers  and  acquisitions  growth strategy may  differ from
companies that grow organically  in several ways.  For  instance,  a  faster growing pace can  be
obtained through mergers and  acquisitions with the cost of a  more  extensive  adaption  period
compared to the slower  organic growth  option  with gradual adaption  to  new businesses.
Furthermore, conglomerates have the  tendency of being  present  in  more  business areas.  These
economical  differences  are most likely to affect the performance of the firm.  However,  in this
paper,  the effects  of the underlying economical events are not examined.  Instead, it is  the
potential  extra risk that  exists  in  the valuation of a company  with  large goodwill  that is of
interest. This extra risk  factor  is  proxied by a  factor  called  goodwillintensity.
Goodwillintensity  is  defined as  goodwill divided by  shareholder´s equity  of common stocks.
This paper tests if goodwillintensity  can be used as a proxy for  one dimension of risk  in
explaining stock returns.  To use proxies for one dimension of risk, regardless of the unnamed
underlying sources of risk,  is not an uncommon practise. Several  acknowledged  publications
have  tested for other factors  used  as proxies  for risk dimensions,  which might  correlate  with
absolute  future stock returns.  More specifically, this  study  is conducted  by examining the
empirical relationship between goodwillintensity  and the  cross-sectional variation  of  absolute
future  stock returns (both unadjusted and risk-adjusted  returns are used  in the empirical tests).

                                                                     2
Furthermore,  the stock returns for firms with goodwill are investigated  during different
market states.
             Previous  literature on  goodwill  has  focused  on short-term effects,  such as stock
price reaction to  goodwill impairments.  Another intensive research area includes the effects
from different goodwill accounting methods on company valuation (purchase method,
pooling method etc.).  A few papers have touched upon the subject “goodwill´s effect on the
market value of the firm”.  Our first contribution to the  literature  is  therefore  the long-term
examination of the  performance  of goodwill stocks, both compared to stocks without
goodwill and  conditional on the  size of  goodwillintensity. Consistent with theories  regarding
the  role of  information risk in asset pricing, we demonstrate that goodwill  has a positive
relationship with  future returns  indicating  a required risk premium for holding  stocks with
goodwill.  This result  is consistent with  the  idea that  information risk  surrounding  the goodwill
item  is priced in the market.
              The possibility of realizing the incorporated expected value of goodwill
increases in times of  market  expansion. When the market state turns down, on the other hand,
expected earnings will decrease along with the expected future abnormal return earned from
the goodwill item.  It is therefore natural to expect that in times of market slowdown, firms
with goodwill  will  present lower  stock performance than those firms without goodwill.  Our
second contribution is  thus  the  finding  that  the goodwill stocks  underperform  in market
downturns and outperform  in market upturns. Taken together,  having controlled for other
factors known to correlate with stock returns,  the results  in this paper  suggest  that
goodwillintensity  can be viewed as a  proxy for exposures to sources of  risk.
              Our main hypothesis and the three sub-hypotheses  (contingent on the main
hypothesis  being true)  are  defined as follows:

Main hypothesis

            H0: β!  =  0   Goodwillintensity  does not correlate with  absolute  future stock
                           returns
            H1: β!  ≠  0   Goodwillintensity  correlate with  absolute  future stock returns

The logic behind this hypothesis  is as follows.  Since goodwill is surrounded by uncertainty
regarding what it is and how much it is worth, this uncertainty  is expected to be reflected in
absolute  future stock returns.

                                                                     3
Sub-hypotheses

(1)         H0: β!  <  0   There is a negative relationship between  goodwillintensity
                          and  absolute  future stock returns
            H1: β!  >  0   There is a positive relationship between goodwillintensity
                          and  absolute  future stock returns

The  first sub-hypothesis is in line with  previous studies, which show that investors require a
risk premium when investing in risky stocks. Therefore, we expect a higher excess return for
stocks when the amount of goodwill in the company´s balance sheet is larger.

(2)          H0:          Firms with goodwill does not underperform in market downturns
            H1:          Firms with goodwill underperform in market downturns

(3)          H0:          Firms with  goodwill does not outperform in market upturns
             H1:          Firms with goodwill outperform in market upturns

The risk incorporated  in the goodwill item  is  expected to be reflected  differently in  stock
returns  during  different market conditions. In  an optimistic market,  investors  can be expected
to be willing to take on more risk  than in  a pessimistic market, thus effecting the valuation of
the goodwill stocks.

3. Theory
This section will first present relevant theories and models followed by previous literature
within the  relevant  research area  of this paper.

3.1 Theoretical framework

3.1.1  The  Principal-Agent problem  and  asymmetric information
The Principal-Agent theory  is a well-known economical theory  describing  the relationship
between a principal and an agent.  A  principal-agency relationship is created when an
arrangement between two parties  exists,  where one  party´s welfare is dependent upon the
other party´s actions.  The acting party is called the  agent and the party  that is affected by the
agent´s actions is called the  principal.  This principal-agent relationship can be applied on

                                                                     4
investors, the owners of the company stocks, and the corporate  management in the company.
The corporate management act as the agent and the stockholders act as principals. The
principal-agent theory assumes that agents do not necessarily act in the best interest of the
principals. Instead, they are driven by their  own goals and interests (apart from monetary
achievements)  such as perks and  power (Pindyck and Rubinfeld, 2009).  Considering  the
separation of ownership and control in a company, there is  thus  a risk that the agent  takes
advantage of the situation and does not maximize the  principal´s utility,  creating  agency costs.
This line of reasoning can be applied  to  the goodwill  item.  There is a risk that this item is not
fairly valued, but  instead  a result of management´s utility maximisation.  Moeller (2010)
argues that this  principal-agency  problem is particularly common in mergers and  acquisitions.
He describes  how  most acquisitions fail and that managerial hubris and chief executive ego
are  main reasons for the failure.  Additionally, boards and management are infected by
exuberance,  resulting  in an overestimation of their  own  ability. The overestimation of their
abilities leads  to overpayment of targets, with large premiums.  The winners are often target
shareholders, managers receiving larger responsibility and corporate advising companies. Left
behind are clients, most employees and  the  acquiring company  thus  hurting  the  stockholders.
To conclude, there is an uncertainty from a principal´s perspective of what the goodwill item
includes and how much it is worth.  Due to the principal-agent problem,  the expected future
economical value  from the goodwill item might not be realized.
             The  principal-agent  problem seems to persist  regarding goodwill,  and
asymmetric information  can therefore  be expected to  exist  between the corporate management
and  investors  whether or not goodwill has the  potential  to generate future excess returns.
Companies´  goodwill can thus be  viewed as a case of adverse selection.  Adverse selection
results in what is called  the lemon principle.  Berk and DeMarzo (2007)  describes the principle
as follows:

  “When a seller has private information  about the value of  a good, buyers will discount the
                price  they are willing to pay  due to adverse selection.”  (p.  535)

3.1.2  Information risk
When investors trade securities  in a market,  Easley and O’Hara (2004)  has  developed a  model
in which  asymmetric  information  transforms  idiosyncratic risk into systematic  risk.  In the
model, there are two types of  rational  investors,  informed and uninformed. The informed

                                                                     5
investor  can  weight his  or her  portfolio, spread the risk and  obtain full diversification of the
idiosyncratic risk.  The  uninformed investor, on the other hand,  is assumed to rationally
diversify him  or herself  to the best of his  or her  ability given the information he  or she  has at
hand.  However, due to  lack of private information,  the uninformed investor is unable to  fully
diversify his  or her  portfolio. This implies that the uninformed investor  will unavoidably  hold
some unwanted  idiosyncratic risk.  Furthermore,  given that the  uninformed investor has
rational expectations, he  or she  will  expect the portfolio to include idiosyncratic risk and will
therefore  require a risk  premium  for  his  or her  investment  as compensation  for the extra risk.
Due to the  asymmetric information  that exists  between investors  and the corporate
management  regarding the future prospects of the goodwill item  as described above,  investors
can be expected to require a risk premium for holding  stocks with goodwill.

3.1.3  Valuation of goodwill
White et al  (2003)  describes the valuation considerations regarding the goodwill item as
follows:

  “To sum up, the existence of economic goodwill is largely independent of the existence of
    accounting goodwill.  The former is a function of economic performance; the latter  is a
 function of accounting standards.  Investors and financial analysts  are primarily interested  in
    economic  goodwill, yet accounting  goodwill exist  in  abundant quality  in the financial
statements of  many companies.  For purposes of analysis, therefore, the analyst should  remove
  goodwill  from reported balance sheets.  When a company clearly earns excess returns,  this
            factor will enter into  the valuation of the company´s share.”  (p.  526)

This implies that in ordinary DCF  -  models, the goodwill item is excluded.  Instead the
goodwill item can be expected to be included in the overall valuation of the firm, potentially
as a risk factor.
           During periods of market stability and expansion, investors are generally more
willing to take on  more risk, risk premiums shrink  and stock prices increase even further. In
the end,  the  bubble is  reversed in which investors  are willing to take on less risk  (Bodie et al.,
2011).  Loss aversion  is a linked theory that presents how  investors are more sensitive  to
losses than to gains,  which further strengthen the  previous  statement  (Bodie et al., 2011).  In
market downturns, the risk  of goodwill  can  therefore  be expected to be to high to bear, which
would result in lower returns for companies with goodwill compared to companies without.

                                                                     6
Malmqvist (2010)  further  argues  that there is a higher risk that companies with high
goodwillintensity needs to make  large goodwill impairments  in market downturns.  These
companies might be forced by credit holders to issue new shares, in order to obtain a
acceptable  level of equity.  Issuing new shares during  market slowdowns are  generally
negatively perceived by investors and the  stock price is likely to fall.  This effect will have a
further risk  factor incorporated  in the goodwill item.

3.1.4  The relationship between risk and return
If looking back a few decades, the capital asset pricing  model of Sharpe (1964), Lintner
(1965), and Black (1972) shaped the way financial academics and practitioners viewed the
relationship between risk and return. The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)  determines
the appropriate required rate of return of an asset  by creating a  relationship between an asset´s
expected return and it´s non-diversifiable (systematic) risk called market beta. The model
specification is as shown below.

where:

 𝑟!  = expected return of security/portfolio  𝑖
𝛼!  =  alpha  for security/portfolio  𝑖
𝑟!  = risk-free rate

                                                   !"#(!!,!!)
𝛽!  = market beta of security/portfolio  𝑖,  =
                                             !"#(!!)
𝑟!  = expected market return
𝜖!  = error term

The formula consists of two parts based on the idea that investors need to be compensated for
two things: the time value of money and risk. The first factor of the model, the risk free rate
(𝑟!), compensates investors for choosing any investment other  than the risk-free one over a
period of time. The other factor, the risk premium  𝛽!( 𝑟!  −  𝑟!), compensates investors for
additional risk incurred in security  𝑖. Hence, combining the two factors, the expected return
on a security/portfolio  𝑖  equals the rate of a risk-free security plus the risk premium. If the
expected return does not meet or exceed the required rate of return, the investment should not
be undertaken.

                                                                     7
             In the CAPM  model,  the  efficient market  hypothesis  is assumed  to hold.  This
hypothesis  states  that stock prices will be updated directly based on new information. The
prices will  therefore  follow a random walk since new information cannot be foreseen.  Based
on this hypothesis, a  theory has developed to include three forms of market efficiency, weak,
semi-strong  and strong  (Bodie et al.,  2011).  In the weak  form, stock prices  includes
information that can be  obtained by observing  market trading data such as  past historical
prices and trading volume.  Semi-strong market efficiency  states that  all public  information  is
reflected in the  stock price.  Finally, in the  strong market efficiency, prices  include  both
private and public information regarding the future prospect of the company.
             Empirical tests of the CAPM assuming strong market efficiency have failed to
confirm the theoretical approach. Several eminent and well-known academics have therefore
argued for the misspecification of the CAPM model. A new approach to asset pricing models
has evolved  with  weak and  semi-strong market efficiency tests.  The weak-form tests examine
trends  in  stock market returns.  The semi-strong market efficiency  tests  are called multifactor
models and can be used to test multiple risk sources and their corresponding risk premiums
and thus extend the research surrounding the relationship between risk  and return (Bodie et al,
2011). A semi-strong test is used in this paper, using some implications of the weak-form
tests  further presented in the next section.

3.2 Previous literature
The literature presented below has been extracted based on the theoretical framework above
in order to highlight  previous  finding in this area.

3.2.1  Accounting quality and risk premium
Previous literature  has  examined  the effect of accounting quality on stock returns.  One
conclusion is  that poor accounting quality and uncertain information incurred  in stock
valuation parameters  are  related to higher expected returns  (Callen et al., 2011).  Moreover,
these results are confirmed  by  another result in which  investors demand a higher return to
hold stocks with greater private information  (Easly and O´Hara, 2004).  Combining the facts
that  investors only have access to public information about the value of the  goodwill  and the
accounting quality cannot be expected to be  reliable, there are reasons to believe that
investors  require  compensation  for holding this  extra  risk. Informed investors are better able
to shift their portfolio weights to incorporate new information.  Lack of information  thus

                                                                     8
constitutes a form of systematic risk. In  equilibrium,  investors require compensation for
bearing that  risk.

3.2.2  Findings from using  weak  -  form and semi  -  strong tests
From the weak-form testing literature, the main findings include the tendency for a stock
price to continue in the same direction  as in the past,  the  so called  momentum  effect.  This
effect implies serial correlation between stock returns  resulting in mispricing.  Another
discovery  from this type of tests  that is  linked to the  momentum effect  is  the  reversal  effect  in
which previous high performers fade back  and previous low performers  recover  after a certain
time of the momentum effect  (Bodie et al., 2011).
              Regarding the findings in the semi-strong tests,  Banz (1981) documents a  small-
firm-in-January effect,  demonstrating  that small  firms earn higher annual return compared to
large firms.  Arbel and Strebel  (1983) developed the small-firm-in-January effect  to  what has
been named  the  neglected-firm effect. They argued that information about smaller firms is less
available.  The lack of  information makes smaller firms a riskier investment  requiring higher
returns.  Merton (1987) strengthens Arbel and Strebel´s finding by showing that neglected
firms expect higher equilibrium returns as compensation for the information risk.  Another
paper by Amihud and Mendelson (1986) combines the small-firm-in-January effect with the
neglected firm effect. They find that investors demand a higher rate of  return for less liquid
stocks incurring higher trading costs.  This could be linked to the previous mentioned authors’
findings, since small-  and lack of information-stocks are less liquid.  In addition,  Basu (1982)
find that E/P ratio can help explain the variation in the return of a stock listed on  the  NYSE.
             Fama and French (1993)  are the most eminent authors and have argued for the
semi-strong tests. They  claim  that if stocks  are priced rationally, a stock´s accumulated risk is
multidimensional.  This implies that several factors except beta can help in explaining the
variation of stock returns.  They  find that  an overall market factor,  size, measured as market
value of equity, and book-to-market equity constitutes three  dimensions of risk  impacting
stock returns. They  explain  their  findings  with  a risk premium associated with the factors
earning higher average returns.  Another result is the  discovery  that the return to their style
portfolios (based on book-to-market or size) can predict GDP growth in many countries.
              Although the factors described above are not natural risk factors, they might act
as proxies for mode fundamental risk determinants.  These previous findings in the literature
provide evidence of several factors other than the market beta,  which  can explain stock

                                                                     9
returns. It is therefore  reasonable  to expect  that  goodwillintensity is  reflected in  absolute
future stock returns  and a proxy for  one dimension of risk.

4. Data  and variables
This section describes the dataset, the adjustments made  and  the variables generated  for the
purpose of our research.

4.1 Sources
Considering  the risk of  selection bias  when using a limited dataset, we decided to use the
widest and largest  sample of  historical  data available to us.  The data used in this paper has
therefore  been obtained from  WRDS (Wharton Research Data Services)  Compustat,  CRSP
and Fama-French & Liquidity Factors. WRDS was chosen due to its position as a leading
data research platform,  and  the  availability  of  strong historical  company  data.  Our data
includes listed US companies on NYSE, NASDAQ and AMEX (and other listed companies)
for the  years  1990-2013.  The  time period was decided upon considering the history of the
economic climate. The intention was  to capture  both the IT-bubble in 2001 and  the global
financial crisis in 2008,  their pre-and post crisis periods.
             Fiscal year-end  accounting  items,  share  data  items  and sic-codes  have  been
retrieved  from  Compustat  fundamentals annual,  daily and  monthly  closing  prices  (adjusted
for dividends and splits)  from  Compustat  securities monthly  and daily  and  value-weighted
and equally-weighted market indexes  from CRSP  stock market  indexes.  Finally, Fama-French
factors have been obtained from  Fama-French & Liquidity Factors  (monthly and daily
frequencies).  Due to higher accurateness in  annual  compared to quarterly  reports,  we chose
annual accounting data  over quarterly.  For comparability purposes, all  variables used are
measured in million US dollars  (excluding CA  dollars).  The Compustat id  item  gvkey  has
been used as company identifier when merging the datasets.  Compared to other  firm
identifiers such as  isin  or  ticker,  gvkey  has the advantage of being  constant over time, thus
allowing for  analysis of companies over time.  Moreover,  monthly  date  format  (%tm)  has been
used  creating  time-series  data  on a monthly basis.  For full list of data sources and variables,
see appendix.

4.2 Dataset and adjustments made
To make the data more applicable to our research question,  adjustments have been made to
the initial dataset.  To start with,  our dataset  includes  only companies in the US,  excluding

                                                                     10
ADRs  to avoid across country differences.  Only common stocks are kept  and  financial firms
(sic code: 6XXX) have  been excluded.  These companies are excluded because the high level
of leverage that is normal for financial firms normally translate into financial distress for
nonfinancial firms.  Furthermore, we have dropped companies that  have  observations  less  than
three  years.  This  limitation  could potentially  cause  survivorship  bias  but increases the
robustness  in regressions.  Another  related  drawback of this  type of dataset is  the presence of
biasing attrition.  It is amiable to follow the same companies over time. However, due to
bankruptcy,  delistings  and IPOs, the composition of companies will vary over time.  If the
companies that leave/join  the listed companies have similar attributes such as financial
distress or rapid growth, a bias will occur.
             Approximately 18% of  all observations have  missing values  for goodwill,  which
makes it worthwhile to fill in the data. If a company had missing values in between
observations with values on goodwill, the missing values were  approximated  to  the previous
value before the missing observations.  If  a company  had  zero goodwill  and  missing values in
the  beginning or the end of the studied period,  the missing values were replaced with zero.
For companies with  goodwill,  missing values in the beginning or the end of  the studied period
were dropped.  Random checks in financial statements have been made and this extension of
the dataset seems to be representative.  The dataset has  also been checked  and adjusted  for
duplicates  and outliers.  Together, these  adjustments and  restrictions result  in an  effective
dataset  of  3259  companies,  where  1306  are companies with goodwill  and  1953  are  companies
without.  It is worth mentioning that the goodwill and  the  non-goodwill group are almost equal
in size  making both groups  large enough for  comparative  analysis.

4.3.1  Operationalization of  key  variables
The aim of this  paper is to examine the  relationship between stock returns and  goodwill.  It is
logical to  analyse the relationship  from a shareholder´s perspective  since it is the market that
determines the  prices of  stocks.  Therefore, when operationalizing  the  main  independent
variable  goodwill, the  absolute value is  related to the book value of  common stock
shareholder´s  equity  (goodwill/equity).  In addition,  this measure ought to be appropriate due
to the fact that  impairments of goodwill are directly impaired against equity.  This is the

                                                                     11
reason  why large values of this measure have the potential of capturing the riskiness of
goodwill.  We call this measure  goodwillintensity.

value of zero  if  the company has no goodwill and one  if the company has goodwill.  The
purpose of the  separation  is to  be able to  compare the two groups. This variable will be
frequently used  throughout the paper.  The dependent variable is  absolute  monthly percentage
returns (with the appropriate adjustments for capital changes such as  stock  splits and
dividends) for all common stocks. The calculation of stock returns is as follows:

4.3.3  Estimating  market  betas
When estimating a  variable  later used in a  main regression, it is important that the estimate
includes as little bias as possible.  If the right-hand side variable of a regression is measured
with error, the slop coefficient will have  a downward bias  and  the intercept an upward bias  in
the main regression  (Bodie et al.  2011).  In order to avoid biased beta estimations, the beta  in

                                                                     12
this study  is calculated using market model  regressions,  which is an empirical version of  the
CAPM.  More specifically,  the Dimson  (1979)  aggregated coefficients  (AC)  method  is used  to
estimate stock  betas.  This method has been developed to overcome  biased estimations of
systematic risk (beta)  due to infrequently traded shares in the dataset.  The major bias occurs
when  prices recorded at the end of a time period represent the outcome of a transaction,  which
took place earlier in,  or prior to,  the period in question.  This results in serial correlation and
downward biased beta estimates.  The sample in this study  covers listed companies in the US
(NASDAQ, NYSE and AMEX  and other listed companies)  and  it is  therefore  probable  that
our price share data  is  subject to infrequent trading and  thin markets.  In  this method,  yearly
stock  betas  are  estimated as  the  sum  of  coefficients  from regressing daily  absolute  excess
stock return for stock  𝑖  in year  𝜏  on  daily  absolute  excess  market  return  in  year  𝜏. The  four
preceding  (lagged),  the  synchronous  (matching)  and  the  subsequent  (leading)  market returns
are  used as the  explanatory variables. According to Dimson  (1979),  at least  one leading term
and four  lagging terms are required  and his advice is  thus  followed  in this study.  The multiple
regressions model can be written as follows:

5. Methodology

The aim of this paper is  to investigate the relationship between goodwill and stock returns
during different economic conditions. This section will explain how the study is  conducted.
As main  sources  of inspiration for the methodology applied in this paper, we have used
Simuntin (2009) “Excess Cash and Stock Returns” and  Fama, French (1992)  “The cross-
section of Expected Stock Returns”.  Firstly,  the correlation between goodwillintensity  and
selected firm characteristics is explored.  Secondly,  we create  style  portfolios  based on
goodwillintensity  by dividing the sample  in a two-step procedure.  Thirdly, we present  the
main  regression setting  of the two-step Fama-MacBeth  regression  method  and  how  it  is used
to analyse the relationship between goodwill and stock returns.  Finally, other asset pricing
models that are used in the robustness test are presented.

5.2  Style  portfolio construction
When using a random sample of individual stocks in asset pricing models, it is most likely
that these stocks are representative for the market as a whole and that the market portfolio will
be the only factor to significantly explain the stock returns. Hence, it would be preferable to
find another method that can highlight risk factors other than the market portfolio. Previous
literature has  argued that forming portfolios is  one solution  that would solve this issue.
Grouping stocks avoid random sampling and increase the chances of emphasizing other
explanatory factors. There are other advantages as well. Firstly, when stocks are grouped into
portfolios, noise in individual stock returns is reduced (Chen et al, 1986).  Secondly,  stocks
may not have constant loadings on factors across time  but  portfolio construction allows stocks
to shift between groups when their factors change.
            As  early indicators of how to form goodwillintensity portfolios,  Table 2  and
Table  3  below  are used.  Table 2  shows the characteristics of the  goodwillintensity.  Based on
the information in the table, the  companies  were formed  into ten  goodwillintensity  groups  at
the end  of each calendar year  𝑡.  Decile one  includes companies with goodwillintensity over
zero and up to 10%,  and  decile two includes companies with goodwill between 10-20%  and
so  on  up to  decile 10,  which  includes companies with goodwillintensity larger than 90%.  This
method was chosen in order  to  create  portfolios with an appropriate scale of the continuous
variable  goodwillintensity.

5.3  Regression setting

5.3.1  Matching of data
When trying to explain stock returns with accounting data it is essential to consider the
structure of the data and how it should be matched. The accounting  data consists of fiscal
year-end data in (any month of) calendar year  𝑡  while stock returns are recorded at the end of
each month for calendar year  𝑡. However, it is not eligible to perform an arbitrary match
between the two for each month since  the  accounting  numbers  cannot be assumed to be
available to  investors  at fiscal year-end. In the US, companies  are required to file  the  so called
10-K reports  including accounting numbers  within 90 days of their fiscal year-ends. However,

                                                                     18
on average,  19.8% do not  submit their  reports  on time.  A common practise in earlier  work has
been to  assume  that the  accounting  data is available within three months of fiscal year-ends.
Fama, French (1992) use a more conservative  approach with a six months lag.  Considering
the  above, a four months lag  is  implemented in this study. The 4-month gap is structured as
follows: the accounting data for all fiscal yearends in calendar year  𝑡  − 1  (1990-2013) is
matched with stock returns for April of year  𝑡  to Mars of  𝑡  +  1.  This match together with the
declaration of time-series data is  a  prerequisite  for a successful Fama-MacBeth regression,
which is  described in the following section.

5.3.2 Two-stage Fama-MacBeth regression
As mentioned above,  the data is declared to be time-series  data.  The alternative to time-series
data is to use  a  panel data  setting.  Although panel data  setting  is generally considered to be a
solid  structure of the data, it  is not  applicable  in  this study.  The main  issue with using panel
data in  this case  is  that  fixed effects  regressions  will  automatically  drop variables that are
constant over time.  The dataset in this  study includes  accounting data  that  is constant over
year  𝑡  and  stock  returns  that  change on a monthly basis.  If a panel data setting together with
fixed effects  regression  had  been  applied,  the results would have omitted  the accounting data,
thus  eliminating the main independent variable goodwillintensity.  In addition, it is worth
mentioning that  none of the other panel data regressions are  fruitful to use  considering the aim
of this study.  Time-series data  methods, on the other hand,  can run multi-cross-sectional
regressions for each time unit  including  an alternative way  of  performing  analysis over time
without dropping  the  variable of interest.  Therefore,  a  time-series data regression method
called  the  two-stage Fama  -  MacBeth regression  is used.
             The two-stage Fama  -  MacBeth regression  is an implementation of  the  two-step
procedure  used in Fama, MacBeth (1973).  In the first step, a cross-sectional regression is
performed for each single time period, and in the second,  the final coefficient estimates are
calculated as the average of the first step cross-sectional coefficient estimates.  The idea is to
run cross-sectional regressions  at different points in time  and  the model is intended to  capture
across-portfolio characteristics  by  minimizing  within-portfolio variance  and thereby testing
the  CAPM.  Thus,  the Fama-MacBeth regression  tolerates  the use of panel data and cross-
sectional analysis  combined,  which is the primary reason for choosing this method.
Furthermore, it is  built to estimate parameters for asset pricing models, more precisely
estimate beta and risk  premium  for any risk factors that are expected to  determine asset prices.
However, it is important to note how  this regression setting provides  standard errors, which

                                                                     19
are corrected  for cross-sectional correlation  but not for time-series autocorrelation.  For  daily
or weekly  stock trading analysis this  error  is not considered to be an extensive  one,  since
stocks have weak time-series autocorrelation.  In  this study,  however,  monthly returns  are used
that  might have higher  autocorrelation  due to the  longer horizon.  The risk of a  potential bias
thus  needs  to be taken into consideration when  evaluating  the robustness of the results.
               The model specification is as follows.  Assume that  ri,t  is the return for stock  i  in
time  t.  Let F1,t, F2,t,…,Fm,t  be  factors which  explanatory  power  of stock returns  is tested.

First stage  regression:

                     𝑟!,!  =  𝛼!  +  𝛽!,!!𝐹!,!  +  𝛽!,!!𝐹!,!  +  ⋯ +  𝛽!,!! 𝐹!,!  +  𝜖!,!
where:

5.4  Other risk-factor  asset pricing  models
As presented in the theory section above, the CAPM  is the most well known  asset-pricing
model  from which many alterations have been made  resulting in  alternative  asset-pricing
models.  Fama-MacBeth that is used in the main regression setting is one of them.  In this
section,  two  other  relatively newly  developed  methods are described:  The Fama-French
three-factor model  and  the Cahart four-factor model.  The Fama-French  three-factor model is
an extension of the Capital Asset Pricing Model  (CAPM)  and is constructed in order to  better
explain the  cross-sectional  variation in stock returns.  Compared to the traditional asset pricing
model CAPM that  only  uses one  explanatory  variable,  the  market beta, this model uses  two
additional  explanatory  variables:  the  size  and the  value  factor.  The Cahart four-factor model

7. Analysis

The first conclusion that can be drawn from the results presented is that  stocks  with goodwill
seem to be  more volatile  over time  compared to securities without goodwill.  Recognizing the
higher mean betas for firms with goodwill further strengthens this argument.  In addition,
companies with goodwill  earn lower return in times  of market slowdowns and higher returns
in market upturns compared to companies without goodwill.  Finally,  the dynamics within the
style portfolios  demonstrate  how  the described effects during different market states are
higher  for companies with higher  goodwillintensity.  Considering that investors are known to
be willing to take on more risk in times of a stable and optimistic market, the goodwill stock
might tend to rise, due to shrinking required risk premiums. On the contrary, in market
downturns,  given  that investors  are loss averse  and less willing to take on more risk, investors
are more careful and require  a  higher risk premium  for  holding the goodwill  stock.  The
greater underperformance in  downturns  compared  to  outperformance in  upturns  indicates
stronger effects  in times of market slowdowns,  which  is further evidence of loss aversion and
the incorporated risk in these stocks.
             One possible concern  is that  a  part of the  effects during  market downturns  might
be caused by  higher debt  in companies with  higher goodwill  or by forced new  issues  of stock.
As shown in  Table  3,  goodwillintensity  can be assumed to  covariate  with debt  and previous
literature argue that  companies with large goodwill have  a higher tendency of issuing new
shares in market slowdowns. Therefore, it is hard to extract the effect on return  from  the

                                                                     26
possible effects from these  variables. However,  even though  the results are not  filtered  for
underlying or linked effects, goodwillintensity may act as a proxy for  a dimension of risk
representing  underlying  fundamental risk determinants  such as  acquisition  financing.
             Table  5  showed  a positive  increase in the mean value-weighted return from
goodwillintensity decile one to decile ten.  This result was further explored in FamaMacBeth
regressions  where a positive  relationship between goodwillintensity and  future  absolute  stock
returns was found.  These results indicate that higher goodwillintensity  result in  higher  stock
returns.  This result  can be interpreted in two ways.  The first  possible  explanation  is that  high
goodwillintensity companies are  generally  relatively underpriced  compared to companies
without goodwill and thus earn higher returns.  However, the underpricing theory does not
conform  well  to  the volatile nature of the goodwill stock, with sharper peaks and declines in
the stock returns.  Instead, a  second possible explanation is that  the goodwillintensity is
serving as a proxy for a risk factor that effects equilibrium expected returns.  This is a more
likely explanation to the observed results, implying that  investors require a risk premium  for
investing in companies with higher goodwillintensity.
             Even though this study performs a semi-strong test, the patterns observed  may
not  necessarily  contradict  the efficient market hypothesis  (EMH).  The CAPM  model, which is
based on the EMH,  includes the systematic risk as the only explanatory variable  for stock
returns.  However, research has shown that  idiosyncratic risk  can be transformed into
systematic risk when information  about the security is sparse.  It might seem natural to view
the risk of goodwillintensity  as being a form of idiosyncratic risk. However,  due to the
information asymmetry  and information risk belonging to this balance sheet item, the entailed
risk transform into systematic risk.  This  reasoning  implies  that  the risk of goodwillintensity
might explain a part of the  true  systematic  risk  used in the  CAPM model.
             On the contrary, another viewpoint is  that  the results  can be  seen  as  evidence of
inefficient markets, thus rejecting the EMH.  Investors  might  overemphasise  past performance
when forecasting  the future  prospects  of the firm. This results  in overpriced firms with  higher
performance  in the past  and underpriced  firms with lower performance  in the past.  When
market participants discover the systematic errors in the forecasting, prices are reversed
towards  the intrinsic value of the firm.  This reasoning is linked to the  momentum and the
reversal effect.

8.  Robustness test
In order to test the robustness of the  findings  in the result section, this  part  further explores
whether  the higher returns earned by  companies with higher goodwillintensity  can be
explained by other risk factors  using a Fama-French regression setting.  Each month,  a
portfolio that takes a long position in stocks  with the highest goodwillintensity and a short
position in stocks with the lowest goodwillintensity  is created. The  positions are then  hold
without rebalancing for the next 12 months.  The high-low goodwillintensity portfolio is
regressed on  risk factors  from 3-  and 4-factor models.  A series of regressions are  then
conducted and the results are shown in  Table  10  below.  As can be seen in the table,  none  of
the regression settings, including the three-factor  Fama-French  model  and the  Cahart  four-
factor  model,  can explain  the  superior  performance of the top goodwillintensity  decile over
the lower  goodwillintensity  decile.  In fact, when adding controls in the table below,  the
intercept only increases.  As a result, the interpretation of  a risk premium associated  with

Another  possible concern is the proxies and assumptions made in this study. One example is
the assumption that accounting data is available four months after fiscal year-ends. A
sensitivity analysis  was therefore  performed, using both  a  three-month lag and  a  six-month

Another  assumption is the definition of  goodwill intensity. When changing the definition to
goodwill divided by total assets and using total assets as a base in the control ratios instead of

equity,  the results still hold.  Nonetheless,  changing  other  assumptions  made in this study
might  still  generate different results.

9.  Problematization
When interpreting the results in this paper, it could be misleading to argue for  causality
between goodwillintensity and future absolute returns.  It is possible that  higher
goodwillintensity leads to higher  future  stock returns. However, it might also be the other way
around.  Higher stock returns might  provide firms with more capital, thus enabling  more
acquisitions.  The results should therefore be interpreted with  caution.
             Perhaps the main  weakness  in  the results is the general  difficulty of  interpreting
semi-strong tests,  which  usually  rejects CAPM  and  the  efficient  market hypothesis.  The
difficulty occurs since these models  need to adjust for risk before  evaluating portfolio
performance.  Beta has commonly been  used to  adjust  for risk, which  is also the case in this
paper. Even if beta theoretically  should be able to represent the  overall market-risk factor, the
estimated beta might  differ from  the true,  theoretical beta and might therefore  not be
representative.  Thus,  other proxies  in the main Fama-MacBeth regression and in the Fama-
French  regression  have  been used  to control for risk. However,  the chance of inappropriately
adjusting for risk  due to invalid  proxies and data  still remains.  Our result that  the  high-low
goodwill portfolio  can generate superior  return  might  be misleading if the risk adjustment
procedure  is incorrect.
             There  is also a  risk of data  mining. Datamining  implies  that a factor  can appear
to  predict future stock return  by  chance.  However, there seems to be one to many  linked
factors significantly affecting stock returns for rejecting the anomalies.  One solution to the
risk of data mining could be to replicate the research with another dataset and see if the same
results  are obtained.

10.  Conclusion

This paper documents a positive relationship between goodwillintensity and absolute future
stock returns.  The mean value-weighted return for companies with goodwill is  0.45%  higher
than for companies without goodwill. Dividing the sample into style portfolios based on
goodwillintensity,  companies with high goodwillintensity outperform companies with low
goodwillintensity  by  0.11%.  The  superior performance  of  a portfolio that takes a long position
in  stocks with the highest goodwillintensity  and a short position  in  stocks with the lowest
goodwillintensity  persists  when  running  three-  and four-factor asset pricing models.  The
positive relationship  is likely  to be caused by a required risk  premium. Due to  the extra risk of
information imperfections incorporated in the goodwill item, investors require a
compensation  for investing in companies with higher goodwill.
             The empirical evidence  also  suggests  a volatile nature for  firms with goodwill
generally  showing  higher market betas  than companies without goodwill.  Furthermore,
companies with goodwill earn lower returns  in times of market slowdowns and higher returns
in  times of  market expansions.  This result is further supported by the dynamics within the
goodwillintensity style portfolios, showing lowest return for the highest goodwillintensity
firms  in market downturns and the highest return in market upturns.  In addition, the range
between the lowest market state and the highest market state  is greatest for the portfolio with
the  highest  goodwillintensity.  These findings can be explained by investors being more
willing to take on more risk in market upturns than downturns, which results in  lower  risk
premium  in upturns  and higher  risk premium  in downturns.  To conclude, goodwillintensity
help in explaining cross-sectional variation is future stock returns.

11. Future research

The results of this paper do  not contribute to the precise nature of the underlying  economical
risk factors.  Therefore, a further study could examine possible  underlying  or related
economical risk factors  that might help in explaining stock returns, such as  “number of
acquisitions”  or  “acquisition financing”.  In addition,  weak-form test theories are  to some
extent  discussed in this paper. However, due to the restrictions of semi-strong tests in this
study,  statistical  weak-form  tests  have not been included.  Therefore,  more  thorough  weak-
form tests  could  be  another  suggested further study  to  test for  momentum and  reversal  effects
in goodwill stocks.  A third  suggestion for further research  regarding the goodwill item  is to
loosen  the rational investor assumption  and allow for irrational behaviour.

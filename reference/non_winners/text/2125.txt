             Value versus Growth on OMXS and
          Asymmetric Responses to Earnings Surprises

                             Carl Elfving  and  Zacharias Sjöberg*

                                 Abstract

Numerous studies have  been conducted  worldwide  mapping  differences in stock returns based on
value versus growth  characteristics.  This paper  examines  whether there are  differences in quarterly
returns between value and growth stocks on  OMX  Stockholm (OMXS)  2004-2014.  The  obtained
results are tested with econometric methods to determine if the  differences  are  explained by
asymmetric reactions to earnings surprises  between the stock classes.  Using  quarterly data for
companies listed on the OMXS subsequent to the dot-com bubble burst,  we form equally  weighted
portfolios based on the financial ratios  Book-to-Price  (B/P),  Earnings-to-Price  (E/P) and  Dividend-
to-Price  (D/P).  Growth portfolios consist of the lowest 30% of  each measure and value portfolios
consist of the  highest 30% of  each  measure.  The results show controversial evidence of  growth
stocks outperforming value stocks  in the B/P and E/P portfolios, while no difference in quarterly
returns are observed between  the D/P portfolios. Furthermore, we find that more pronounced
reactions to positive earnings surprises for growth stocks  partly  explain  the differences  in quarterly
return.

Keywords: Value, Growth,  Asymmetric Reactions, Earnings Surprises

2. Introduction

For many years, value stocks have earned  higher average return compared to growth stocks  (Fama &
French, 1992; 1993; Lakonishok, et al., 1994; Basu, 1977; Jaffe, et al., 1989).  The value premium  –
denoting  superior performance of value stocks  –  has  since the discovery  baffled economists and
resulted in speculations of investor overreactions  (Lakonishok, et al., 1994; Daniel, et al., 1998),
phenomenon  uncaught by regular asset pricing models,  and fundamental adjustments of  firms’
systematic  risk  (Berk, et al., 1999; Carlsson, et al., 2004; Zhang, 2005). Consistent with the risk-
return tradeoff theory, Zhang  (2005)  argues that a value  premium ought to  exist,  as value firms are
less flexible to adjust  their  asset bases  and costs to meet negative shocks during recessions. On the
contrary,  Lakonishok et al.  (1994)  find no support for the  supposed  higher risk and argue that the
value premium is explained by a contrarian theory  dependent on faulty expectations from investors
and agency problems troubling institutional investors.

Following  the conclusions of  Lakonishok et al.  (1994)  related to the absence of excess risk in value
firms, criticism to the value premium has emerged. The premium is largely dependent  on  the
portfolio  formation  and is mainly examined on US equity markets  (Brailsford, et al., 2012).  The
value premium  is also argued to be sample specific  (Scislaw & McMillan, 2012)  and exposed to
several  biases  (Andrikopoulos, et al., 2006). When Fama and French  (1998)  examined  international
portfolios in order to estimate the pervasiveness of the value  premium globally,  US and Japanese
stocks accounted for a  total weight of 73.5%.  In these  two countries individually, the value premium
was statistically verified in 5 out of 6 portfolios. Interestingly, when Fama and French  (1998)  formed
country specific portfolios, they only managed to show  significant evidence of the value premium in
11 out of 39 cases. Yan  et al.  (2004)  stress that Fama and French’s  (1998)  study does not examine
the smaller stock markets in detail, using Singapore as an example with  an average weight of 0.7% in
Fama and  French’s  international portfolio. The same  discussion can be applied for  the Swedish
market, with an average weight  of 0.8% in the same portfolio. To provide further insight of the
premium on smaller stock markets, we  focus on  the premium in Sweden.

This paper  studies  the time period subsequent to the one  examined by Fama and French  (1998).
The chosen timeframe is of interest as limited research on the value premium has been made after
the new  millennium, a time when  Swedish companies have experienced revolutions in information

technology and increased productivity  -  coined as “the new economy”  (Andersson & Ådahl, 2005).
The years 1998-2003  contain  substantial shocks related to the dot-com bubble and the  counter
reactions that followed. To retrieve data representing normal market conditions, these years  have
been excluded.  Furthermore, the years 1995 to 1997 have  been excluded in order to  obtain  a
coherent and continuous sample (for further clarification, see  section 4.1). Thus, quarterly data from

The “value investment” strategy is widely used by investors despite the fact that the  research  outside
the US is inconclusive  (Brailsford, et al., 2012). With this in mind,  the aim of this study is to answer
the question:

         (i)  Is there a difference in return between value and growth stocks on OMXS 2004-2014?

Following our conclusions from question (i), we aim to determine the reason for the  potential
difference in returns.  Fama and French  (1993)  find a significant value premium in their  US  market
portfolio  between  1975-1995.  However,  Lakonishok  et  al.  (1994)  provide  evidence  that  the
differential return is not due to higher fundamental risk for value stocks.  Skinner and Sloan  (2002)
suggest that  the  stock return  differentials can be explained by investor overreactions.  This concept is
unexamined  on the  vast  majority of markets,  including the OMXS. We therefore aim to answer the
second  question:

        (ii) Could  any  potential  difference  in return  be explained by asymmetric stock price reactions to earnings
        surprises between  value and growth stocks?

Our results for research question (i) contradict the previous, primarily American, research by
showing a growth premium  in two out of three portfolio measures  during the examined time period.
Evaluating the findings from question (ii), we provide evidence that  the growth premium  partially
can be explained by a more pronounced reaction to  positive earnings surprises for growth stocks.
No difference in reaction to negative earnings surprises has been found between the stock classes.

3. Previous Literature

Previous  studies of the value premium are abundant, but the methodology varies. Fama and French
(2006), among others, examine the US equity market using value-weighted portfolios with size
breakpoints based on the New York Stock Exchange. Other researchers determine the  same
breakpoints based on all companies in the sample  (Brailsford, et al., 2012). Depending on the
methodology, the results differ. Fama and French  (Fama & French, 1993; 2006)  argue that the value
premium is pervasive across all sizes of US stocks, with a somewhat stronger result for  smaller
stocks. However, Kothari et al.  (1995)  and Loughran  (1997)  imply that their results are non-
conclusive as the premium in fact  only  can be seen in the portfolios containing smaller US stocks.

The research on non-American  markets is more limited. Evidence of  the value premium has been
seen  on  the Japanese stock market  (Daniel, et al., 2001)  and the Australian stock market  (Halliwell, et
al., 1999; Gaunt, 2004; Kassimatis, 2008)  but the results are only conclusive within the three out of
five largest market capitalization portfolios. Chui and Wei  (1998)  find the opposite,  namely  that the
value premium exists  in the smallest market capitalization portfolios, on several Pacific-Basin stock
markets. Due to the differences in portfolio formation methodology  a comparison is difficult to do.
In order to fully examine the effect  of the growth and value characteristics, this  paper  forms
portfolios solely on the respective key figure (Book-to-Price,  Earnings-to-Price  and  Dividend-to-
Price)  not taking the size of the company into consideration.

Studies examining  the difference in  stock price reactions to earnings  surprises are  rare. La Porta et al.
(1997)  find  that  abnormal expectations on  growth  stocks result in  inferior return around quarterly
report releases. Skinner and Sloan  (2002)  show that the return around the quarterly reports differs as
growth stocks react more pronounced to negative deviations from consensus earnings estimates
than value stocks, while growth stocks outperform value stocks during normal market conditions.
Moreover, they do not find a difference in reaction to positive surprises between the stock classes.
The results of Skinner and Sloan  (2002)  are consistent with Poorebrahimi et al.  (2012),  showing that
negative earnings surprises increase  volatility more than positive earnings surprises. In contrast to
Poorebrahimi et al.  (2012)  among others, this paper does not aim to explain the  difference in returns
by connecting it to the efficient market hypothesis, but rather through regressing it on asymmetric
stock price reactions to surprises.

4.3 Data Treatment
Firstly,  Bloomberg reports the measures Price-to-Book, Price-to-Earnings and  Dividend-to-Price.
To ease the portfolio formation process, the price-to-book and price-to-earnings ratios retrieved by
Bloomberg have been  inverted into  Book-to-Price  and  Earnings-to-Price.  This has been done  to  be
consistent  in the denotations for the value and growth portfolios. Rearranging P/B and P/E into
B/P and E/P allows  all portfolios with high figures  (top 30%)  in the respective financial ratio (B/P,
E/P, D/P) to be  allocated into  value portfolios (denoted with an indicating “H”, for High). The
same is true for the firms with low  B/P,  E/P  and D/P  (lowest 30%),  being  allocated into growth
portfolios (denoted with an indicating “L”, for Low). Details of  portfolio formation  are provided  in
section 5.1.

Secondly, outliers in the return  and surprise  variables  have been disregarded  from  the data set. The
highest and lowest 0,135% of the observed values in the variables return and surprise have  been
dropped through omitting outliers:

                          Outlier1 <  μ  -  3σ  and  μ  +  3σ  < Outlier2

Furthermore, concerning the regressions aiming to  test  the surprise effect on stock returns, all
observations with missing values in the surprise variable  have been dropped.  This is due to the lack
of news-value from the observations where the  consensus  earnings  forecast  is missing.

5.2 Econometric Methods
Section 6:  Empirical Results  provides  paired t-tests for the difference in quarterly returns for growth
and value stocks in each of the different portfolios (H-LB/P, H-LE/P and H-LD/P). The purpose
of the tests is to answer question (i) stated in the introduction, namely whether a stock  return
differential  can be observed between value and growth stocks on OMXS  2004-2014.

Following the  results from the t-tests, quarterly stock  returns are regressed on portfolio belonging,
sign of  earnings  surprise and magnitude of the earnings surprise. Furthermore, variable interaction
between portfolio belonging and the  independent  variables are included in the regression models.
These variables’ coefficients provide  the  differential  return  in regards to portfolio allocation. The
regressions aim to  investigate  whether  a  potential difference in return between the growth and value
portfolios can be explained by asymmetrical stock  price reactions to  surprises between the
portfolios.  The regression equations are analogous to the ones used  by Skinner and Sloan  (2002).

This regression specification allows for a simple, straightforward, interpretation of the coefficients.
The intercept measures the  mean  return of a value stock, while the coefficient of Growth  provides
the estimated difference in return between growth and value stocks. If Growth loads with a  positive
(negative)  coefficient,  this infers  that there is a higher (lower) return for  growth stocks than value
stocks. The coefficient of SignSurprise  states  whether  the sign on the surprise is a determinant factor
of  quarterly returns.  This  coefficient is expected to be positive  and significant,  assuming  that
quarterly returns correlates  with  the earnings surprises.  Going forward, the  coefficient of  the variable
Surprise determines if the magnitude of the surprise has an impact on quarterly returns.  The
interaction terms SignSurprise*Growth and Surprise*Growth measures incremental return for
growth stocks reporting non-zero surprises, accounting for  sign and magnitude, respectively.

second  regression  model  control for such factors:

As opposed to regression model  (1), regression  model  (2)  does  not take the magnitude of the
surprise into consideration, but rather  focuses on  the portfolio allocation  and the sign of the
surprise. It allows for asymmetric responses to positive and  negative surprises  between stocks with
value versus growth characteristics.

Similar to regression (1), regression  (2) allows for an  intuitive interpretation of the coefficients. The
intercept  is interpreted as  the expected return for a value stock  reporting a zero surprise  (Surprise =
0).  The coefficient of the variable Growth measures  the incremental expected return from being
allocated into a growth portfolio  and  reporting a zero surprise. If Growth loads with a  positive
(negative)  coefficient,  the performance of the value portfolio is  inferior (superior)  to the growth
portfolio.  The coefficient of the  variable  Good  (Bad)  gives the incremental expected return for a
value stock from reporting a positive  (negative)  earnings surprise. The Good*Growth  (Bad*Growth)
variable is the  expected return  differential  between a growth stock reporting a positive  (negative)

surprise   and   a   value   stock   reporting   a   positive   (negative)   surprise.   If   the   coefficient   on

the same argumentation holds regarding the size of the coefficient.  No difference in reaction to

Concluding Section 7.2, we  do not find  a similar  growth premium  in the three-day data  as the one
observed in the quarterly data. Apart from the shortened return window, the executed regressions in
this section are analogous the regressions on the quarterly data. Our quarterly sample contains
observations  affected by pre-earnings announcements in the form of profit warnings.  The consensus
earnings forecasts in the quarterly data  are reported 90  days prior to the earnings announcements.
This implicates that  the profit warnings and their effect on stock price are taken into account  in the
regressions on quarterly returns. For the regressions on the shortened interval, the profit warnings’
effect on stock price and earnings forecasts  occurred before the three-day window. It  is thus already
adjusted for in both the earnings estimates and the stock price.  As the majority of profit warnings
occur when the firms fall short of the estimated earnings, the  three-day period fail to account for
these major declines in stock price.

Despite the absent  growth premium in the three-day  data, we provide evidence  of more pronounced
reactions to positive surprises for growth stocks in the B/P portfolio (see Table X). No asymmetric
reactions to negative surprises are seen in any of the  portfolios. These findings  are in line with the
regression results on  the  quarterly data.

8. Conclusion &  Future Research

For many years, value stocks have earned higher average return compared to growth stocks. The
value premium  –  denoting superior performance of value stocks  –  has  since the discovery
confounded economists.  Studies of  the  value premium  are mainly focused to the US equity market
and are debated to be inconclusive  on  smaller stock markets.  To contribute to the  research, this
paper focuses on the OMX Stockholm (OMXS) in 2004-2014.

We  provide  surprising  evidence of a growth premium on the OMXS for the portfolios formed on
Book-to-Price  (B/P)  and  Earnings-to-Price  (E/P)  during  the  period  2004-2014.  Our  results
contradict previous  research,  for which there are a number of potential reasons.  Firstly,  forming
equally  weighted portfolios  disregards  the size effect of companies  and  differs from previous
methods. It might serve to further  isolate the effect of the growth-  and value characteristics on
returns. Secondly, the time  span differs from previous research.  We have seen no indication that any
study has been done on the OMXS  during the period  following the turbulent dot-com bubble burst.
Thirdly, previous studies on the Swedish market lack significant results pointing towards either a
value-  or growth premium.

The found  growth premium on OMXS during the time period 2004-2014  serve as further  criticism
toward the established concept of the value premium. The results show that the value premium is
not consistently observed  on all markets. One  should note that previous research have shown that
the value premium is dependent on portfolio formation  (Brailsford, et al., 2012). The results  of this
paper primarily support  this conclusion.  However, it also shows that it is possible for investors to
receive superior returns by investing in growth stocks rather than value stocks on  the  OMXS. To
conclude, investors should not depend  solely  on  financial ratios and value stock characteristics  when
forming portfolios but rather  rely  on other properties  for determining  stocks that  will generate
superior returns. Further research in  the field has to be carried out  in order to determine why the
value premium during some periods, in certain  portfolios,  transforms  into a significant growth
premium.

This paper provides evidence that the growth premium on the OMXS during the  study  period can
partly be explained by the fact that growth stocks react more pronounced to positive surprises than

                                                                           31
value stocks. The  findings  show no difference in  reaction to negative surprises. For both positive
and negative surprises, the sign of the surprise is  a determinant factor for quarterly returns while the
magnitude is of lesser importance. It  is safe to conclude that earnings announcements are far from
the only surprises that firms encounter  during  a  fiscal quarter. To assume that the  surprises around
the earnings announcements fully explain  the difference in  quarterly  returns would therefore be
inappropriate.   We   control   for   this   fact   by   examining   three-day   returns   around   earnings
announcements. Within this sample, the same pronounced reaction  to positive  earnings surprises for
growth stocks is  observed in  the B/P portfolio. No asymmetric reaction to negative surprises was
observed.

Our findings imply that,  during times when investors expect reported earnings to beat the market’s
expectations  on the OMXS,  one should invest in stocks with growth characteristics as the price
reactions within this category are  more prominent  than  for value stocks. This  investment strategy
becomes even more attractive taking into account that the stock price reactions on shortcomings  in
earnings  are indifferent between the value and growth portfolios. The conclusion regarding superior
return of growth stocks during favorable market conditions  is consistent with previous research
(Skinner & Sloan, 2002).  Nonetheless  our study has been conducted during a period with fluctuating
market conditions and still shows superior performance of growth stocks.  Supplementary studies
have to be carried out  in order to determine whether this investment strategy holds for longer
timeframes on the OMXS.

9.  Problematization

Our data consists of an extensive sample of stocks listed on  OMXS for the past ten years. In many
studies examining the value premium, the time horizon is longer. We have limited our timeframe in
accordance with the specifications in Section 4.1  in order to  analyze the years subsequent to  Fama
and French  (1998). Despite that our chosen period may seem short,  we regard it  as  a sensible
limitation since the aim is  to analyze the market following the dot-com  bubble  burst.

Prior  research  data  is  known  to  be  subject  to  survivorship  biases  (Kothari,  et  al.,  1995;
Andrikopoulos, et al., 2006).  Survivorship bias  skews the results as  delisted companies and
companies that have filed for bankruptcy are omitted from the sample.  Attempts have been made to
minimize the survivorship bias in our data by manually controlling for delisted firms during the
period and through double-checking the  data against Datastream, I/B/E/S and Bloomberg.
However, the  quarterly  consensus forecasts used when examining the  asymmetric reaction to
surprises  have been difficult to gather for delisted firms.  This is something the reader should be
aware of.

Finally, another important limitation worth mentioning  concerns missing values in the data sample.
In the  initial  years of the examined period,  all companies were  not  covered by equity researchers and
thus report  missing  surprise  values. Also,  smaller firms are less covered than large firms in our
sample and  some companies have  only one earnings estimate  as base for  the consensus forecast. As
the  consensus  earnings  forecast  is the median of all  earnings  estimates, this has  a negative impact  on
the preciseness of the surprise estimates.

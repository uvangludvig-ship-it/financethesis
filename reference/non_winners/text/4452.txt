Value creation through spin-offs: Swedish evidence between 2010-2018

Abstract:
International studies have reported significant excess returns in conjunction with
announcements of spin-offs. However, there is to some extent a lack of professional
research on stock market reactions of spin-offs in Sweden. This study aims to
investigate whether spin-offs in Sweden after the 2008 global financial crisis create
shareholder value. The research is conducted using an event study investigating a
sample of 22 spun-off and parent firms. The findings suggest the abnormal returns are
lower the 100 trading days after the spin-off compared to the 100 trading days prior to it
at 5% significance. However, we can show that the spin-offs exhibit positive abnormal
returns over the full 200-day window at 10% significance. Results indicate that the
abnormal returns are mainly occur within the 100 trading days prior to the spin-off date,
where abnormal returns are positive at 1% significance. We speculate whether abnormal
returns connected to the spin-offs might be shifted in time to before the actual spin-off
date.

Keywords:
   Keywords: Spin-off, abnormal returns, event study, Swedish stock market, Lex Asea.

Definitions

Spin-off: Defined as the corporate action of separating one entity into two independent
and separately traded entities.

Parent: Refers to the entity which is noted on the stock market before the spin-off.

Spun-off entity: Defined as the part of the business that is separated from the parent.

Announcement date: Refers to the day when the parent is separated into two and the
two entities are traded independently.

Event window: Refers to the time period of interest after the announcement.

Estimation window: Refers to the period of time of interest before the announcement.

Abnormal return: The actual return less the expected return. Abnormal return can
either be positive or negative.

Cumulative abnormal return: An aggregate of the individual abnormal return over a
specified time frame.

Cumulative average abnormal return: Cumulate return based on average abnormal
return over a specified time frame.

1. Introduction

A spin-off is when a parent company decides to form a new separate entity consisting of
a part of the parent. The existing shareholders of the parent receives a proportionate, pro
rata, share of the new business - also called the spun-off entity. Following the spin-off,
the spun-off entity becomes an independently traded firm on the stock exchange with the
initial same owner structure as the parent company. Simplified, shareholders hold shares
in two traded firms instead of one after the spin-off. Unlike other divestiture actions, a
spin-off does not include a cash transaction and therefore is the corporate action not
considered to be motivated by the need of liquidity - contrary to for example an IPO.
             Mergers and acquisitions (M&As) aim to find value in generating synergies
between businesses by combining two or more entities. Meanwhile as a contrast, spin-
offs instead try to create value by the divestiture and separation of companies. The
antithesis between M&As and spin-offs can initially seem abstract. However, several
previous studies have found that spin-offs yield an abnormal return which is when the
actual return is higher or lower than the expected return. Abnormal return is found both
in conjunction with the announcement date, the date when the firm becomes two entities,
as well as in the long-term post announcement date.
             Previous research argues that the size of the abnormal returns can be
explained by various factors including industry classification and the level of prior
information asymmetry in the parent. The main reasons behind carrying through a spin-
off are that there are relatively small synergies between the business parts or that a part
of the business is unrelated to the core operations. A spin-off would therefore allow for
increased operational efficiency, strategic focus and managerial improvements (Cusatis,
Miles Woolridge, 1993, Dorsey, 2005).
              In comparison to M&As, corporate events such as spin-offs have received
less attention in the academic world. The existing research of spin-offs is predominantly
focused on the US. Meanwhile, the smaller sample of European studies often show
contrasting outcomes compared to the America studies. We hypothesize that by
narrowing down the scope to a national level the outcomes would differ even more.
              Additionally, many studies use a dataset that overlaps financial crises. One
assumption is that crises affect both management and investor behavior and sentiment

                                   4
which consequently affect the motivation and performance of spin-offs. Moreover,
valuation and value are non-static definitions which are not always comparable over time,
geography and markets. In the apparent absence of professional academic studies of spin-
offs in Sweden the aim is to test if the results from international studies are relevant today
on the Swedish stock market.
              This study aims to examine whether spun-off entities and parent
companies traded on the Swedish stock markets create shareholder value in conjunction
with spin-off announcements. This will be estimated in terms of abnormal returns over a
100 trading days’ time period before as well as after the announcement date. According
to hypothesis 1, the null hypotheses is that abnormal returns are equal during the 100
trading days following the announcement date compared to the 100 trading days
preceding the announcement date.

Hypothesis 1:

     H0: Abnormal returns 100 trading days preceding and 100 trading days
      following spin-off announcement are equal.
     H1: Abnormal returns 100 trading days preceding and 100 trading days
      following spin-off announcement are not equal.

              We also aim to determine if abnormal returns are significant over the
entire 200 trading days’ time period, the 100 trading days preceding the announcement
date as well as the 100 trading days following it.

Hypothesis 2:

     H0: Abnormal returns are equal to zero over
      a) the entire 200 trading day interval
      b) the 100 trading days preceding the spin-off announcement
      c) the 100 trading days following the spin-off announcement
     H1: Abnormal returns are not equal to zero over
      a) the entire 200 trading days’ interval
      b) the 100 trading days preceding the spin-off announcement
      c) the 100 trading days following the spin-off announcement

2. Literature review

In this section we will describe the theoretical base for this study. We will define what a
spin-off is, describe the theoretical framework efficient market hypothesis, outline
previous research findings to why spin-off generate abnormal returns including aspects
such as corporate focus and information asymmetry. Furthermore, we will also specify
the regulation of spin-offs on the Swedish market, known as Lex Asea. Lastly, we will
summarize the results from previous studies.

2.1. Definition of spin-offs

Managers engage in corporate restructurings in order to improve the organization. The
reason behind a restructuring can be operating underperformance (Lehn, Poulsen 1989),
stock market underperformance (Kaplan, 1991), economic shocks in the industry
(Mitchell, Mulherin 1996) and factors such as global competition, technology changes or
regulations (Jensen, Ruback, 1983, Jensen 1991). Restructuring can be done through
either organizational or ownership restructuring. In this study, we emphasize ownership
restructurings which in turn can be separated into private and public transactions.
             Public transactions are executed involving the stock market. These
transactions can be divided into spin-offs, split-offs, carve-outs and tracking stocks. An
equity carve-out is when the parent firm sells part of the shares of a subsidiary via an IPO
were the parent receives cash. In a split-off, the parent firm shareholders are allowed to
exchange their shares in the parent to shares in the new subsidiary. A tracking stock is a
common stock that tracks the performance of a certain division.
              A spin-off is defined as a pro-rata distribution of shares of a fully owned
subsidiary to the parent’s shareholders. Consequently, the subsidiary becomes a new and
independently traded entity on the equity market and is separated from the parent (Cusatis
et al., 1993). Subsequent to the spin-off, the shareholders in the parent now holds two
securities – both in the parent and the spun-off new entity. As the shareholders have stocks
in both companies, the shareholders can individually choose how they desire to construct
their portfolio. The separation implicates that the spun-off entity can engage in
independent decision-making (Johnson, Klein, Thibodeaux, 1996). Spin-offs differ to

                                   6
other ownership restructurings since a spin-off is not associated involving exchange of
cash. This corporate action is not driven by the need of capital or liquidity, which can be
the case for financially distressed companies (Desai, Jain, 1999).

2.2. The efficient market hypothesis

The efficient market hypothesis implies that market prices should at any point in time
reflect all the available information to investors. Theoretically, this implies that investors
cannot achieve abnormal returns since the pricing is always correct. However, the theory
regarding the efficient market is dependent on the assumption that investors have identical
information, act rationally and therefore interpret information equivalently. The
hypothesis also assumes that there are no taxes or transaction costs and investors have
equal forecasts of cash flow (Berk, DeMarzo 2014).
         According to Fama (1970) the level of information efficiency can be
separated into three forms of market efficiency; strong, semi-strong and weak.
              In the strong form state, it is impossible to achieve superior returns based
on any information, whether the information is public or private. Therefore, information
asymmetry does not exist, and all information is reflected in the market prices.
Confidential information cannot be used to gain superior returns and insider traders have
no information advantage.
             The semi-strong form implies that market prices are reflected by all
available public information. This includes historical prices and returns as well as public
information that may affect the market price such as the macro environment, industry
news and company specific news. Therefore, the only way to obtain superior returns is
by inside or private information.
             In the weak form state market prices are based on historical prices and
returns. Price changes follow a random walk, which is an unpredictable factor.

2.3. Motives to spin-offs

2.3.1. Information asymmetry
Krishnaswami and Subramaniam (1999) studied whether spin-offs decrease information
gaps between the management of the firm and the external capital market. They argue
that spin-offs reduce information asymmetry by revealing information concerning the
spun-off entity and parents’ efficiency and its cash flows. Separate information and
financial reporting leads to a reduction of information asymmetry and therefore improve
fair market valuation. The authors also find that firms with more information asymmetry
prior to the spin-off in relation to their industry and size counterparts choose to spin-off
subsidiaries more frequently. Firms with more information asymmetry tend to exhibit
higher announcement returns and the information asymmetry also tend to decrease
following the spin-off.
             Gilson, Healy, Noe, and Palepu (2001) find that analyst coverage both in
quantity and quality improve following a spin-off. Resulting in stronger accuracy in
earnings forecasts. The authors argue that the enhanced precision in forecasting is due to
better information disclosure in both the parent and spun-off entity.
             Similarly, Habib, Johnsen, and Naik (1997) also argues for an information-
based explanation for the level of abnormal return for spin-offs and parents. If entities are
traded separately the number of traded securities on the market would increase.
Consequently, the price system would become more informative and uninformed
investors would be able to take better decisions, which in turn would improve the quality
of investments. Both parents and the spun-off entities increase in value due to the
reduction of information asymmetry.
              As an opposing view, some research suggests that spin-offs would rather
increase information asymmetry. The theory is based on how informed traders can use
its private information more accurately and therefore benefit at the expense of uninformed
traders. Huson and MacKinnon (2003) compare the benefits of a spin-off between
informed and uninformed traders where informed traders have information advantage
concerning a specific division in the firm. If a company realize a spin-off the share prices
become more volatile to firm-specific information. This would be exploited by informed
investors making their precision of valuation more accurate since their private
information can be complemented by the additional public information that follows a

                                   8
spin-off – such as financial reporting and press releases. The implications suggest that
spin-offs increase information asymmetry and informed traders would gain at the expense
of uninformed traders. As a solution, the information asymmetry can be neutralized in a
more diversified firm instead of several separate entities.
             In the European study written by Veld and Veld (2004) they do not find any
relationship of how abnormal returns can be explained by the level of information
asymmetry in a firm before the spin-off.
             A closely related topic to information asymmetry is agency theory. Due to
the information asymmetry the more informed party, agent, engages in actions that is in
line with their own best interest which negatively impact the less informed party,
principals. In this perspective, the management in terms of the agent undertake actions
which will not guarantee the shareholders, principals, interest of value maximization
(Pratt, Zeckhauser, 1985). If spin-offs reduce information asymmetry as research suggest
this could indicate that the management’s and shareholders interest are more in
accordance which would benefit value maximization.
          Another solution to the information asymmetry could be signaling. When
the principal cannot observe the characteristics of the agent, the agent can send a credible
signal to the principal reducing information asymmetry (Spence, 1973). In this context,
the theory implies that management can send signals to the investors which decreases the
information asymmetry.

2.3.2. Corporate focus hypothesis
Desai and Jain (1999) compare the abnormal returns between corporate focus increasing
spin-offs and non-corporate focus increasing spin-offs. Corporate focus is measured in
three different ways:
    1) As the number of segments in a company. If the number of segments decreases
        the corporate focus increases.
    2) As spin-offs where the parent operates in a different industry compared to the
        spun-off entity. This is also called a cross-industry spin-off.
    3) As the Herfindahl index. The index is the sum of squares of each segment’s
       revenue divided by the total revenue.

                                   9
       The authors find that focus increasing spin-offs exhibit significantly higher
announcement returns as well as long-run abnormal returns compared to their
counterparts – those who are not focus increasing.
               Daley, Mehrotra, and Sivakumar (1997) examined cross-industry spin-offs.
Cross-industry spin-offs is defined as when the parent and spun-off entity are classified
by separate Standard Industry Classification codes. They find that cross-industry spin-
offs generate more value in terms of higher abnormal returns and a higher operating
performance than intra-industry spin-offs. An intra-industry spin-off is when the spun-off
entity and parent still operate within the same industry. They attribute this value
enhancement to corporate focus. The authors conclude that cross-industry spin-offs
discharge unrelated divisions and hence support managers to focus on the core operations
in which they are best suited to manage and lead.
            Similarly, Berger and Ofek (1995) suggest that diversification reduces
value. In this case, diversification refers to having a conglomerate operating in multiple
industries instead of a more focused and specialized business. The authors estimate that
if the segments of a conglomerate operated separately they would have achieved higher
operating profitability and valuation. Hence, the equity of diversified companies is traded
at discount which they estimate to 13-15%. According to the authors a spin-off is an
approach to increase focus and prevent diversification discount.
             Comment and Jarell (1995) support the findings of Berger and Ofek (1995)
regarding diversification discount. They argue that increased corporate focus is consistent
with value creation, efficiency and shareholder wealth maximization.
              Other studies have also argued that corporate focus leads to value creation.
John and Ofek (1995) presents a hypothesis arguing that focus facilitate for managers to
concentrate on the core business. More efficient operations and improved performance
would in turn result in value creation.

2.3.3. Geographical focus
A raised issue in the prevalent literature is whether geographical focus increase or
decrease firm value. Geographical focus is when a firm spin-off a foreign subsidiary. Veld
and Veld (2004) suggest three reasons to why geographical focus would reduce value.

                                  10
The three reasons are:
    1) Geographical focus can lead to decreased economies of scale in production.
    2) The company can be at a relative disadvantage to international competitors.
    3) If management decides to increase geographical focus it would signal to the
       shareholders and capital markets that the management might have engaged in a
       previous poor decision of expanding into foreign markets.
            As a contrasting view, Veld and Veld (2004) also put forward three
arguments that would support the assumption that geographical focus increase firm value.
The arguments are:
       1) Global companies are more complex in comparison to domestic firms.
           Reduction of complexity can decrease monitoring and coordination costs.
       2) Managers might in the first place engaged in geographical diversification in
          order to decrease their own personal risk of failure at the expense of
           shareholder value. The action to increase geographical focus would indicate
           that managers unravel their previous poor decisions.
        3) The opportunity to cross-subsidize underperforming segments in the company
           would decrease.
            However, Veld and Veld (2004) found geographical focus as a negligible
factor explaining abnormal returns associated with the announcement. The authors
conclude that the geographic focus effect on abnormal returns is dependent of the relative
strengths of focus and if the positive effects overweigh the negative.

2.3.4. Relative size
How the relative size of a spin-offs effects the abnormal return is a matter of academic
debate. The relative size refers to the proportion of assets spun-off from the parent.
Previous research suggests that abnormal returns are higher when a larger percentage is
spun-off. However, the scope of the prevalent research is often limited to only study how
relative size explains the level of abnormal returns of the parent.
             Chemmanur and Yan (2004) argued that the reason behind the size effect
on abnormal returns is based on the assumption that the parent is more probable to become
a targeted company for a takeover which would increase firm value. Other researchers

                                  11
found similar observations including Krishnaswami and Subramaniam (1999) and Veld
and Veld (2004).
             Kirchmaier (2003) provides an insight that adds a dimension to the results
in the above referred studies. In contrast to the other studies, he argued that the observed
size correlations seem to be neutralized in the long run and also that relatively smaller
spin-offs present higher excess returns over time.

2.3.5. Regulation and taxes
The Swedish Lex Asea regulation (1999:1229) was introduced in 1991 in order to
facilitate for corporate restructurings. The regulation enabled spin-offs to become tax
exempt which incentivize companies to engage in spin-offs. As a result of the law
shareholders are not required to pay usual capital taxes, instead the tax is postponed until
the sale of the distributed shares (Skatteverket, 2011). The frequency of spin-offs has
increased in Sweden after the implementation of the law.
            Certain criteria have to be fulfilled in order to qualify for a Lex Asea
treatment according to the Swedish law (SFS 2007:1419), including:
    1) The parent company must be traded on the Swedish (or in the EES area) stock
       market
    2) The shares must be distributed pro rata.
    3) The new company was previously a subsidiary and the shares of the new company
        are distributed.
             American studies have found observations of a relationship between tax and
regulatory motives to engage in spin-offs (Copeland, Lemgruber, Mayers 1987, Schipper,
Smith 1983). In contrast, Veld and Veld (2004) suggest that these variables are irrelevant
for the European market since it is normally possible to defer taxes on the capital gains
from a spin-off in Europe.

2.4. Summary of results from previous studies

As previously mentioned, spin-offs are a well-researched subject in the US. The first
studies were conducted in the 80s and the phenomenon gained high attention in the 90s.
Meanwhile, this type of corporate event is relatively less studied in Europe in general and
Sweden in particular. The existing Swedish studies is predominantly carried out by
bachelor students and the only professional study focusing on Swedish spin-offs is written
by Scheutz (1988). However, this study can be difficult to compare with since it was
conducted prior to the implementation of Lex Asea in 1991.
             The prevalent research is mainly focused on abnormal returns in
conjunction with announcement of the spin-off rather than the long-term value impact.
Research has reported cumulative abnormal returns ranging between 1% to
approximately 5% over small event and estimation window of three days. An event
window refers to the time period of interest after the announcement meanwhile
estimation window refers to the period of time of interest before the announcement.
            However, a few studies do not find any relationship between abnormal
returns and spin-offs, one example could be Schipper and Smith (1983). Several studies,
such as Kirchmaier (2003), Lehtonen (2008) and Cusatis et al. (1993), have found
negative abnormal returns over a 6-month event window.
              Many researches have studied the short-term wealth effects of spin-offs. In
these studies, such as Johnson et. al (1994) and Hite and Owers (1983), have found
positive abnormal returns prior to the announcement date. However, they also find
negative abnormal returns after the announcement date.
             On the contrary, evidence on long-term wealth suggest positive abnormal
returns both prior to the announcement and years after. This is the case both for parents
and spun-off entities. Cusatis et. al (1993) studied a period of three years after
announcement of spin-offs in the US. The authors reported significant positive abnormal
return of approximately 17% for both parents and spun-off entities during the three-year
period.

This chapter includes a description of the data selection process, the collected data as well
as the methodology used to answer the research question. In the data section, we will
specify which spin-offs this study examines, and the data associated with them. In the
methodology section we will describe the methodology to measure and calculate the
returns and how to test the power and significance of the results.

3.1. Data

data for the final sample of this study. First of all, due to the Swedish spin-off tax
regulation Lex Asea, the Swedish National Tax Agency publishes a list of historical spin-

3.1.2. Share prices and index
Infront/FactSet has been used to retrieve historical daily share prices for each individual
spin-off and parent over the estimation and event windows. Infront/FactSet has also been
used in order to collect the historical daily market index prices. We have chosen OMXSGI
as market index since the all-share index take the value of all publicly traded stocks into
consideration and thus provide a more general picture of all publicly traded stocks on the
Stockholm Stock Exchange. Both OMX and First North are included in the OMXSGI.
Furthermore, OMXSGI reinvests dividends which is in accordance with our dataset and
methodology.
              Regarding limitations of the index, the OMXSGI is not representative for a
specific industry but only to the market in general. This implies that a share with a certain
industry classification code can exhibit different abnormal returns in relation to the
OMXSGI, compared to the development of a relevant industry specific index.

3.2. Methodology

3.2.1. Event study methodology
According to MacKinley (1997), an event study examines whether a certain event can
influence the return of a company. The actual returns following the event can be compared
to the expected returns and thereby determine whether the event gives rise to abnormal
returns. The author presented seven steps in the process of carrying out an event study.

                                  17
             Firstly, the task is to define the event and estimate the interesting time
period, also known as the event window. In this thesis we will focus on spin-offs
conducted in Sweden over a 100 trading days’ event window after the announcement.
Secondly, a selection criterion over which data to include must be carried out. The
selection process and collected data is described in section 3.1.1 and 3.1.2. Thirdly, you
need to determine a model in order to calculate the abnormal returns. Our abnormal return
approach is defined in the methodology section 3.2.3.
             Fourth, the estimation window can be determined. The estimation window
is used to calculate the expected returns and can be described as the period of returns for
each day before the announcement date. The event window and estimation window do
not overlap. This implies that the event period is not included in the estimation period in
order to avoid the spin-off from affecting the results. In this case the 100 trading days
prior to the announcement date constitute our estimation window. Figure 1 provides an
overview of the timeline of an event study where 0 is the announcement date.

Figure 1: Event study timeline

             The fifth step includes defining the null hypothesis and calculating the
abnormal returns. Sixth, tests are run to determine statistical significance. We will
accomplish this by regressing the data set over the estimation and event windows.
Seventh, a presentation of the empirical results and conclusions is to be compiled.
             The event study methodology is applied to the first hypothesis, where we
want to investigate the difference in abnormal returns before and after the spin-off
announcement date. By doing this we compare the sample data found within the
estimation window to the sample data found within the event window.

                                  18
3.2.2. Regression methodology
Continuing on the first hypothesis, we run OLS-regressions on the sample data to
conclude the statistical power of our findings. Regressions are run using two-tailed t-tests
due to the intrinsic uncertainty of results regarding whether they might be positively or
negatively deviating.
            Dummy variables are used to signify the estimation and event windows
respectively, set to 0 for the estimation window observations and set to 1 for the event
window observations. We can further analyze the calculated slope coefficients, standard
errors as well as t-statistics and p-values to arrive at a significance level of our results.
             The abnormal return observations with dummy variables of 1 are calculated
as value weighted averages of the two companies created in the spin-off in proportion to
their size when separated (see Table 2).
             For our second hypothesis, where we investigate the abnormal returns over
specified time periods in our data set, we run two-sided t-tests to find the power of our
results. Again, we run two-sided t-tests due to the intrinsic uncertainty of results regarding
whether they might be positively or negatively deviating from zero.

3.2.3. Abnormal returns

Model (MacKinley 1997) in order to calculate the expected returns.
             The Market Adjusted Model can be considered as more restrained model in
comparison to other types of models since certain coefficients are prespecified. Due to
the use of a more restricted model, it is important to contemplate over the risk of biases.
For example, the return of a certain spin-off on a particular day may affect the market
index and hence generate deflated abnormal return results. Furthermore, the model does
not take CAPM and systematic risks into account. We do not consider real and nominal
price differences due to the short time periods.

5. Implications and conclusions

Our findings are in accordance with some current studies. Just like our study, Hite and
Owers (1983) found a positive average abnormal return until the announcement date and
the following period after announcement displayed negative returns. Moreover, Johnson
et al. (1994) also found positive abnormal returns before announcement and a negative
cumulative abnormal return after announcement. The main differences between our study
and theirs is the sample size, method used, geographical area and time investigated. Even
though our studies differ in these elements, the results are very similar. This gives an
indication that the international research such as Johnson et. al, (1994) and Hite and
Owers (1983) can be applied to the Swedish market.
             Many researchers suggest that the spun-off entity tend to decline
immediately after the announcement (Johnson et al. 1994, Hite and Owers, 1983).
However, the spun-off entities exhibit positive abnormal returns in the following years
after announcement. Our results display similar characteristics – an immediate decline
after announcement. Even though we do not statistically prove the direct downturn after
announcement, we would like to speculate that one reason to the immediate decline is
attributed to the way spin-off shares are being distributed.
              The shares in the spun-off entity are distributed pro rata to shareholders in
the parent. The parent shareholders are more likely to be more interested in the parent
rather than the spun-off entity why some shares in the spun-off entity are immediately
sold. For example, institutional investors with a market capitalization limitation policy
might be forced to sell the spin-off since they are not allowed to hold a company with a
too low market capitalization. For further studies, investigating the sellers of shares a few
days after announcement would be appropriate to draw a fully motivated conclusion.
              The abnormal returns yielded from spin-offs have previously been studied
through the perspective of information asymmetry. Krishnaswambi and Subrnamiam
(1999) found that spin-offs reduce information asymmetry in general and that spin-offs
that decreases information asymmetry more than other spin-offs achieve higher abnormal
returns. If investors interpret spin-offs as an approach to reduce the information
asymmetry and if transparency is considered positive, then the investors might value the
firm higher than previously.

                                  30
             Additionally, the reduction of information asymmetry can be interpreted
with signaling theory. The management, agent, holds more information than the investors,
principals, regarding the true value of the firm. By conducting a spin-off, the management
can credibly send a signal of increased transparency and make the value of the underlying
assets more visible for the investors. Hence, investors could improve the precision of
valuation of the firm. However, measuring the level of information asymmetry
explanatory power on the level of abnormal returns was outside this study’s focus.
            Although, if we were to speculate we would assume that the transparency
on equity markets has probably increased since the study by Krishnaswambi and
Subrnamiam (1999) was conducted. This could indicate that the level of information
asymmetry is relatively less successful in explaining the level of abnormal returns.
Another remark is that the study was carried out in the US. Sweden is in general
considered as a relatively transparent country which could also decrease the explanatory
power of information asymmetry effect on the level of abnormal returns in Sweden in
comparison to the study by Krishnaswambi and Subrnamiam (1999).
             Signaling and underperformance as an explanatory factor to the abnormal
returns can also be linked. If a company underperforms for a long period of time, in the
sense of yielding lower returns than industry average for a number of years, investors
would have an incentive to put pressure on the management to deliver better results. A
restructuring would probably send positive signals to the investors. The spin-off signals
a change in the firm and possibly also a confirmation from the management that they
consider the past share price development as disappointing and unacceptable.
            However, we have not found any highly regarded previous study with the
factor long-term underperformance explaining the size of abnormal returns. This would
pose an interesting research question for further studies. We reason that investors demand
actions that generate a turnaround for companies in decline. A spin-off could send a
credible signal of change and therefore exhibit higher abnormal returns in comparison to
firms that are not underperforming.
             In the prevalent literature corporate focus is considered as a factor
explaining the size of the abnormal returns. Daley, Mehrotra, and Sivakumar (1997)
suggest that cross-industry spin-offs generate higher abnormal returns than intra-industry
spin-offs. Our sample consisted of 11 cross-industry spin-offs (see appendix Table 8),

                                  31
which we consider as insufficient in order to run a regression and compare the outcomes
between intra- and cross-industry spin-offs. In the Swedish study written by Brink and
Falk (2009) they investigated spin-offs between 1991-2008 in Sweden. The authors
conclude that cross-industry spin-offs do not give any explanatory power on abnormal
returns. However, as our samples are different the results could show a different outcome.
             A relevant question is whether it is the motives that affect the level of
abnormal returns or if it is the restructuring per se that can explain the abnormal returns.
A corporate restructuring create publicity and the attention in itself could be the
explanation for abnormal returns in the short run assuming that publicity and stock prices
are correlated. In the long run, spin-offs might increase the motivation of employees. If
the organization becomes smaller the employees have a greater chance of climbing in the
corporate hierarchy and therefore incentivized to work harder. The management in
smaller organizations also have greater possibility to effect the financial results and they
will be incentivized make an effort to report positive results due to stock options.
              To summarize there is an underlying consensus that spin-offs at least in
some way generate value in the long term, whether it be owing to information asymmetry,
corporate focus or any other factor. If investors and management of firms do not believe
spin-offs yield value why would companies engage in the voluntary restructuring in the
first place?
            However, when viewing our results, we find data that support quite the
opposite. We have proven significantly lower abnormal return 100 trading days after the
spin-off compared to the 100 days before the announcement. Albeit 100 trading days is a
short time frame relative to years ahead, we find our results interesting. We still, although,
note that abnormal returns are significantly larger than zero over the whole 200 trading
days’ period.
              We want to speculate there is an underlying investor belief that spin-offs
create shareholder value in the long run. Since abnormal returns post spin-off
announcement has been proven in the past, it is only natural for investors to want to buy
shares before everyone else does and thus not miss the opportunity of achieving abnormal
returns. Consequently, the abnormal returns are shifted in time to before the spin-off
actually is completed. This idea can be linked with the efficient market hypothesis.

                                  32
              According to the efficient market hypothesis prices shall at any point in time
reflect all available information to investors. If investors are aware of previous studies
conclusion that spin-offs generate abnormal returns and their own investor experience is
the same as research argues then investors might incorporate their anticipation in the price
before the realization of the transaction according to the efficient market hypothesis.
             This might be a good explanation to our findings; stocks overperform the
index in anticipation and once the spin-off is actually undertaken, much of the initial
abnormal returns, connected to a spin-off restructuring, have already been realized.
             Further research on long term abnormal performance would be interesting
in order to follow up this study. We cannot speculate on how the outcome would be
several months or years ahead. International studies have reported a positive cumulative
abnormal return over a three-year period after the announcement. It would be interesting
to test this thesis with a Swedish sample with spin-offs conducted post the financial crisis.
Many of the spin-offs in our sample were conducted recently, with five spin-offs in 2018.
Therefore, studying long run abnormal returns with an event window of one to three years
would have been unattainable today but possible in a few years from now.
             Additionally, several companies on the Swedish stock market, such as
Sandvik and Electrolux, have declared that they will conduct spin-offs in the near future,
while MTG completed a spin-off during the completion of this paper. More firms will
probably engage in spin-offs in the coming years given the recent trend. These firms
would add to the sample of spin-offs in Sweden since the financial crisis. The larger
sample could be used to test factors that with significance explain the level of abnormal
returns. Factors of interest for further research would be information asymmetry,

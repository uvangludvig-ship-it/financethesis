Guilty Profits: Does The Market Reward the Unethical?
Abstract:
This paper examines the performance of U.S. sin stocks – alcohol, tobacco and gambling – over
the period 1965-2023, focusing on risk-adjusted abnormal returns. Using time-series regression
across the CAPM, Fama-French Three-Factor model, Carhart Four-Factor model, and Fama-
French Five-Factor model we find that sin stocks generate significant abnormal returns, with a
monthly alpha of 42 basis points under the Fama-French Five-Factor model. Industry-level
analysis finds that while all sin industries exhibit positive abnormal returns over the full period,
only alcohol stocks maintain significance during 2007-2023, a time marked by heightened ESG
adoption. However, findings from a placebo test suggest that the sin stock classification and its
associated premium is influenced by random noise. This challenges the robustness of the sin
premium and its attribution to systematic factors or social norms.

   Sin stocks, Abnormal returns, Exclusionary screening

1. Introduction
One of the most widely adopted and growing sustainable investing practices is exclusionary
screening (GSIA 2018, 2022). Exclusionary screening involves the exclusion of certain assets
from the universe of investments, usually the most socially controversial assets, such as alcohol,
tobacco and gambling who collectively are known as “sin stocks”. Merton (1987) proposed that
neglected stocks carry a premium as compensation for reduced liquidity, higher transaction costs
and limited risk sharing. This concept suggests that when stocks, such as sin stocks, are excluded
from portfolios they may deliver higher expected returns, resulting from a smaller investor base.
Empirical studies on sin stock performance support this notion, suggesting that sin stocks
experience higher returns than the market or their comparable stocks (Salaber 2007, 2008;
Fabozzi, Ma and Oliphant 2008; Hong and Kacperczyk 2009; Statman and Glushkov 2009;
Liston and Soydemir 2010). These studies found that sin stocks exhibit positive significant
alphas in the U.S., European and global markets under the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM),
the Fama-French Three-Factor model (FF3) and the Carhart Four-Factor model (Carhart). The
mechanism driving the higher expected returns of sin stocks has been attributed to neglect by
norm-constrained investors and heightened litigation risk amplified by social norms (Hong and
Kacperczyk 2009). Similar to Merton (1987), Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) suggest that the
avoidance of sin stocks by an important set of investors, particularly institutional investors, leads
to depressed stock prices relative to their fundamental values due to limited risk sharing.
Consequently, sin stocks should yield higher expected returns as compensation. However, the
literature presents conflicting findings regarding the persistence of the “sin premium”. While
earlier studies have demonstrated that sin stocks generate positive abnormal returns, more recent
studies have shown that this overperformance can be explained by the Fama and French (2015)
quality factors profitability and investment (Richey 2017; Blitz and Fabozzi 2017; Sagbakken
and Zhang 2022). These studies find that under the Fama-French Five-Factor model (FF5) the
significant positive alpha for sin stocks disappears. However, Zerbib (2022) shows that
exclusionary screening significantly affects asset returns, and the impact of sustainable investors
on a company’s cost of capital can be substantial in many cases.
In this paper, we investigate whether U.S. sin stocks in the alcohol, tobacco and gambling
industries, known as the “triumvirate of sin”, deliver positive abnormal returns from 1965 to
2023 on a risk-adjusted basis. The most cited work on sin stocks up to date by Hong and
Kacperczyk (2009) covers the period 1965-2006. We continue where their study ends and
provide a time segmented analysis of the period 2007-2023. The trend of sustainable investing
during this period, with exclusionary screening as a widely adopted strategy, motivates an up-to-
date examination of sin stock returns. Investigating this will contribute further knowledge of the
tradeoff between sustainable investment practices and financial gains. Moreover, we identify
inconsistencies in methodologies across studies that present opposing conclusions about sin
stock returns. By synthesizing elements from different studies and employing a comprehensive
methodological approach we aim to address these inconsistencies and provide a robust
assessment of the returns of sin stocks.
We test our predictions by performing time-series regression on the monthly returns of sin stocks
for the CAPM, FF3, Carhart and FF5 models. First, we find that a value weighted portfolio
comprised of the monthly returns of U.S. alcohol, tobacco and gambling stocks, net of the risk-
free rate, show abnormal returns over 1965-2023. This finding is robust across all models and

                                       3
contradicts others who find the abnormal returns of sin stocks to disappear when accounting for
the Fama-French quality factors profitability and investment (Richey 2017; Blitz and Fabozzi
2017; Sagbakken and Zhang 2022). Our results show a positive alpha of 42 basis points (bps)
significant at the 1% level. Second, we run time-series regressions for the time-periods 1965-
1985, 1986-2006 and 2007-2023, all else equal. We find that in the period 2007-2023, alpha
remains positive and significant. For the FF5 model the alpha is estimated to 47 bps, significant
at the 5% level. Third, we segment our portfolio by industry, all else equal, and find that each of
the sin industries generate positive and significant alphas over the period 1965-2023 across all
factor models. However, over the period 2007-2023 we find that only the alcohol portfolio
delivers a significant alpha under the FF5 model. The tobacco and gambling portfolios’ alpha
remain positive but insignificant in the FF5 model. Finally, we change the setting of the
dependent variable, consistent with Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) we find that an equal weighted
portfolio long on sin stocks and short their comparable stocks have a positive significant alpha
over the period 1965-2023. This finding is robust in the FF5 model. The long-short portfolios’
alpha is estimated to 21 bps, significant at the 10% level. However, the results from a placebo
test suggest that the observed outperformance of sin stocks relative to their comparables may be
driven by random noise, raising questions about the robustness of these findings.
Our findings come with several implications. They oppose the findings of studies who find that
the abnormal returns of sin stocks disappear when accounting for the Fama-French quality
factors profitability and investment (Richey 2017; Blitz and Fabozzi 2017; Sagbakken and Zhang
2022). Moreover, our results show that there may exist a tradeoff between Environmental, Social
and Governance (ESG) goals and financial returns as we show that sin stocks generate abnormal
returns of 47 bps monthly or 5.64% annually between 2007-2023. This could potentially be
explained by the fact that exclusionary screening significantly affects asset prices (Zerbib 2022).
However, our study cannot tell whether the observed abnormal returns are due to the neglect
effect (Merton 1987), or systematic exclusion of these stocks driven by social norms (Hong and
Kacperczyk 2009). In addition, our results are limited to the U.S. and traditional sin stocks,
specifically the triumvirate of sin.
Our paper contributes to the body of research on sustainable investing practices and their impact
on asset pricing. Within that literature, our work is most closely related to studies examining the
returns of excluded assets, or sin stocks. There are several papers covering the topic of sin stock
returns, but the closest papers to our research are Hong and Kacperczyk (2009), Richey (2017),
Blitz and Fabozzi (2017) and Sagbakken and Zhang (2022). Each examines the returns of sin
stocks but with notable methodological differences. Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) studied U.S.
sin stocks in the alcohol, tobacco and gambling industries (the triumvirate of sin) over 1965-
2006. The authors employ an equal weighted approach and use the monthly returns of sin stocks
minus the monthly returns of comparable stocks as the dependent variable in their regressions.
Their analysis includes the CAPM, the Fama-French three-factor model and the Carhart four-
factor model. In contrast, Richey (2017) extends the definition of U.S. sin stocks beyond the
triumvirate of sin to include payday lenders, adult entertainment and defense stocks. The studied
period is 1986-2016 and the Fama-French five-factor model is included in addition to the models
applied by Hong and Kacperczyk (2009). Richey (2017) adopts a price weighted approach with
daily sin stock returns less the risk-free rate as the dependent variable. Blitz and Fabozzi (2017)
examine sin stocks in the U.S., Europe and Japan. In their U.S sample the authors include
alcohol, tobacco and defense stocks over the period 1963-2016. They employ a value-weighted
approach and use the monthly sin stock returns over the monthly market return as the dependent

                                       4
variable. Like Richey (2017) the authors include the FF5 model. Sagbakken and Zhang (2022)
focus on the European market from 2007 to 2020, studying both “traditional” sin stocks, alcohol,
tobacco, gambling and defense, but also “new” sin stocks, identified as stocks in the coal, oil and
gas, metals and mining and uranium industries. They use a value weighted approach and use the
monthly returns of sin stocks less the risk-free rate as the dependent variable. Consistent with
Richey (2017) and Blitz and Fabozzi (2017) they apply the Fama-French five-factor model in
their analysis. In all three studies they conclude that the significant positive alpha for sin stocks
become insignificant under the FF5 model, meaning that sin stocks do not generate positive
abnormal returns when the profitability and investment factors are included. The methodological
differences between the study of Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) and the more recent make direct
comparisons challenging. There are disparities in weighting, dependent variables and sin stock
sample. Our thesis addresses the methodological differences, and we employ the equal weighted
long-short portfolio approach as Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) and a value weighted excess sin
portfolio. This enables us to assess whether the abnormal return of sin stocks persists under the
FF5 model for different portfolio constructions and make direct comparison with Hong and
Kacperczyk (2009). We find that, contrary to the conclusions of Richey (2017), Blitz and
Fabozzi (2017) and Sagbakken Zhang (2022) that the abnormal returns of sin stocks do not
disappear under the FF5 model over the extended period. This holds true for both the equal
weighted long-short portfolio and the value weighted excess sin portfolio. Furthermore, to our
knowledge, no prior study has applied a placebo test in this context. Our results challenge the
validity of interpreting the sin premium as conclusive evidence of outperformance and highlight
the need for further investigation into the methodological challenges of isolating sin stocks'
performance. Therefore, a key contribution to the literature from this paper is the placebo test.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 presents the background on and
selection of our sample, the data and portfolio constructions, the variables in the time-series
regression and the employed factor models with their respective equations. Section 3 presents the
empirical results and discussion. Section 4 shows robustness tests and section 5 concludes.

2. Data and Methodology
In an ideal scenario, we would employ an empirical approach that would establish a causal
relationship between the sin stock classification and abnormal returns. This would require a
randomized experimental setting that firms are randomly assigned to sin or non-sin categories.
Such a design would isolate the effect of the sin stock classification on returns and eliminate the
influence of confounding factors. However, this lacks feasibility as firms independently select
into the industries they operate in. This non-random assignment introduces a selection bias,
meaning that sin stocks may possess observed characteristics influencing their returns
independent of their classification of sin stocks. To maintain comparability with previous studies,
we adopt the time-series regression approach to investigate the abnormal returns of sin stocks.
This approach, while rigorous, has limitations compared to the ideal setting. One concern is
potential endogeneity, as our time-series regressions do not control for firm-level characteristics
that may confound the relationship between the sin classification and returns. For example,
cross-sectional regression methods could address omitted variables by accounting for individual
stock attributes. We test different model specifications to see if our results hold and perform tests
in the robustness section to further evaluate our setting.

2.1. Background on and selection of sin stocks
This section provides an overview of the rationale and methodology for selecting sin stocks for
this study. It is divided into two parts: a discussion of the background on sin stock selection and
the process used to construct the sin stock sample.

2.1.1. Background on Sin Stock Selection
The focus on the Triumvirate of sin (alcohol, tobacco and gambling) has been carefully
considered for this study for several reasons. First, these industries are widely regarded as sinful
due to their addictive properties and the negative social consequences of excess use, a perception
reinforced by public health concerns and regulatory frameworks in countries like the
U.S. (Salaber 2007; Hong and Kacperczyk 2009). Second, these industries are consistently
examined in the sin stock literature, enabling meaningful comparisons with previous research.
Third, firms in these industries are always excluded by Socially Responsible Investing (SRI)
funds, further supporting their classification as the main sin stocks (Zerbib, 2022). Other studies
have expanded the classification of sin stocks to include industries like adult entertainment and
defense (Richey 2017; Blitz and Fabozzi 2017). We exclude adult entertainment due to the lack
of stock return data availability, as most firms in this industry are privately held. Zerbib (2022)
follows Hong and Kacerpczyk (2009) in excluding the defense industry because not all US
investors regard it as unethical. While perceived as unethical by some, it also exhibits
fundamentally different characteristics from the triumvirate of sin. This is because the defense
industry is heavily affected by unpredictable geopolitical events (Baker, Bloom and Davis 2016).
In contrast, alcohol, tobacco, and gambling are primarily influenced by consumer demand,
regulatory interventions and litigation risk due to being consumer products (Hong and
Kacperzcyk 2009). Due to these fundamental differences, we exclude the defense sector from
our analysis. While stocks with high environmental footprints, or "brown stocks", are
increasingly excluded by investors, they are not considered here. Brown stocks are not
consistently defined as sin stocks in established literature, and their diversity across multiple
industries makes it challenging to form a comparable set of firms. Therefore, they fall outside the
scope of this study.

2.1.2. Construction of Sin Stock Sample
Based on the triumvirate of sin, we identify sin stocks from the universe of stocks in alignment
with Hong and Kacperczyk (2009). We begin by classifying sin stocks based on the Fama-
French (1997) industry classification, which sorts stocks into 48 industries according to SIC
codes. Sin stocks in our study are drawn from industry group 4 (beer or alcohol) and industry
group 5 (smoke or tobacco). Specifically, stocks with SIC codes 2080-2085 are categorized as
alcohol related, while those with SIC codes 2100-2199 are tobacco related. However, the Fama-
French classification does not distinguish gambling stocks from Fama-French (1997) industries
Meals and Hotels and Fun. To address this, we supplement our classification with North
American Industry Classification (NAICS) codes to identify gambling stocks, which include the
following: 7132, 71312, 713210, 71329, 713290, 72112, and 721120.

Our final definition of sin stocks, therefore, includes the Fama-French industry groups 4 and 5,
along with the specified NAICS gambling codes. To further refine this list, we extend our search
to the business segment level. Using Compustat Segments data which offers operational
segmentation data for over 23,000 North American companies from 1976 to 2024, we capture
additional information on SIC and NAICS codes for different business segments within each
company. Compustat Segments offer several SIC variables (Standard Industry Classification
Code, SIC Code (Primary), SIC Code (Secondary)) as well as several NAICS variables (North
American Industry Classification Code, Primary Historical NAICS, NAICS Code (Primary),
NAICS Code (Secondary)). To find companies that have diversified operations across multiple
industries, we use all of these variables to screen for companies involved in our sin stock
definition. This is crucial to accurately find sin stocks as many of them are diversified and have
multiple businesses. A company is initially classified as a sin stock if any of its business
segments falls under the specified SIC codes for alcohol, tobacco or the NAICS codes for
gambling. Within this list on Compustat Segments, we identify 384 unique company names,
accompanied by 384 unique CUSIP codes, over the period 1976 to 2024. The next step in the
process is to search for these companies in The Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP)
database, which contains a comprehensive collection of security price, return, and volume data
for the NYSE, AMEX and NASDAQ stock markets from 1925 to 2023. Since Compustat data is
only available from 1976, we backfill the list in CRSP from 1976 to 1965, meaning that a
company once considered sin, is considered sin throughout its history. The best alternative to
transfer the companies from Compustat to CRSP is to use company specific CUSIP codes, but,
since CUSIPs differ between Compustat and CRSP for some companies, we complement the
CUSIPs with manual search for each Company Name in CRSP. In total, we find 283 unique
securities in CRSP, denoted by the PERMNO variable. PERMNO allows tracking of a security
through its entire trading history. Next, we manually screen each stock to confirm whether the
company has or has had operations in any of the sin industries and then we label them
accordingly. A security that we are not able to label as sin is excluded. Through this manual
screening process, we filter out 45 securities and classify the remaining 238 as sin, using their
PERMNOs. Next, we filter the dataset to only include PERMNOs with a Share Code of 10 or 11
which indicates that the security is an ordinary common share incorporated in the U.S. Excluded
share codes are for example certificates, ADRs, SBIs and Units. After this filter, we are left with
188 manually classified SIN stocks that are or have been trading on NYSE, NASDAQ or
AMEX. This is our finale sin stock sample.
We have also performed cross-checks with previous research, mainly Hong and Kacperczyk
(2009). Considered together, our screening procedure yields an accurate and comprehensive list
of stocks in alcohol, tobacco and gambling industries.

2.2. Construction and Selection of Comparable Stocks Sample
In accordance with Hong and Kacperczyk (2009) we identify comparable non-sin stocks as those
belonging to the Fama and French (1997) industry groups 2 (food), 3 (Soda), 7 (fun) and 43
(meals and hotels). The authors explain that in some classification schemes, beer is often lumped
together with soda, as is tobacco with food and gaming with fun and meals. One could argue for
more specific comparable definitions, but we follow the method of Hong and Kacperczyk (2009)
for comparability and find our sin sample to exhibit a very high correlation with the comparable
stocks. From the comparable stocks industry groups, we obtain SIC codes used to screen CRSP
for our comparable stocks. As we are only interested in stocks that belong to these industry
groups, it is not necessary to screen on the industry level. This search gives us 1640 unique
stocks. Next, we filter on Share Codes 10 and 11 as with the sin dataset, which gives us 1501
unique stocks. Next, we filter the comparable dataset to exclude all companies that are present in
the sin dataset by PERMNO. This is important since gambling stocks are not distinguished from
the industry groups Meals and Hotels and Fun. We now have 1413 stocks. Next, we filter on our
SIC codes again to ensure that CRSP has not included any other industries, we now have 1392.
Lastly, we manually screen the companies and exclude any companies that belong to sin
industries. We find 28 sin stocks in the sample. They are all small companies, and they are not
present in the Compustat database, otherwise they would have been filtered out by PERMNO.
These companies are not included in our sin stock sample as they were not identified through our
sin stock screening in Compustat. We are left with a dataset of 1364 comparable non-sin stocks
that are or have been trading on NYSE, NASDAQ or AMEX. This is our final comparable stock
sample.

2.3. Data and portfolio construction
We use monthly return data from CRSP to run time-series regressions across several factor
models. We obtain monthly returns, closing prices and shares outstanding for NYSE, Amex and
Nasdaq stocks over the period 1965-01 to 2023-12. The data used in the asset pricing models,
including the risk-free rate, the value-weighted market premium and Fama and French factors
size, value, profitability, investment and the Carhart factor momentum are obtained from
Kenneth French’s data library. The monthly return data over the sample period yields a sample
size of 35 684 observations for the total portfolio of sin stocks and 153 352 observations for the
total portfolio of comparable stocks. Only missing return data in 717 observations (2%) for our
sin portfolio and 4 136 observations (2.7%) for our comparable portfolio. Missing values are
filtered out. Survivorship bias is not an issue as both Compustat and CRSP include historical
companies, independent of their current trading status. Therefore, our samples include active and
non-active stocks that have been delisted for reasons such as acquisitions, bankruptcy and change
of exchange.

2.4. Variables in time-series return regressions
This section introduces the variables used in the regressions to analyze the abnormal returns of
sin stocks and their relationship with established risk factors.

2.4.1. Dependent Variables
Our study examines the abnormal returns of sin stocks through the construction of various
dependent variables. For the value-weighted analysis, we create the following portfolios: SIN,
representing the value-weighted monthly excess return of all sin stocks; ALCOHOL, for the
alcohol portfolio; TOBACCO, for the tobacco portfolio; and GAMBLING, for the gambling
portfolio. The focus on value-weighted portfolios ensures consistency with the market portfolio
(MKTPREM), aligns with recent literature, and reflects realistic capital allocation and liquidity
preferences in developed markets, where large firms dominate.
For the equal-weighted analysis, we construct SINEW-COMPSEW, an equal-weighted long-
short portfolio that is long on our sin stocks (SINEW) and short on our comparable non-sin
stocks (COMPSEW). This approach isolates the "sin premium" following Hong and Kacperczyk
(2009). Equal weighting is employed to allow direct comparability with their study and assess
whether the significant alpha of the long-short portfolio persists under the FF5 model.

2.4.2. Independent Variables
The data for the independent variables is obtained from Kenneth French’s data library which
provides value-weighted average monthly returns throughout the entire study period.
MKTPREM is the excess monthly return of the value-weighted CRSP index. SMB is the return
of a portfolio that is long on small stocks and short on large stocks. HML is the return of a
portfolio that is long on high book-to-market stocks and short on low book-to-market stocks.
MOM is the return of a portfolio that is long on past 12-month return winners and short on past
12-month return losers. RMW is the return of a portfolio that is long on stocks with robust
operating profitability and short on stocks with weak operating profitability. CMA is the return
of a portfolio that is long on stocks with conservative investment strategies and short on stocks
with aggressive investment strategies. The risk-free rate (RF) is represented by the one-month
Treasury bill rate.

2.5. Asset Pricing Models
We employ several asset pricing models to study the return performance of our sin portfolios. In
this section, we briefly explain the theory and specify the models for dependent variables.

2.5.1. Capital Asset Pricing Model
We begin with the Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM) with Jensen’s alpha as a measure of
abnormal performance. Developed by Jensen (1968), Jensen’s alpha quantifies the extent to
which a portfolio’s realized returns deviate from its expected returns based on systematic market

                                      10
risk, as captured by the Sharpe-Lintner CAPM (Sharpe 1964; Lintner 1965). The specification is
as follows:

                                𝑆𝐼𝑁𝑡 = 𝛼𝑝 + 𝛽𝑝(𝑀𝐾𝑇𝑃𝑅𝐸𝑀𝑡) + 𝜖𝑡

In this model, SIN_t is the monthly excess return of the SIN portfolio, which can be replaced by
ALCOHOL, TOBACCO, GAMBLING, or SINEW-COMPSEW. The coefficient beta_p reflects
sensitivity to market risk (MKTPREM_t), while epsilon_t is a random error term. Jensen’s alpha
(alpha_p) measures the portfolio’s abnormal return, with positive values indicating
outperformance and negative values indicating underperformance.

2.5.2. Fama French Three-Factor Model
While the CAPM is fundamental in finance, it faces criticism for its inability to explain return
anomalies beyond systematic market risk. Fama and French (1992, 1993) addressed these
limitations by introducing the Three-Factor Model, which extends the CAPM by incorporating
two additional factors: Small minus Big (SMB), capturing the size effect, and High minus Low
(HML), capturing the value effect. These factors enhance the explanatory power of CAPM in
evaluating stock performance. We extend the CAPM specification to the three-factor model
regression as follows:

                  𝑆𝐼𝑁𝑡 = 𝛼𝑝 + 𝛽𝑝(𝑀𝐾𝑇𝑃𝑅𝐸𝑀𝑡) + 𝛾𝑝 (𝑆𝑀𝐵𝑡) + 𝛿𝑝(𝐻𝑀𝐿𝑡) + 𝜖𝑡

Here, SMB_t and HML_t are the size and value factors, respectively, while gamma_p and
delta_p measure the portfolio's sensitivity to these risks. All other variables, including
MKTPREM_t, epsilon_t, alpha_p, and beta_p, remain the same as in the CAPM. This model
builds on CAPM by accounting for firm-specific characteristics, offering a more comprehensive
framework for explaining stock returns.

2.5.3. Carhart Four-Factor Model
Carhart (1997) extends the Fama-French Three-Factor Model by introducing a momentum factor
(MOM), which captures the tendency of stocks with strong past performance (winners) to
continue outperforming and stocks with poor past performance (losers) to underperform. This
strategy is based on prior 3–12 month returns. The Carhart model is specified as:

Here, MOM_t represents the momentum factor, and mu_p is the portfolio’s sensitivity to this
factor. All other variables and coefficients are as defined in the Fama-French Three-Factor
Model.

2.5.4. Fama-French Five-Factor Model
Fama and French (2015) address limitations in the Three-Factor Model, particularly its inability
to explain return variations related to profitability and investment. They expand the model by
incorporating two additional factors: Robust minus Weak (RMW), which reflects the return
difference between portfolios of firms with high (robust) and low (weak) profitability, and
Conservative minus Aggressive (CMA), which captures the return difference between firms with
low (conservative) and high (aggressive) investment levels (asset growth). These factors enhance
the model's explanatory power, capturing dimensions of stock performance that earlier models
could not. The model is specified as:

Here, RMW_t represents the profitability factor, and CMA_t represents the investment factor.
The coefficients rho_p and tau_p indicate the portfolio's sensitivity to these respective factors.
All other variables are as defined in the Fama-French Three-Factor Model.

significant for the FF5 model suggesting that not all drivers of the sin premium are captured by
the models.

addition, the findings of Zerbib (2022) in that exclusionary screening has a significant impact on

statistically significant positive abnormal returns of the SIN portfolio across the period 1965-

robustness of our results, and test if the observed alpha is unique to the sin stock classification.

3.4.1. Placebo Test
To investigate whether the observed alpha is inherently tied to the sin stock classification or
driven by random noise, we perform a placebo test. This test involves randomly assigning sin
status to 188 comparable stocks (COMPS) and constructing 500 randomized long-short
portfolios. By rerunning the regression for these placebo portfolios, we generate a distribution of
placebo alphas to compare with the observed alpha.
The null hypothesis assumes no systematic return differences between SIN and COMPS, while
the alternative hypothesis posits a return difference. If the observed alpha falls within the center
of the placebo distribution, we cannot reject the null hypothesis, suggesting that the return
premium is due to random noise rather than an inherent sin premium.

5. Conclusion
In this study, we show that the Fama-French five-factor model, including the profitability
(RMW) and investment (CMA) factors, does not necessarily make the alpha of sin stocks
disappear over the period 1965-2023. Our results indicate that sin stocks in the alcohol, tobacco
and gambling industries generate positive abnormal returns, even after accounting for established
risk factors. This suggests that the factors driving the sin premium are not fully captured by the
FF5 model. Additional elements, such as systematic neglect or exclusion based on social norms
may play a role in sustaining this premium.
Over the period 2007-2023, characterized by increased exclusionary screening and market
efficiency, sin stocks continued to deliver positive risk adjusted abnormal returns. We find that
the excess sin portfolio generates monthly positive abnormal returns of 47 bps, or 5.64%
annually over the period 2007-2023. This has economic significance and points toward a trade-
off between sustainable investing strategies and financial gains. Investors excluding sin stocks
may forgo potential abnormal returns. Sin stocks may continue to be underpriced due to limited
risk sharing stemming from social norms, leading to higher expected returns as compensation.
During this time, our results show that only the alcohol industry exhibits a positive significant
alpha under the FF5 model.
However, we cannot conclusively say that over the period 1965-2023, sin stocks outperform
their comparable stocks due to issues of random noise identified in the placebo test. This finding
shows that the sin stock classification and its associated premium may be inherently incorrect.
Future research could further explore the robustness of the sin classification to validate the
implications more conclusively.

SWEDISH IPO PROSPECTUSES

Abstract:
This paper studies whether the EU Prospectus Regulation (EU) 2017/1129 changed
the way Swedish IPO prospectuses are written, and whether these changes affect
first-day underpricing. We analyze 269 Swedish IPOs between 2006 and 2026 and
use the textual decomposition method from Hanley and Hoberg (2010) to separate
the prospectus language into standard and informative content. The results indi-
cate that the Regulation did not mechanically increase firm-specific disclosure once
firm characteristics, year effects, and industry effects are accounted for. We also
find no clear unconditional relationship between prospectus content and first-day
underpricing in the Swedish setting. However, joint Wald F -tests of the content ×
regulation interaction terms indicate that the Regulation changed how the market
prices disclosure – particularly for the Total Document (F = 5.319, p < 0.001) and
the Risk Factors section (F = 3.621, p < 0.01) – suggesting that the Regulation
affects pricing not only by changing what issuers disclose, but also by altering how
investors interpret disclosure.

Keywords: IPO Underpricing, Prospectus Disclosure, EU Prospectus Regulation,
Information Content, Swedish IPO Market

1. Introduction

An IPO prospectus – the written disclosure document in which a firm describes
its business, risks, and financial position before going public – is meant to reduce
information asymmetry before the offer price is set. But the effect of regulation
on this disclosure is not obvious. A new rule can make issuers provide more firm-
specific information, or it can standardize the format in which existing information
is presented. This paper studies this distinction using textual analysis of Swedish
IPO prospectuses around the EU Prospectus Regulation (“Regulation”). We find
that the Regulation does not robustly change the amount of informative content in
IPO prospectuses, but it changes how the market prices standard and informative
disclosure.
   The textual analysis of our paper is based on Hanley and Hoberg (2010). They
decompose prospectus text into standard, boilerplate content and informative, firm-
specific content, finding that greater informative content produces more accurate
offer prices and roughly 8% lower underpricing. We replicate and extend their
methodology to the Swedish market to investigate how a different institutional set-
ting, as well as a changing regulatory environment, affects price. The theoretical
motivation for the link between prospectus content and price comes from Benveniste
and Spindt (1989), who say that underpricing is the price an issuer pays to extract
private information from investors during bookbuilding, so the more an issuer de-
pends on bookbuilding for price discovery, the more money it must leave on the
table. Prospectus quality directly mediates that dependence: when an issuer invests
more time in pre-market due diligence and discloses more upfront, investors arrive
at the bookbuilding already better informed, narrowing the information gap that
underpricing must bridge.
   Sweden presents an interesting setting for studying these questions. The country
has for decades been a hub for entrepreneurial activity and company-building, and
in terms of IPO activity, Sweden stands out as one of the most active markets in
Europe – during 2025, Nasdaq Stockholm accounted for the highest share of global
IPO transaction value among all European exchanges (Dalhäll, 2025). Sweden’s
combination of an active IPO market, a rather concentrated investment base, and
smaller firm size may give rise to disclosure patterns that differ from those observed
in larger markets such as the United States (US) (OECD, 2026). In July 2017,
the EU Prospectus Regulation (EU) 2017/1129 (“Regulation”) entered into force,
stipulating in detail when and how a prospectus should be written, while also in-
troducing the EU Growth Prospectus to reduce the regulatory burden for smaller
issuers. For Sweden, as for all EU member states, this meant that IPOs completed
after 21 July 2019, the full application date of the Regulation, had to comply with
the new regulations. Whether prospectuses of firms operating under this regulatory
framework look any different, and if this plays a role on the first-day prices, is the
open empirical question throughout the paper.
   The timing of this study is particularly relevant. As for global markets, IPO ac-
tivity in Sweden declined sharply following the pandemic boom in 2021 and shrank
even further during 2022 and 2023 (White & Case, 2024). The Swedish IPO market
has begun to recover from these low levels, not fully reaching pre-pandemic levels,
and pricing stands as a central consideration for issuers re-entering the market (Nas-
daq, 2024). If a higher share of informative disclosure can lead to more accurate

pricing, the question of how to structure a prospectus can have an impact finan-
cially. This matters even more, given that producing prospectus content is costly –
underwriter fees, which cover the drafting, researching, and verifying of prospectus
content, represent the highest direct cost of going public, typically around 3–4% of
proceeds in European markets (Abrahamson, Jenkinson, and Jones, 2011). Knowing
which elements of prospectus content contribute to more accurate pricing is a way
for issuers and their advisors to make better-informed decisions on where to allocate
the costly consulting hours.
    Along with the rise of IPOs, the rise of artificial intelligence (AI) is a motivation
for this paper. AI is used increasingly in the financial industry, and language models’
abilities to draft and compile reports are growing. As an example, the CEO of
Goldman Sachs, David Solomon, has noted that AI can accurately compile around
95% of an IPO prospectus, reducing what previously required a six-person team two
weeks of work to a matter of minutes (Financial Times, 2025). As AI tools take on
a growing role in prospectus production, the question of which type of disclosure
is associated with better pricing outcomes is relevant for how these tools should be
designed and used.
   Following Hanley and Hoberg (2010), we distinguish between the standard con-
tent of a prospectus, defined as language that mirrors recent and past industry IPOs,
and the informative content, defined as disclosure unique to the issuing firm that
is not explained by industry-wide patterns. Using this decomposition, we examine
whether greater informative content leads to more accurate initial offer prices and
lower underpricing, and conversely, whether greater standard content is associated
with larger offer price revision and higher initial returns. We apply this framework
to the Swedish market, using a sample set of 269 firms that are registered in Sweden
and did their IPO on a Swedish stock exchange between January 2006 and February
2026.
   We have structured the empirical analysis into two parts. First, we test if the
EU Prospectus Regulation changed the informational composition of prospectuses
(Prediction 1) by regressing standard and informative content scores – extracted for
the whole document and six individual sections per prospectus – on dummies for the
full Regulation and the EU Growth format. Second, we test if the different types of
prospectus content predict first-day underpricing (Prediction 2), and if this result
shifts under the new regulatory regime.
   The results indicate that the Regulation did not mechanically increase the amount
of firm-specific disclosure in Swedish IPO prospectuses. Once firm characteristics,
year effects, and industry effects are accounted for, the Regulation itself is not asso-
ciated with a significant change in either standard or informative content. Similarly,
we do not find a clear unconditional relationship between prospectus content and
first-day underpricing, meaning that the 8% reduction in underpricing documented
by Hanley and Hoberg (2010) does not directly appear in the Swedish setting. How-
ever, the results suggest that the Regulation changed how the market prices disclo-
sure. In the interaction regression, the joint Wald F -test rejects the null that the
EU regime does not moderate the content-pricing relationship, particularly for the
total document and risk factor sections. This implies that, while the Regulation did
not substantially change what issuers disclose, it altered the pricing relevance of a
given unit of disclosure. The strongest interpretation is therefore that the Regula-
tion reshapes the information environment indirectly: not by increasing volume or

uniqueness of disclosure, but by changing how investors interpret standard and in-
formative prospectus language. The EU Growth format shows a significant increase
in informative content, though this is the only finding that does not hold up in our
robustness checks and should be interpreted with caution. The remaining findings
are stable across a range of robustness checks, including alternative error structures,
PCA-based dimensionality reduction, and sub-period stability tests.

2. An Overview of the Context

This section provides the relevant context for the study, covering the related lit-
erature and the regulatory background that governs prospectus disclosure in the
Swedish IPO market.

2.1 Related Literature
The introduction of the Regulation in July 2019 enables a quasi-experimental design,
because the regulatory implementation itself can be treated as an exogenous shift
in the information environment rather than a choice made by individual issuers.
A well-identified example of how the literature exploits regulatory implementation
in this way is Chang, Ljungqvist, and Tseng (2023), who use the staggered rollout
of EDGAR to show its consequences on analyst behavior, finding that mandatory
disclosure constrains their strategic forecasting, making predictions less optimistic
and more accurate. We adopt a parallel approach, using the EU/ESMA regulatory
shift as an exogenous event to look at how it altered the informational composition
of prospectuses and the pricing of disclosure.
   When assessing IPO success, much of the literature focuses on first-day prices.
Carter and Manaster (1990) look at the underwriter reputation implication on first-
day prices; Megginson and Weiss (1991) look at the implication of venture backing on
first-day prices; Aggarwal, Prabhala, and Puri (2002) look at whether institutional
demand can serve as a proxy for first-day prices. Our study similarly uses first-day
price, comparing it to the price stated in the IPO prospectus, to measure pricing
accuracy – defined as the initial offer price relative to the price set by the market
on the first day of trading.
   To analyze the textual content of the IPO prospectuses, we follow Hanley and
Hoberg (2010), who ask whether issuers face a tradeoff between investing in pre-
                                                                               1
market due diligence and relying on bookbuilding to price their issue.  They de-
compose prospectus text into two components: standard content, defined as lan-
guage that mirrors prior IPOs within the same industry, and informative content,
defined as disclosure unique to the issuing firm that is not explained by industry-
wide patterns, using the latter as a proxy for pre-market effort expended. Their
central finding is that greater informative content leads to more accurate initial of-
fer prices, smaller price revisions between the initial filing and the final offer, and
approximately 8% lower underpricing. The opposite holds for standard content – a
higher share of boilerplate language is associated with larger upward price revisions
and greater first-day returns.

    Pre-market due diligence involves the production of information about the firm ahead of the
offering, which feeds into the prospectus content. Bookbuilding is the process by which bids from
investors are collected, typically by the underwriter, to determine the offer price.

   This tradeoff is grounded in Benveniste and Spindt (1989), who show that under-
pricing is the payment an issuer must make to extract private information investors
have about the value of the issuer during bookbuilding. The more an issuer de-
pends on bookbuilding for price discovery, the more it must leave on the table to
compensate investors for sharing their private information. Prospectus quality di-
rectly mediates this dependence: when an issuer discloses more upfront, investors
arrive at bookbuilding already better informed, narrowing the information gap that
underpricing must bridge. Cornelli and Goldreich (2001) provide evidence of this
mechanism, showing that bookbuilding allocations are used strategically to reward
investors who submit more informative bids and investors with repeated participa-
tion in the IPO market. It is in this setting that an informative prospectus can reduce
the dependence on costly investor information during bookbuilding, as Hanley and
Hoberg (2010) show by finding that standard content leads to higher underpricing.
In other words, relying on bookbuilding instead of pre-market due diligence leads to
higher underpricing.
   However, underpricing can also arise for reasons other than incentivizing in-
formation revelation during bookbuilding. An alternative explanation comes from
Rock (1986), who shows that when some investors are better informed than others,
uninformed investors face a winner’s curse: they are more likely to receive shares
in unattractive offerings and to be crowded out of attractive ones. Therefore, to
keep uninformed investors willing to participate, issuers must offer shares at a dis-
count. The relation between prospectus content and underpricing may depend on
the IPO pricing mechanism. If underpricing mainly compensates informed investors
for revealing information during bookbuilding, the informative content of a prospec-
tus should matter directly for pricing accuracy. If underpricing instead reflects a
discount to increase participation, the link between informative prospectus content
and first-day return may be weaker.
   The studies discussed above establish the theoretical and methodological foun-
dation of our analysis, but they are rooted primarily in the US market context.
Replicating Hanley and Hoberg (2010) in a Swedish setting can imply a different
empirical result, as market structure, investor base, and regulatory environment dif-
fer. Sweden is a small open economy with a highly developed equity market relative
to its size, with new listings taking place on the regulated markets Nasdaq Stock-
holm and Nordic Growth Market (NGM), as well as on multilateral trading (MTF),
including First North, Spotlight Stock Market, and NGM SME (SEB, 2026). Par-
ticipation in Swedish IPOs is dominated by a concentrated domestic investor base
– traditional institutional investors, including investment funds, pension funds, and
insurance companies, collectively owned more than half of all listed Swedish com-
panies at the end of 2024, with a small number of large repeat players, such as the
national pension funds and major family-owned investment firms, operating contin-
uously across the same industries and intermediaries (OECD, 2026). These features
make Sweden an interesting setting to test whether the content-pricing relationship
documented in the US setting holds in a setting where price discovery may rely less
on prospectus disclosure alone and more on the interactions and relations of issuers,
underwriters, and investors.
   Against this backdrop, this paper makes several contributions to the literature.
First, it offers a replication of Hanley and Hoberg’s (2010) textual methodology out-
side the US market, testing whether the distinction between standard and informa-

                                    4
Gummesson & Jäger What the Market Reads, 2026

tive prospectus content has the same pricing implications in a different institutional
setting. Second, we go a step further than the original framework by including the
regulatory environment and treating it as something to be studied rather than a
background condition – using the introduction of the EU Prospectus Regulation
in 2019 as a natural experiment to examine whether mandatory disclosure require-
ments change what issuers write and how the market prices it. To the extent that
we find a relationship between informative content and first-day prices, the logic
of Benveniste and Spindt (1989) and Hanley and Hoberg (2010) provides a way to
interpret it: issuers who disclose more upfront reduce the information gap between
themselves and investors, which in turn reduces the need to underprice. In this
way, the paper extends an established methodology to a new market while adding
a regulatory dimension that the original study did not address.

2.2 Regulatory Background
The regulatory framework governing prospectus disclosure in Sweden has under-
gone substantial transformation over the period covered by our sample. Prior to
                                                                          2
2005, Swedish prospectus rules consisted of multiple different laws,  guidelines from
the Swedish Financial Supervisory Authority (“Finansinspektionen”) and industry
self-regulation, such as exchange rulebooks and market practice (Andersson, 2009).
These parallel regimes applied different concepts and thresholds for when a prospec-
tus was required and what it was obligated to disclose, resulting in prospectuses
that varied substantially in both structure and content.
   For Sweden, this domestic heterogeneity was resolved with the implementation
of the EU Prospectus Directive (2003/71/EC) (“Directive”) – adopted at the EU
level to harmonize prospectus requirements across member states – through SFS
          3
2005:833 , which came into effect on 1 January 2006 (Swedish Parliament, 2005).
The reform consolidated prospectus rules into a single, comprehensive chapter of the
Act on Trading in Financial Instruments (LHF) 1991:980 (Swedish Parliament, 1991)
and assigned Finansinspektionen (FI) the responsibility for prospectus approval. It
also introduced a uniform materiality standard, requiring disclosure of all informa-
tion necessary for investors to assess the issuer’s financial position and prospects.
The detailed content requirements were further specified by the European Commis-
sion (2004), which prescribed annexes covering historical financial information, risk
factors, business description, governance, ownership structure, and dividend policy,
among other items. This created the baseline structure for all Swedish IPO prospec-
tuses on regulated markets between 2006 and 2019, and it is the regime under which
the first part of our sample was produced.
   A following, more fundamental shift occurred with the adoption of the EU
Prospectus Regulation (European Parliament and Council, 2017), which replaced
the Directive and took full effect on 21 July 2019. The motivation behind the Reg-
ulation was based on evaluations of the Directive’s finding that prospectuses had
become excessively long and increasingly built on generic, compliance-oriented lan-
guage, which was more to manage issuers’ liabilities than to provide meaningful

3. Data Description

To construct our sample of IPO prospectuses from Swedish firms, we employed a
web crawling algorithm on FI’s website. We extracted three separate datasets from
FI: (1) the prospectus archive, with 1,510 prospectuses; (2) the current prospectus
registers for EU Growth prospectuses, with 500 prospectuses; and (3) the current
prospectus registers for full prospectuses with, 219 prospectuses. The prospectuses
available from FI range from October 2006 to January 2026, which sets the outer
bounds for the sample period. The files were saved using a standardized naming
convention of firm name followed by prospectus date (FirmName_Date.pdf). We
have included IPOs from regulated and unregulated markets, as both produce a full
EU prospectus: regulated markets in every case, and unregulated markets when
the offer exceeds 2.5 million euros, and the issuer qualifies as an SME under the
MiFID II definition, as explained in Section 2.2.
   To identify true IPO transactions, we collected a dataset from S&P Capital IQ,
containing all completed IPO transactions in Sweden between 2006 and 2026, re-
sulting in 725 observations. This dataset includes parameters such as firm name,
transaction date, and the percentage change in stock price one day after the trans-
action, which are used in the main analysis. The transaction date is particularly
important since, after regulatory approval, firms have 365 days to issue shares but
are not obligated to do so – and our analysis requires confirmed transactions only.
All algorithmic matches between the name on FI’s website and the transaction name
from S&P Capital IQ were subsequently verified manually to ensure accuracy. For
transactions lacking a corresponding prospectus in the FI archive, we attempted to
source the correct document manually. In the majority of these cases, we did not
find the full prospectus but an information memorandum for the firm, which is a
common lighter document to present when exempted from issuing a full prospectus.
The information memoranda are not included in the sample, as they are not offi-
cial prospectuses and do not contain the structured disclosure content required for
our analysis. This matching and filtering process left us with 409 transactions with
a corresponding prospectus. The significant attrition from the sample of around
700 prospectuses is primarily explained by the number of simplified prospectuses
(“Förenklat prospekt”) in the raw data. Like the memoranda, these prospectuses
are a lighter version of a full prospectus for firms conducting secondary offerings,
employee share schemes, or certain merger-related share issuances. Because these
documents do not follow the same disclosure structure as full IPO prospectuses
and often do not represent first-time public offerings, they are excluded from our
analysis.
   We then compare the prospectus dates, taken from the file name, with the trans-
action date from the S&P Capital IQ dataset, to ensure that each prospectus is
available within a span of 365 days from the offering, the legal time limit within
which the firm must issue after approval of their prospectus. This reduces the sam-
ple to 380 prospectuses, which are subsequently converted from PDF to HTML using
Claude Opus 4.6 (Anthropic) to enable text comparison. Due to the structural dif-
ferences across documents – including variation in formatting, embedded tables, and
scanning quality – 11 prospectuses could not be converted reliably, leaving a sample
of 269 prospectuses.

4. Text Measurement and Content Decomposition

This section explains how the textual content of each IPO prospectus is transformed
into measurable variables. We start by defining the key pricing, firm-level, and text-
based variables used in the analysis. We then describe how each prospectus is
divided into six core sections and converted into normalized word vectors. Finally,
we explain how these word vectors are decomposed into standard and informative
content, which form the central disclosure measures used in the empirical analysis
in Section 5.

4.1 Variable Description
With the data in our master dataset, we compute several different variables, all
necessary for the research question and control variables we aim to examine. Panel A
consists of three main variables: price adjustment (∆P ) declares the direction of the
adjustment between the price found in the prospectus and the price on the first day
of trading; absolute price adjustment (|∆P |) is the magnitude of that price direction;
and the initial return (IR) is the underpricing, meaning the percentage change from
the offer price to the first-day closing price in the secondary market.
   In Panel B we find variables important for the pricing data, as well as charac-
teristics of the firm, its underwriter, and the market the firm operates in. These
variables and their individual descriptions are found in Appendix A.
    As for the prospectus text variables, Panel C, the total length of the document is
noted as total words in document. The documents are then split into sections to see
each part of the document’s importance to the price adjustment. These six sections

4.2 Research Design
We aim to explore how the information content has changed after the EU Regulation
went into effect in 2019, and subsequently, if this has influenced the pricing of IPOs
on the Swedish market. To do this, we recreate the text analysis model employed
by Hanley and Hoberg (2010) to divide each IPO prospectus into standard and
informative content.
   By looking at the information content before and after the Regulation came into
effect, we can examine whether the Regulation had an effect on the Swedish IPO
market. From this, we expect that:
Prediction 1: The EU Prospectus Regulation will be associated with an increase
   in informative content, particularly in the Summary and Risk Factors sections.
Prediction 2: A higher degree of informative content will be associated with lower
   initial returns, as greater pre-market disclosure reduces information asymmetry
   between issuers and investors.

4.3 Text Measures
Each IPO prospectus was divided into six core sections. These six sections were
chosen to reflect the major building blocks of a Swedish IPO prospectus as mandated
under European Commission (2004) and the Regulation. Each section serves a
distinct informational purpose and targets different aspects of the issuer and the
offering, meaning that content similarity and informational properties are likely to
vary systematically across them. Analyzing sections separately, therefore, allows us
to assess whether the effects of the Regulation were concentrated in specific parts
of the document rather than distributed uniformly. A detailed description of the
content and role of each section is provided in Appendix B.
   For each IPO i, we stored the HTML prospectus text into seven different word
vectors, denoted:
   • we tot,i containing the entire document
   • we sum,i containing the Summary section
   • we risk,i containing the Risk Factors section
   • we B&M,i containing the Background & Motive section
   • we bus,i containing the Business Description section
   • we fin,i containing the Financial Information section
   • we other,i containing the Other section
   All these vectors are based on the same world root vector, containing 3,496
unique word roots. We use word roots rather than actual words to capture varia-
tions in words that, in essence, are the same. Furthermore, certain types of words
have been excluded, such as articles, conjunctions, and personal pronouns (for more
information about the creation of the word root vector, see Appendix D). By con-
struction, all seven vectors for a given IPO i have the same length, 3,496, where
each element of the vector shows how many times that specific root word exists
in the text/section. All vectors are then normalized in order to only capture the
relative occurrence of a word, disregarding how long the text/section as a whole is.
This normalization is important for comparison, as EU Growth prospectuses follow

                                   11
Gummesson & Jäger What the Market Reads, 2026

a similar disclosure structure to full prospectuses, but in a shorter format.

4.4 Common IPO Content
With the normalized word vectors constructed, we can look at what drives similari-
ties and differences in prospectus content across our sample. To do this, we compute
the cosine similarity between every possible pair (i, j) of IPOs, both for the total
document and for each of the six sections separately. The cosine similarity between
two word vectors is bounded between 0 and 1, where a value of 1 indicates that the
two documents have an identical relative word distribution, and a value of 0 indi-
cates that they share no words at all. With a sample of 269 IPOs, this yields 19,503
unique pairs per word vector. We then regress these pairwise similarities on a set
of variables describing how similar two IPOs are in terms of participant variables,
market and timing, and firm characteristics, with standard errors clustered by IPO
i to account for each IPO appearing in multiple pairs:

          CosineSimij,s = α + β1Pij + β2Mij + β3Dij + β4Cij + εij (1)
where CosineSimij,s is the cosine similarity between IPO i and IPO j for section
s. Participant variables Pij include indicators for Same Lead Underwriter, Same
Underwriter Counsel, and Same Auditor. Market and timing variables Mij include
Same Industry Group and Same Transaction Window, where the window is set to 90
days following Hanley and Hoberg (2010). Dummy variables Dij include indicators
for Both Tech, Both PE-backed, Both EU Regulation, Both EU Growth, and Both
Regulated Market. Continuous variables Cij include the absolute difference in firm
age (in years), the absolute difference in prospectus year, and the absolute difference
in log filing size. Standard errors are clustered by IPO i, to account for each IPO
appearing in N − 1 = 268 pairs.
    A positive coefficient on a given variable therefore means that sharing that char-
acteristic is associated with more similar prospectus content, while a negative coeffi-
cient indicates the opposite. The regression thus tells us what factors systematically
explain why two prospectuses look alike – in other words, it reveals the sources of
standardized content in our sample. This is an important step, as it shows that
there are clear and systematic factors behind content similarity, which supports and
motivates the next step, where each prospectus is decomposed into standard and
informative components.

4.5 Informative versus Standard Content

Regulation, we decompose each prospectus word vector, we tot,i, into its standard and

content as content that is unique to a given IPO.

same-industry IPOs filed more than 90 days before IPO i, with no upper bound. The
90-day cutoff ensures that the two sources do not overlap. As shown in section 4.4,
both components have a strong systematic relation to the wording of prospectuses,
consistent with the findings of Hanley and Hoberg (2010).

5. Empirical Strategy

Section 4 develops the key text-based measures that are the foundation for this
section. By comparing each prospectus to relevant peer documents, we decompose
disclosure into standard and informative content and show how these evolved around
the Regulation. With these measures in place, we now turn to testing whether the
Regulation affected disclosure and whether these changes are related to IPO pricing
outcomes.
   The empirical analysis is structured around the two predictions formulated in
Section 4.2. The first step examines whether the Regulation changed the informa-
tional composition of IPO prospectuses (Prediction 1). The second step examines
whether the balance of standard and informative content within a prospectus is
associated with first-day underpricing, and whether that relationship is moderated
by the regulatory regime (Prediction 2). Both steps build directly on the content
measures constructed in Sections 4.3–4.5.

5.1 Testing Prediction 1 – Content and Regulation
To test whether the Regulation altered the informational composition of prospec-
tuses, we estimate OLS regressions built on those used by Hanley and Hoberg (2010).
These regressions have a dependent variable that is either the standard or the in-
formative content scalar for IPO i in section s, as derived from the decomposition
in Section 4.5. For each section and content type, we estimate two specifications:

 Contenti,s = α + β1 · EURegi + β2 · EUGrowthi + γ · Controlsi + δt + δind + εi (7)

    where EURegi equals 1 for IPOs filed after 21 July 2019, and EUGrowthi equals
1 for IPOs using the EU Growth Prospectus format. Since EU Growth firms are
a strict subset of post-Regulation issuers (EUGrowth ⊂ EUReg), the two dummies
are separately identified: EUReg captures the effect of the full Regulation on firms
filing a standard prospectus, while EUGrowth captures the incremental effect of the
Growth format relative to that baseline. Column (1) includes the regime dummies
with year and industry fixed effects but without controls. Column (2) adds the full
set of firm and market controls. If coefficients shift materially between columns, this
indicates that the apparent regulatory effect is driven by the composition of firms
going public under each regime rather than the Regulation itself.
    Year fixed effects (δt) and industry group fixed effects (δind) absorb aggregate
time trends and sector-level disclosure norms. Standard errors are clustered two-
way by industry group and year throughout, following the approach of Hanley and
Hoberg (2010). This accounts for the concentration of IPO activity within years,
most notably the 2021 boom (as seen in Appendix G, Table 3 and 4), while capturing
residual correlation across firms within the same industry.
   We estimate all specifications separately for the total document and each of
the six prospectus sections, though the main text focuses on the total document,
Summary, and Risk Factors sections, which were subject to significant regulatory
changes. Full section-level results are reported in Appendix G.

5.2 Testing Prediction 2 – Content and Pricing
To test whether prospectus content is associated with underpricing and whether that
link is moderated by the regulatory regime, we estimate two complementary sets of
                                                                                               8
regressions, both using the initial return (IR) as the primary pricing outcome.
    The first set follows a step-by-step structure to assess how sensitive the content
coefficients are to the inclusion of controls and regulatory dummies. The dependent
variable is winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles to mitigate the influence of
outliers; robustness to alternative winsorisation levels is examined in Appendix G
Table 30. Column (1) regresses IR on standard and informative content with year
and industry fixed effects only. Column (2) adds the Regulation and EU Growth
dummies. Column (3) adds the full set of firm and market controls:

        IRi = α + β1 · Standi,s + β2 · Infi,s + β3 · EURegi + β4 · EUGrowthi
              + γ · Controlsi + δt + δind + εi (8)

   If the content coefficients shift substantially across columns, this would indi-
cate that regulatory timing or firm characteristics confound the raw content-pricing
relationship. If they remain stable, this supports a more direct interpretation.
   The second set holds controls fixed across all columns and instead varies the
treatment of the regulatory regime. Column (1) is the baseline specification with full
controls. Column (2) adds the EU regime dummies additively. Column (3) further
introduces four interaction terms to test whether the content-pricing relationship
differs across regulatory regimes:

        IRi = α + β1 · Standi,s + β2 · Infi,s + β3 · EURegi + β4 · EUGrowthi
                                                            
             + β5 ·  Standi,s × EURegi  + β6 ·  Infi,s × EURegi
                                                                   
             + β7 ·  Standi,s × EUGrowthi  + β8 ·  Infi,s × EUGrowthi
             + γ · Controlsi + δt + δind + εi (9)

   The null hypothesis that the EU regime does not moderate the content-pricing
channel (H0: β5 = β6 = β7 = β8 = 0) is tested formally using a joint Wald F -test.
Rejection would indicate that the Regulation altered not only what issuers disclose,
but also how the market prices a given unit of standard or informative content.
   Fixed effects, controls, and the two-way clustering structure are identical to
those used in the content regressions in Section 5.1. All regressions are estimated
separately for the total document, the Summary section, and the Risk Factors section
as the main specifications, with full section-level results in Appendix G.

5.3 Limitations with the Methodology
A key limitation is that prospectus content is likely endogenous to expected pricing
outcomes. Firms that anticipate difficult market conditions may choose to use more
standardized language, meaning that content and pricing are jointly determined

rather than clearly causal. Addressing this would require an instrument that affects

   Another limitation relates to data quality. Unlike the US, Sweden does not
provide public filings in HTML format, but only in PDF. The prospectuses are
converted from PDF to HTML to machine-readable text, which can introduce noise

older documents. Although we have manual checks, some degree of measurement
error is likely in the word vectors. This is especially important for the section-level
analysis, since words may sometimes be assigned to the wrong section. Smaller
sections, such as Background & Motive, are more sensitive to this type of error
because a few misplaced words can have a larger effect on the section’s measured
content.
   Thirdly, with 269 IPOs, our sample is substantially smaller than the 1,750 IPOs

used by Hanley and Hoberg (2010),  which reduces statistical power and limits the
precision of the standard content estimation, particularly for sections with relatively
few words, such as Background & Motive.

6.2 Results – Prediction 2: Effects on Price
Using the step-by-step method outlined in Section 5.2, Table 5 presents OLS regres-
sions of the initial return (IR) on: (1) standard and informative content; (2) the
EU Regulation and EU Growth dummies; and (3) the full set of firm and market
controls.
   Across all three selected sections in Table 5, neither standard nor informative
content is statistically significant. Adding the EU Regulation and EU Growth dum-
mies in Column (2), does not materially change the coefficients, and adding the full
set of controls in Column (3) does not alter the main conclusion. This suggests
that prospectus content does not have a clear unconditional relationship with IPO
underpricing in the Swedish setting.
    Figure 1 confirms this result visually. The confidence intervals for both standard
and informative content are wide and consistently include zero across specifications.
The direction of the coefficient signs for the Total Document and Risk Factors sec-
tions are consistent with Hanley and Hoberg (2010), with standard content generally
being positively and informative content negatively, but the estimates are too impre-
cise to draw a conclusion. Thus, unlike Hanley and Hoberg (2010), we do not find
evidence that informative content is associated with lower underpricing in Sweden.
   Taken together, Table 5 and Figure 1 point to the same conclusion: the estimates
are too imprecise to say whether the relationship between disclosure content and
underpricing is positive or negative. While the estimates for the total document
and Risk Factors sections show standard content leaning positive and informative
content leaning negative – directions consistent with Hanley and Hoberg (2010) –
the confidence intervals are wide enough that we can not tell the directions of the
findings. This is a difference from Hanley and Hoberg (2010), who find a clear and
significant relationship in their US sample: informative content reduces underpricing
by approximately 8%, while standard content is associated with larger first-day
returns. We find the same directional pattern, but the effect is far weaker and
indistinguishable from zero.
    This difference is important in connection to the discussion in Section 2.1. Hanley
and Hoberg’s (2010) framework, informative content reduces underpricing because
it substitutes for costly information production during bookbuilding, consistent with
the logic of Benveniste and Spindt (1989). If this mechanism were visible in Sweden,
informative content should be more clearly associated with pricing accuracy, which
is not the case. One potential reason is that 77% of Swedish IPOs in our sample
have zero price adjustment, indicating that fixed-price offerings are common. When
the offer price is not revised in response to investor demand, the bookbuilding chan-
nel through which disclosure affects pricing is partly muted. In such a setting,
underpricing may instead function more as a participation discount for uninformed
investors, as in Rock (1986)), than as compensation for information revelation dur-
ing bookbuilding. Price discovery may therefore rely less on prospectus text alone
and more on institutional features of the IPO process, including repeated interac-
tions between underwriters and institutional investors, as emphasized by Cornelli
and Goldreich (2001). This interpretation helps explain why the content-pricing
relationship is weaker than in the US setting studied by Hanley and Hoberg (2010).

rejection here is more statistical than economic.

Swedish IPOs – the strongest empirical support is found neither in the content com-
position regressions (Prediction 1) nor in the pricing regressions (Prediction 2), but
in the interaction tests. The key contribution is therefore slightly different from the
original predictions: the Regulation did not mechanically increase firm-specific dis-
closure, and it did not produce a simple reduction in underpricing through increasing
informative content. Instead, the Regulation appears to have altered how the market
prices disclosure content. This connects to the mechanism documented by Chang,
Ljungqvist, and Tseng (2023), where the introduction of EDGAR affected analyst

behavior not by increasing the amount of information available, but by changing
how mandatory disclosure was processed and interpreted. In a similar way, our
results suggest that the Regulation changed investors’ interpretation of prospectus
language, not just its structure. A possible explanation is that, by forcing the Risk
Factors section to be more specific, material, and ranked in order of importance,
boilerplate language became easier to identify and therefore more informative about
a firm’s underlying state. Post-Regulation, a unit of standard risk content may
therefore signal more information asymmetry than pre-Regulation, explaining why
the content-pricing relationship strengthened.

6.3 Robustness Checks
The findings in Sections 6.1 and 6.2 depend on several empirical choices: how the
joint Wald F -test holds across alternative specifications, how standard errors are
clustered, how the sample is split around the Regulation and whether a Chow test
confirms a structural break, how the price-revision channel is treated given Sweden’s
prevalence of fixed-price offers, how underwriter fixed effects are handled, how the
decomposition window is defined, how outliers are winsorised, whether EU Growth
firms are confounded with multilateral trading facility listings, and how the 2021
IPO boom is handled in inference. We test each of these choices in turn to establish
which conclusions from Sections 6.1 and 6.2 are robust to alternative specifications
and which are sensitive to specific modelling choices.
PCA Dimensionality Reduction of Word Vectors
   The word vectors used in the baseline analysis span a 3,496-dimensional vocab-
ulary space (constructed as described in Section 4.3), far exceeding the 269 obser-
vations in our sample. While the Hanley and Hoberg (2010) methodology does not
require the word vectors to be full rank – the decomposition operates via OLS on
peer-average vectors, not on the raw vocabulary matrix – a legitimate concern is
that many vocabulary dimensions are redundant and that the standard and infor-
mative content measures are sensitive to noise in the high-dimensional space. We
                                               11
address this by re-running the full analysis  after compressing the word vectors via
Principal Component Analysis (PCA); for the methodology, see Appendix F.
   There is a substantial increase in the informative content mean as more PCA
components are retained, rising from 3.82 at k = 40 to 6.56 at k = 125, remaining
                                               12
well above the baseline of 0.58 throughout.   This is, however, a mechanical artifact
rather than a substantive finding. PCA discards the low-variance vocabulary tail,
                          2
and the subsequent L -normalization re-scales the residual space, meaning that the
OLS residuals from the decomposition land in a geometrically different subspace,
inflating the sum of absolute residuals that constitute the informative content mea-
sure. The cross-sectional rank ordering of prospectuses is preserved even though
absolute levels are not, which is what matters for our regressions. A related obser-
vation is that the mean standard content falls slightly while its standard deviation
increases substantially, indicating that PCA increases cross-sectional variation in the

specification does not drive the significance patterns reported in Sections 6.1 and
6.2, we re-estimate all key results using three alternative covariance structures: HC1

year, and underwriter.    The check covers six cells; definitions are provided in

out analysis supports the robustness of the main findings: the null on individual
content-pricing coefficients is stable across all year exclusions, but our moderation
finding is sensitive to exclusion of the boom year.
Summary of Robustness Checks
   The robustness tests broadly supports the main findings. The null on individ-
ual content-pricing coefficients is stable across all tests – no specification produces
a significant result and sign changes are rare. The Regulation moderation finding
survives most tests but is sensitive to the 2021 boom, which amplifies the Total Doc-
ument and Risk Factors results; the Summary section remains significant regardless.
The decision to use IR over ∆P is validated by the bookbuilding subsample check.
The one finding that does not survive is the EU Growth effect on informative con-
tent, which disappears under PCA dimensionality reduction and should therefore
be interpreted with caution.

7. Conclusion

This study aims to answer whether the EU Prospectus Regulation (EU) 2017/1129
(“Regulation”) changed the informational composition of Swedish IPO prospectuses,
and whether this affects first-day pricing. The headline answer is that the Regulation
did not change what issuers disclosed, but appears to have changed how the market
prices a given unit of disclosure.
   Prediction 1, that the Regulation would increase the degree of informative con-
tent in prospectuses, receives mixed support. For the average post-Regulation
prospectus, the Regulation itself is not associated with a robust change in either stan-

                                   36
Gummesson & Jäger What the Market Reads, 2026

dard or informative content once firm characteristics are controlled for. However,
the baseline results show a positive association between the EU Growth Prospec-
tus format and informative content, suggesting that the lighter prospectus regime
may have changed disclosure composition for the smaller issuers. Though, this find-
ing should be interpreted with caution, as it disappears under PCA dimensionality
reduction and may partly reflect changes in regulatory vocabulary rather than a
robust increase in firm-specific disclosure. Prediction 2, being that informative con-
tent would reduce underpricing, yields an unconditional null result, and the 8%
underpricing reduction Hanley and Hoberg (2010) document does not appear in the
Swedish setting. However, the joint Wald F -test shows that the content-pricing
relationship differs across regulatory regimes. This suggests that the Regulation did
not change the overall level of underpricing, but changed how the market values
different types of disclosure. Post-Regulation, a unit of standard content appears
to be priced more positively – in other words, the cost of template language has
increased.
   In contrast to Hanley and Hoberg (2010), who find an unconditional 8% reduc-
tion in underpricing, we cannot find a similar pattern in Sweden. Point estimates
are consistent with their framework – standard content loads positively on initial
returns and informative content negatively – but the confidence intervals are wide
and the magnitudes are difficult to interpret precisely. This is likely due to Sweden’s
institutional setting. The Swedish IPO market is characterized by a smaller, more
concentrated investor base, a higher prevalence of fixed-price offers accounting for
77% of zero price revisions, and a substantially smaller sample of 269 IPOs compared
to the 1,750 in Hanley and Hoberg (2010). This setting provides a potential expla-
nation for the muted content-pricing relationship. In a market like Sweden, where
a small number of repeat investors hold most of the relevant private information,
the bookbuilding process can extract that information effectively – as Cornelli and
Goldreich (2001) document. In other words, the price is largely set through direct
interaction between the issuer and a familiar group of investors, leaving less work
for the prospectus to do. This is the key structural difference from the US setting:
in Sweden, textual disclosure matters less at the margin because the bookbuilding
process has already done much of the heavy lifting before the offer price is set. To-
gether, these features help explain why our results look different from Hanley and
Hoberg (2010) – the theory was built on assumptions that fit the US market better
than Sweden, and when these assumptions cease to hold, a weaker content-pricing
relationship is what we would expect to find.
   One contribution of this paper is therefore that it is a replication of the tex-
tual methodology of Hanley and Hoberg (2010) outside the US, showing that the
standard–informative decomposition can be applied in a non-US regulatory envi-
ronment, even if the magnitudes differ. The regulatory setting further allows the
decomposition to be used as a before–after test of whether harmonised disclosure
rules reshape the content–pricing link. More distinctly, the paper shows that con-
tent composition and the market’s pricing of that content can move independently
of each other – the Regulation did not significantly change what issuers wrote, but
it changed how much that writing mattered for pricing.

8. Limitations and Suggestions for Future Research

There are limitations of this study that give directions for future research. One
central limitation is geographic scope. The Regulation came into force across all
27 member states in July 2019, meaning the studied regulatory shift is not unique
to Sweden. Replicating the Hanley and Hoberg (2010) decomposition for other,
larger markets such as Germany, France, and the Netherlands would directly test
whether our findings are specific to Sweden or reflect an EU-wide regulatory effect.
A cross-country study would also substantially increase statistical power beyond
what a single-country sample can provide.
   A second direction concerns topic-level composition of prospectus content. This
paper constructs topic-specific word lists following Hanley and Hoberg’s (2010) cat-
egorization of content into valuation, accounting, marketing, and due diligence lan-
guage, but these are not brought into the regression analysis. Including them would
make it possible to test more precisely where the moderation comes from – for in-
stance, whether it is concentrated in risk factor language or distributed evenly across
sections.
   On the topic of moderation, a further limitation is that, while this paper finds
that the Regulation changed how content is priced, it cannot fully explain why. The
most intuitive explanation is that the Regulation made boilerplate language easier
to identify, so investors began treating it as a signal that the issuer is not being
forthcoming – but this remains an interpretation rather than a directly tested find-
ing. Future research could examine this more directly by measuring how specific or
generic the risk factors in a prospectus are, for instance by counting uncertainty-
related words following Loughran and McDonald (2011) or tracking how often con-
crete company-specific details appear, and testing whether these measures predict
underpricing.
   Additionally, this paper argues that Sweden’s concentrated investor base re-
duces the marginal informational value of prospectus disclosure, drawing on Cornelli
and Goldreich (2001), but this hypothesis is never directly tested. With allocation
data, for example from post-IPO holdings, future research could make the proposed
mechanism more concrete by linking investor concentration to the degree to which
prospectus content moves the first-day price.
    As for AI, its rise serves as a motivation for this paper (Financial Times, 2025),
but it is never tested whether the difference between human-written and AI-generated
content plays a role. A future study could compare AI-drafted sections to human-
drafted equivalents and ask whether AI-generated content is systematically more
standard or more informative in the Hanley and Hoberg (2010) sense – and how
that maps to pricing outcomes. Given that the post-Regulation environment ap-
pears to price standard language more harshly, the answer has direct implications
for how such tools should be designed.

E. Cosine Similarity

This appendix explains how we compute the pairwise document similarity between
prospectuses i and j. The procedure closely follows Hanley and Hoberg (2010),
adapted for Swedish-language prospectuses. The same method applies to any doc-
ument section, in which case the result is referred to as section similarity.
   To measure the similarity between prospectuses i and j, we take the dot product
of their normalized word vectors divided by the product of their vector lengths.
This quantity is known as the cosine similarity because it measures the cosine of the
angle between the two vectors on a unit sphere. Formally, following equation (A1)
in Hanley and Hoberg (2010):

                                               we tot,i · we tot,j
                   Document Similaritytot,ij =                              (10)
                                            ∥w e tot,i∥ ∥w e tot,j ∥
    Because all elements of the word vectors are non-negative, the cosine similarity is
bounded in the interval [0, 1]. A value of 1 indicates that the two prospectuses have
an identical relative word distribution, while a value of 0 indicates that they share
no vocabulary at all. In practice, values closer to 1 reflect more similar prospectus
content.
   The cosine similarity is computed for the total document vector as well as for
each of the six sections separately: Prospectus Summary, Risk Factors, Background
& Motive, Business Description, Financial Information, and Other. With a sample
of N = 269 prospectuses, this yields N (N − 1)/2 = 35,778 unique prospectus pairs
per word vector.
   After constructing the per-prospectus word-count matrices, the normalized vec-
tors are unit-normalized (divided by their Euclidean norm), and the full similarity
matrix is obtained as the matrix product of unit-normalized row vectors, clipped to
[0, 1] to correct for floating-point errors. The procedure is equivalent to eq. (10) and
produces a symmetric N × N similarity matrix.
   One adaptation relative to Hanley and Hoberg (2010) concerns the stemmer.
They use English word roots identified via Webster.com. Because our prospectuses
are written in Swedish, we implement a Swedish Snowball stemmer, which applies
a sequence of suffix-stripping rules calibrated to Swedish morphology.

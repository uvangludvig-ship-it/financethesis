Leading the Wallet: How Female Headship Shapes Household Financial Behaviour
Across the Eurozone

Abstract:

   This thesis investigates the drivers of female financial headship and its impact on
   household portfolio allocation across asset classes and gender-based normative
   contexts. We develop a two-stage utility maximisation model in which households
   assign financial headship based on observable financial potential and forward-looking
   expectations of investment performance, subject to gender norms. Using microdata
   from the European Central Bank’s (ECB) Household Finance and Consumption
   Survey (HFCS), we empirically test this framework and examine the impact of female
   headship on household financial investment decisions. First, we find that while female
   headship does not affect aggregate market participation or returns, it leads to distinct
   patterns across five core asset classes. Second, our results indicate that female headship
   is associated with lower financial literacy and reduced risk-taking. Finally, we show
   that financial behaviour and investment patterns systematically vary with a country’s
   prevailing gender norms. These findings highlight the importance of gender equality
   and the broader normative context in shaping household financial decision-making.

Keywords:

   Household finance, Gender equality, Household head, Investment decisions

I. Introduction

Gender equality is a fundamental human right, explicitly recognised in Article 23 of the Charter of Fun-
damental Rights of the European Union: “Equality between women and men must be ensured in all areas
[...]” (European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights, 2015). However, formal recognition of this right
does not guarantee its realisation in practice. Gender equality remains uneven across sectors, with finance
continuing to exhibit notable disparities (Forum, 2024; World Bank, 2015; ˇCihák & Sahay, 2018).
   To this end, we examine an underexplored dimension of gender inequality: the intra-household assign-
ment of financial decision-making authority, or “financial headship”. Traditional economic models, such as
Becker (1993), adopt a unitary approach in which a household is treated as a single decision-making entity,
implicitly assuming that financial authority defaults to the spouse with the highest comparative advantage
                                                                                                         th
in market production. This assumption has had implications beyond economic theory: in the 20  century,
male financial authority was institutionalised through numerous welfare regimes and social policies (Orloff,
1993). While this logic continues to support many models of pooled household decision-making, recent
studies highlight that perceptions of financial competence are not solely economic but deeply cultural. For
example, Alesina, Giuliano, and Nunn (2013) shows that norms are historically rooted in gendered labour
divisions, such as plough agriculture, which continue to influence attitudes across generations and geogra-
phies. Building on this literature, we reconceptualise financial headship not as a passive outcome of eco-
nomic advantage but as a forward-looking delegation decision shaped by observable traits, intra-household
dynamics, and prevailing gender norms. This paper addresses two central questions: First, what drives
the likelihood that a woman is designated as the household’s financial head? Second, how does female
headship influence household portfolio choices across asset classes, and how do these effects vary across
different gender-based normative contexts? In doing so, we link micro-level delegation processes to macro-
level patterns in household investment behaviour, offering new insights into how financial agency is shaped
(and constrained) by gender norms.
   We used household-level microdata from the European Central Bank’s Household Finance and Con-
sumption Survey (HFCS), covering four cross-sectional waves (2010, 2014, 2017, 2021) across 23 Eurozone
countries. The HFCS provides harmonised data on household balance sheets and demographics, allowing
cross-country comparability. The Eurozone offers a unique empirical setting: while unified by a common
monetary system, its member states differ substantially in institutional structures and prevailing gender
norms. To capture this normative heterogeneity, we classify countries as either Egalitarian or Traditional
based on the Gender Equality Index (GEI) developed by the European Institute for Gender Equality (EIGE).
   We begin our analysis by developing a two-stage utility maximisation model to analyse how households
assign financial decision-making authority. At time t = 0, households evaluate the expected financial poten-
tial of each spouse based on observable characteristics such as income, education, participation in the labour
market, age and retirement status and weigh this against the disutility of deviating from the prevailing gender
norms. Crucially, this model implies that this delegation is forward-looking. Households form expectations
about the financial head’s future portfolio decisions and returns at time t = 1. These expectations feed back
into the headship decision, incorporating investment considerations into the initial delegation of authority.
This is an extension of the framework developed by Guiso and Zaccaria (2023). However, our model intro-
duces a tractable norm penalty that varies across countries based on structural indicators of gender equality,
allowing us to empirically assess how norm salience affects the probability of female financial headship
across institutional contexts. We test the predictions of the model by estimating the likelihood of female

                                          1
leadership as a function of household characteristics, identifying the conditions under which women are
more likely to assume financial leadership, both in aggregate and in differing normative contexts.
   Next, we examine how female headship influences portfolio allocation across five core asset classes:
government bonds, other fixed income, stocks, mutual funds, and deposits. We further disaggregate bond
holdings (e.g., bank, corporate, and other) and mutual funds (e.g., equity, bond, real estate, hedge funds, and
other) to capture granular differences in risk exposure and investment preferences. This multi-asset approach
allows us to capture gendered differences across the full spectrum of household financial portfolios.
    We thereafter assess financial outcomes by estimating both total and asset-class specific returns. To inter-
pret these differences, we analyse risk tolerance and financial literacy among household heads, highlighting
behavioural variables that may explain observed gendered patterns in portfolio allocation.
   Finally, we look at how normative contexts influence these dynamics. Using the Gender Equality Index
(GEI), we compare patterns in egalitarian and traditional countries to determine how institutionalised gender
norms impact the relationship between female financial headship and household portfolio behaviour.
   Our results show that the proportion of female financial heads in the Eurozone has declined modestly over
the past decade, with the steepest decline appearing in traditional countries. This trend continues despite
advances in factors such as female labour force participation and educational attainment, implying that
structural progress has not yet translated into greater intra-household financial authority. The persistence of
this gap indicates the importance of informal norms in constraining women’s decision-making power within
households.
    We also find that female financial headship has no significant impact on aggregate financial market partic-
ipation or portfolio total returns. However, a disaggregated analysis reveals systematic variation in portfolio
composition and asset-level returns. Female-headed households are more likely to invest in mutual funds,
particularly equity and other mutual funds, while holding fewer fixed income securities, such as corporate
bonds. Additionally, female headship is associated with significantly higher returns on mutual fund invest-
ments, suggesting that female-headed households may outperform their male-headed counterparts in this
asset class.
   We subsequently find that female household heads in our sample report lower levels of financial literacy
and exhibit greater risk aversion. Both of these variables influence investment behaviour, reinforcing the
idea that gender differences in financial preferences and capabilities contribute to portfolio heterogeneity
across households.
   Finally, we find that these dynamics differ meaningfully across normative contexts. In egalitarian coun-
tries, financial potential plays a more decisive role in the assignment of household headship, and the prob-
ability of a woman being designated as the financial head is significantly higher. In contrast, in traditional
countries, the impact of financial characteristics is muted, and the threshold for women to assume headship
appears higher. We also find that portfolio allocation, investment returns, and financial behaviour vary sys-
tematically with a country’s prevailing gender norms. This normative framework emphasises the significant
role that the broader cultural and institutional environment plays in shaping intra-household financial roles
and decisions.
   One possible limitation of our findings is that the normative classifications derived from the Gender
Equality Index (GEI) may not fully reflect cultural attitudes or unobservable aspects of gender norms. To
address this, we perform a robustness check on an alternative, attitudinally based measure of gender norms
derived from the European Values Study (EVS), which captures self-reported beliefs about gender roles.
We find that the main drivers of female headship, market participation, and financial returns remain largely

robust. However, our findings on portfolio allocation differ, implying that investment decisions are more
influenced by how gender norms are measured. This most likely reflects fundamental differences between
the two measures: the EVS measures individual attitudes, whereas the GEI measures structural indicators
of gender equality at the societal level.
   This paper contributes to multiple strands of literature. First, we bridge two areas that are often exam-
ined in isolation: intra-household financial decision-making and gender differences in investment behaviour.
We develop a conceptual framework in which financial headship is assigned through a forward-looking,
utility-maximising delegation, based on each partner’s financial potential and constrained by prevailing
social norms. This approach moves beyond models that assume symmetric or pooled decision-making,
offering a more dynamic view of how financial authority is allocated within households. Early work in
household economics typically relied on unitary models, which assumed that couples shared financial au-
thority to maximise joint utility and income (Becker, 1993; Dew, 2008; Kim, Gutter, & Spangler, 2017).
More recent research challenges this view, showing that spouses often have divergent preferences and that
financial responsibilities are frequently delegated to one partner (Bertocchi, Brunetti, & Torricelli, 2014;
Kim et al., 2017). However, such delegation tends to follow traditional gender lines. For example, Meier,
Kirchler, and Hubert (1999) finds that women are more likely to defer investment and savings decisions to
male partners, while Skogrand, Johnson, Horrocks, and DeFrain (2015) reports that men are more inclined
to take charge of household finances, often due to greater experience or confidence. Yet, these patterns are
not uniform: Bertocchi et al. (2014) shows that women are more likely to assume financial headship when
their income, education, or age matches or exceeds that of their partner. These insights are extended by
collective household models, which incorporate preference heterogeneity and intra-household bargaining
dynamics (Chiappori, 1992; Lundberg, Pollak, & Wales, 1997; McElroy & Horney, 1981). In parallel, a
large body of literature on gender and financial behaviour shows that women tend to be more risk-averse,
less confident in their financial knowledge, and less likely to invest in stock markets (Bajtelsmit & Bernasek,
1997; Barber & Odean, 2001; Bucher-Koenen, Janssen, Knebel, & Tzamourani, 2023; Lusardi & Mitchell,
2008; Wagner & Walstad, 2023). However, much of this work focuses on individuals, overlooking how
investment decisions are made within households. By contrast, we bridge these strands, and our contri-
bution thereby becomes twofold: first, we reconceptualise financial headship as an endogenous outcome
shaped by observable traits – such as income, education, and labour market participation – and constrained
by prevailing gender norms; second, we explore how the gender of the designated financial head influences
household investment decisions and asset-specific returns. In doing so, we demonstrate that gendered finan-
cial outcomes are shaped not only by individual characteristics and preferences but also by gender norms
that govern who is empowered to act.
   Second, we extend the empirical scope of household finance along two key dimensions: asset cover-
age and geographic scale. First, most existing studies focus narrowly on a single asset class, typically
stocks or retirement accounts (Bajtelsmit & Bernasek, 1997; Barber & Odean, 2001; Itzkowitz, Itzkowitz,
& Schwartz, 2023), with the notable exception of Guiso and Zaccaria (2023), who analyse five asset classes.
Our study builds on this by not only covering five core asset categories, but also further disaggregating hold-
ings within fixed income and mutual funds to capture greater heterogeneity in investment behaviour and risk
exposure. Second, while many prior studies are based on a single country (Brown, Henchoz, & Spycher,
2017; Davoli, 2020; Fuchs-Schündeln, Masella, & Paule-Paludkiewicz, 2020), our analysis spans 23 Euro-
zone countries, enabling a broader investigation of normative variation. Third, while existing studies have
examined the role of cultural determinants in household financial decision-making (Gupta & Blight, 2024;

                                          3
Ke, 2018), we take a unique approach by classifying countries into traditional or egalitarian groups based
on their Gender Equality Index (GEI) scores. To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to use this index
as a proxy for gender norms, providing new evidence on systematic differences within the Eurozone.
   Lastly, we advance the conceptual framework of intra-household financial delegation by extending the
utility maximisation model proposed in Guiso and Zaccaria (2023). We extend it in four key ways. First, we
define financial potential explicitly using observable household characteristics such as income, education,
labour market participation, age, and retirement status, rather than treating ability as a latent construct. Sec-
ond, we operationalise the gender norm penalty as a tractable, country-level classification based on structural
indicators, rather than a latent community-specific variable. Third, we incorporate forward-looking expec-
tations by explicitly linking headship decisions at time t = 0 to anticipated investment outcomes at time
t = 1, thus formalising the role of expected portfolio returns in delegation. Finally, we formulate and solve
a decision-making rule that determines under what conditions a female is chosen as the household head.
   The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. Section II. outlines the conceptual framework.
Section III. presents the data. Section IV. details the empirical methodology and main results. Section V.
provides a robustness check. Section VI. concludes the paper.

II. Conceptual Framework

We model household financial behaviour as a two-stage utility maximisation process spanning across two
distinct time periods. At time t = 0, households assign financial headship by weighing each spouse’s ex-
pected financial potential against prevailing gender norms. This decision is forward-looking: households
form expectations about how each spouse would perform as a financial head, specifically in terms of portfo-
lio allocation and returns at time t = 1. These expectations inform and feed back into the headship decision
at t = 0. Our model builds on the work of Guiso and Zaccaria (2023), who frame headship as a trade-
off between expected financial returns and conformity to gender norms. We retain this core structure but
extend and refine it in four key ways. First, we explicitly define financial potential using a set of observ-
able household characteristics, including income, education, age, retirement status, and participation in the
labour market, rather than treating the ability as a latent construct. Second, we operationalise the term
gender norm penalty as a tractable, cross-country classification, rather than a latent parameter. Third, we
explicitly link financial headship to forward-looking expectations of portfolio returns. Lastly, we formulate
a decision-making rule that determines under which conditions a female is selected as the household head.
    To formalise these dynamics, we express household i’s utility from assigning financial headship to either
spouse as:

III. Data Sources and Description

A. Data Sources

Our primary data source is the Household Finance and Consumption Survey (HFCS), a cross-national
dataset coordinated by the European Central Bank (ECB) in collaboration with national central banks and
statistical institutes. The HFCS provides detailed household-level information on balance sheets, income,
consumption, private pensions, employment, and demographics, enabling a comprehensive micro-level anal-
ysis of household financial behaviour across Eurozone countries. The HFCS is administered in a decen-
tralised manner, with each country’s national central bank or statistical office responsible for conducting
fieldwork while the ECB oversees coordination, data pooling, and quality control. Given the substantial
cultural and institutional differences across Eurozone countries, the ECB acknowledges the need for some
flexibility in question formulation to ensure the collection of comparable data. While participating central
banks and institutes produce harmonised survey data for their respective countries, they are not required to
use identical questionnaires. Instead, a common template serves as a guiding framework for both designing
country-specific surveys and defining the intended output. Despite possible variations in the questionnaire
design, participating institutions are mandated to report a standardised set of core output variables. This
standardisation is crucial to ensuring cross-country comparability of the data.
   The HFCS questionnaire comprises two main sections. The first focuses on the household as a unit and
collects information on real and financial assets, liabilities and credit constraints, as well as consumption and
saving behaviours. The second section focuses on individual household members, providing demographic
information for each member, along with questions on topics such as employment, income, pensions, and
                                                                                 3
other related areas. Crucial to our study, the survey contains standardised questions  to identify the respon-
dent most knowledgeable about the household’s finances – referred to as the “financially knowledgeable

are directed to the FKP, while questions concerning personal matters (e.g., income) are addressed to each
individual household member. Consistent with Guiso and Zaccaria (2023), we refer to the FKP as the
household head. Therefore, the terms FKP and household head are used interchangeably throughout this

   To construct our sample dataset, we utilise all available survey waves for which data access was granted,
specifically the 2010, 2014, 2017, and 2021 waves. Fieldwork for the first three waves was conducted
during 2010–2011, 2013–2015, and 2017, respectively. The 2021 wave, originally scheduled for 2020,
experienced delays and partial disruptions due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Consequently, fieldwork was
conducted at different times across countries between early 2020 and mid-2022, raising some comparability
concerns given the varying economic conditions during the pandemic. We further restrict our sample to
households composed of heterosexual couples in either legally married or consensual union (cohabitating)
arrangements. Additionally, we retain only households where the financially knowledgeable person (FKP)
is one of the two spouses, excluding cases in which the FKP is an external individual (e.g., a private wealth
manager). Finally, our constructed sample contains one observation per household, including demographic
characteristics of the FKP and household-level responses to core financial questions.
   This processing results in a filtered dataset comprising over 135,000 households across all four waves,
which we use to examine the relationship between the gender of the household head and financial behaviour.

have contributed to the stagnation and decline in female headship despite institutional progress (Staveren,
2001).

heads increased from 38.2% in 2010 to a peak of 39.2% in 2017, followed by a modest decline thereafter.

female headship peaked at 36.3% in 2014 but fell sharply to 29.0% by 2021. This divergence signals a
growing polarisation: egalitarian countries demonstrate normative resilience to gender bias, while traditional
countries may be experiencing a reinforcement of gender norms. As such, the overall decline in female
headship across the Eurozone appears to be largely driven by the pronounced post-2014 decrease observed
in traditional countries.

itarian countries. In contrast to the egalitarian group, the traditional group includes two countries, Portugal
and the Czech Republic, with exceptionally low rates of female headship, reporting just 10.5% and 4.8%,
respectively. These wide within-group and between-group disparities are consistent with the heterogeneous
nature of gender norms and point toward the continued influence of institutional and cultural barriers to

IV. Methodology and Results

A. Drivers of Female Household Headship

We begin by addressing our first research question, focusing on what drives female financial headship. We
construct a linear specification for the probability that a woman is the head (FKP) of the household:

                    FemaleHeadi,c,t = α + γFi,c,t + δ Hi,c,t + θ FtMi,c,t + λc + τt + εi,c,t , (11)

where FemaleHeadi,c,t is a binary indicator equal to 1 if the household head in household i, country c, and
wave t is female, and 0 otherwise. We control for household characteristics through three main vectors.
First, Fi,c,t captures family demographics, such as the number of adults and children in the household.
Second, Hi,c,t includes attributes of the household head, such as age, retirement status, education level,
home ownership, and income. Third, FtMi,c,t represents measures of gender-based comparative advantages,
including hours worked by men and women. We include country fixed effects (λc) as well as time fixed
effects for the respective waves (τt ) to account for unobserved heterogeneity across countries and over time.
Furthermore, we use education level as a proxy for financial literacy, and age, together with retirement
status, as a proxy for risk preferences.

   As shown in column (2) of Table 5, when we control for financial literacy using education level as a
proxy, we find that higher educational attainment is associated with a lower probability of the household
head being female. This result is unexpected, as it contrasts with our conceptual framework, which posited
that higher education enhances financial potential and, consequently, increases the likelihood of being se-
lected as the household’s financial head. In column (3), we include proxies for risk preferences, age and
retirement status, and observe a similarly negative relationship: both higher age and being retired are asso-
ciated with a lower probability of female financial headship. This finding, however, is consistent with our
framework, which suggested that older age and retirement may reduce financial potential, possibly through
their influence on risk tolerance. Our full model in column (4) confirms the robustness of these findings,
with several household and individual characteristics found to be statistically significant in explaining the
likelihood of female headship. Consistent with prior literature suggesting that traditional family structures
often preclude women from formal headship roles, both the number of adults and children in the household
are negatively associated with female headship (Employee Benefit Research Institute, 2025). Specifically,
each additional child is associated with a 0.7 percentage point decrease in the probability that a woman is

                                          16
designated as the head of household, while each additional adult decreases the probability by 0.5 percentage
points. Turning to individual-level attributes, income, education, age, and retirement status are all nega-
tively and highly significantly correlated with female headship. The probability that a woman is the head of
the household decreases by 0.3 percentage points for each additional year of age. Retirement is associated
with a 32 percentage point lower probability of female headship, suggesting a strong association between
life-cycle status and intra-household decision structures. Furthermore, both higher education and income
levels are associated with a reduced probability of female headship. These results are surprising, as they
contradict our earlier framework, which suggested that higher education and income would increase the
likelihood of being selected as the household financial head. In contrast, home ownership and hours worked
by women are, as expected and consistent with our framework, positively associated with female headship,
indicating that economic contribution and asset ownership strengthen women’s standing within the house-
hold structure. We seek to understand plausible reasons why higher education and income do not translate
into household financial decision-making among women. Prior research (Barber & Odean, 2001; Bertrand
et al., 2015; Ke, 2018; Kim et al., 2017) indicates that gender norms and confidence gaps between men and
women often reinforce male authority in financial matters. These patterns highlight that economic capacity
alone is insufficient to shift intra-household power structures, as social norms and behavioural considera-
tions continue to shape who is perceived as the "natural" financial decision-maker, which we recognise and
empirically test for in Table 13.

B. Effects of Female Household Headship on Financial Investments and Behaviour

We now investigate whether the gender of the household head influences household financial decisions, in-
cluding investment choices and financial behaviour. To perform this analysis within a multivariate regression
framework, we estimate the following model:

                Yi,c,t = α + β FemaleHeadi,c,t + γFi,c,t + δ Hi,c,t + θ FtMi,c,t + λc + τt + εi,c,t . (12)

Let Yi,c,t represent the various dependent variables related to financial investments and behaviour that we
seek to investigate. The binary variable FemaleHeadi,c,t is set to 1 if the household head in household i,
country c, and wave t is female, and 0 otherwise. We control for household characteristics using three main
vectors, consistent with those presented in Eq. (11). Country fixed effects (λc) and time fixed effects (τt ) are
included to account for unobserved heterogeneity across countries and over time.
   First, we examine the effect of female headship on financial market participation. Table 6 presents the
results from a linear probability model where the outcome variable is a binary indicator equal to 1 if the
household reports holding investments in any of the five core asset classes. That is, the dependent variable
is AssetParticipationi, as defined previously in Eq. (8). Our results indicate that the gender of the household
head has no significant effect on financial market participation. In the full sample, as shown in column (1),
the coefficient is -0.004 and statistically insignificant, indicating no meaningful relationship between female
headship and the probability of participating in financial markets. The result holds in column (2), where
restricting the sample to households with heads under 65 yields a slightly positive but still insignificant
coefficient of 0.007. This indicates that the findings are not driven by age-related participation patterns and
helps mitigate concerns that the hump-shaped investment profile around retirement distorts the relationship
between headship and financial market participation (Fagereng, Gottlieb, & Guiso, 2017).

We now turn our attention to another crucial determinant of financial decision-making: financial literacy.
While risk preferences capture individuals’ tolerance for volatility, financial literacy shapes their ability to
form expectations about asset returns and understand the structure of financial markets.
   To empirically examine the effect of female headship on financial literacy, we draw on four financial
literacy questions included in a subset of HFCS waves which assess respondents’ understanding of interest
rates, inflation, diversification, and the risk–return characteristics of financial instruments. The full wording
of each question and its possible responses is presented in Table B.7. To allow for a more nuanced analysis,
we construct two binary indicators capturing different levels of financial literacy. First, we construct a
binary variable, Basic Fin Literacy, which equals 1 if the household head answered one or two questions
correctly. Second, we define a binary indicator, Advanced Fin Literacy, which takes the value 1 if the head
answered three or all four questions correctly. This approach allows us to distinguish between varying

                                          23
degrees of financial knowledge, such as between a respondent who may have guessed one answer correctly
and another who demonstrates a high level of financial understanding. However, a notable limitation of this
approach is restricted data availability: the financial literacy questions were included only in Luxembourg
(Wave 2), Greece (Wave 3), and Slovakia (Waves 2 and 3). This reduces our sample to fewer than 5,000
observations, which is substantially smaller than the full HFCS dataset.
   In Table 12, we begin by estimating several regressions where the dependent variable is a binary indi-
cator equal to 1 if the head demonstrates basic financial literacy (column (1)), or if they showed advanced
financial literacy (column (2)). Our results show that female headship significantly reduces the likelihood
of answering one or two financial literacy questions correctly by almost 10 percentage points. Interestingly,
however, we find no statistically significant effect of female headship on advanced financial literacy. This
suggests that, within our highly restricted sample, female headship appears to negatively affect only lower
levels of financial literacy. While these findings align with existing literature documenting persistent gender
                                                       11
gaps in economic knowledge (Wagner & Walstad, 2023),  we provide new evidence suggesting that female
headship significantly reduces basic financial literacy but does not appear to impact more advanced levels
of financial knowledge.
   We next examine whether varying levels of financial literacy impact stock market participation. To this
end, we estimate a regression where the dependent variable is a binary indicator equal to 1 if the household
holds investments in stocks. The explanatory variables include female headship, as well as indicators for
basic and advanced financial literacy. In this way, we test both the findings of van Rooij, Lusardi, and
Alessie (2011), which show that individuals with higher financial literacy are significantly more likely to
invest in stocks, and our framework developed in Section II., which posits that household heads will only
invest in risky assets if they possess sufficiently strong financial skills. For this analysis, we proxy financial
skills using financial literacy.
   While our earlier analysis using the full sample found no significant effect of household head gender on
stock market participation, the restricted sample reveals that female headship is significantly and negatively
associated with stock market investment (see column (3)). We further find that basic levels of financial lit-
eracy are significantly and negatively associated with the likelihood of stock market participation, whereas
more advanced levels increase this likelihood. This result is somewhat surprising, as it contrasts to some
extent with the findings of van Rooij et al. (2011). Rather than observing a consistent positive relationship
between financial literacy and stock market participation, we find a non-linear pattern: participation likeli-
hood initially decreases at lower literacy levels but increases at more advanced levels. Interestingly, only
the interaction between female headship and basic financial literacy is positive and statistically significant,
suggesting that although female-headed households are generally less likely to invest in the stock market,
those with basic financial literacy are more likely to do so. We find no such effect for female heads with
advanced financial literacy.
   Although we cannot directly observe whether the household head meets the threshold of financial ability
required to invest in risky assets rather than safe bank deposits, as outlined in our framework, our findings
suggest that higher financial literacy increases the probability of stock market participation. Therefore, we
can infer that higher financial literacy may play a crucial role in surpassing this threshold and choosing to
invest in risky assets rather than opting for a safe bank deposit.

vestigate whether the relationship between female headship and, respectively, risk preferences and financial
literacy varies across normative contexts. As previously established, both factors are important determinants
of household financial behaviour. However, in this section, we explore whether these relationships differ de-
pending on the normative country group, given that both risk attitudes and financial knowledge are likely
shaped not only by countries’ cultural and socioeconomic contexts but also by the prevailing national gender
norms – whether traditional or egalitarian.
   For instance, our sample shows that household heads in egalitarian countries have significantly higher
levels of education than those in traditional countries (see Table 3), which may serve as a proxy for greater
financial literacy among heads in egalitarian contexts. Household heads in our sample in both egalitarian and
traditional countries are of nearly the same age, and a similar proportion are retired, which may proxy for
comparable risk preferences. However, it is plausible to assume that cultural factors shape risk preferences,
which may systematically differ with normative orientation.
   As reported in Table 18 column (1), our results show that both female headship and egalitarianism
individually reduce tolerance for financial risk-taking. However, we cannot find a significant result that the
effect of female headship on risk-taking differs by national gender norms. With regard to investments in
risky assets, as reported in column (2), we find no statistically significant effect of female headship when
considered in isolation. However, both the risk-taking indicator and its interaction with female headship are
statistically significant, with coefficient signs consistent with prior results (see Table 11). When controlling
for normative contexts, we observe that only the standalone effect of egalitarianism is significant, suggesting
that households in more egalitarian countries are less likely to invest in risky assets. Nonetheless, we find no
evidence that the effect of female headship on investment in risky assets varies across such normative groups.
Thus, we find no indication that the effect of female headship on either risk preferences or investment in risky

V. Robustness Check

Given our primary assumption that GEI scores provided by the EIGE allow us to accurately classify coun-
tries as traditional or egalitarian, one might question if this metric sufficiently captures cultural aspects and
unobservable dimensions of societal norms. To address this concern, we conduct a robustness check using
an alternative measure of traditional gender norms derived from responses in the European Values Study
(EVS). Following prior literature (e.g., Ke (2018)), we proxy traditional gender norms by the proportion of
respondents in each country, across all survey waves, who agree with the statement, “When jobs are scarce,
men should have more right to a job than women.” We then compute the cross-country average share of
agreement and classify countries as egalitarian if their national share falls below this average and as tradi-
tional if it exceeds it. This approach enables us to include citizens’ expressed attitudes and cultural values,
which may serve as a more direct proxy for cultural gender norms and underlying gender norms than an

                                          33
index based solely on observable structural indicators, such as labour market participation.
   As shown in Table 20, several countries shift classification under the alternative measure. Specifically,
Estonia, Croatia, Hungary, and Latvia are reclassified from traditional to egalitarian, while Austria and
Belgium shift from egalitarian to traditional. For detailed information on each country’s responses to the
EVS, refer to Fig. B.1(b) in the Appendix, which maps the proportion of agreement by country.

VI. Conclusion

This study offers a comprehensive examination of gendered delegation in household financial decision-
making across 23 Eurozone countries. Using a two-stage utility maximisation framework, we reconceptu-
alise financial headship as a forward-looking choice: one shaped by each spouse’s financial potential and
constrained by the disutility of departing from prevailing gender norms.
   Empirically, we identify three key findings. First, despite a rise in factors such as female education
and labour market participation, the share of female financial heads has declined over the past decade –
especially in countries with more traditional gender norms – suggesting that informal social constraints
continue to limit women’s financial authority within households. Second, while female headship has no
aggregate effect on financial market participation or overall returns, it is strongly associated with differences
in portfolio allocation and asset-specific performance. For instance, female-headed households are more
likely to invest in mutual funds and less in other fixed income securities, and notably, they earn higher
returns on mutual fund investments. Third, normative context plays a vital role. In egalitarian countries,
headship more closely reflects financial ability, while in traditional contexts, strong norm penalties dilute
the role of financial ability in delegation. These findings reinforce our model’s central prediction: gender
norms distort merit-based delegation, with possible financial consequences for household portfolios.
   To assess robustness, we re-test our results using an alternative measure of gender norms based on
attitudinal data from the European Values Study (EVS). While investment allocation results vary slightly, our
main findings on headship drivers and asset-specific returns remain consistent. This supports the validity
of our structural classification based on the Gender Equality Index (GEI), while highlighting conceptual
differences between institutional and attitudinal measures of gender norms. We acknowledge that further
research may be necessary to develop an index that captures both structural indicators of gender equality as
well as cultural attitudes and beliefs regarding gender norms.
    Together, these findings contribute to existing household finance literature by offering a new lens on intra-
household finance. First, they challenge models that assume pooled decision-making or income dominance.
Second, they highlight the relevance of differences in gendered investment behaviour. Third, by linking
normative contexts to financial agency and outcomes, this study provides a foundation for how cultural and
institutional factors shape household financial behaviour.
   Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, due to the absence of asset-class specific income
data in the HFCS, we construct a return proxy that distributes total financial income across assets in pro-
portion to their portfolio weight. While this approach offers a tractable solution, it cannot capture the true
performance of individual assets and may understate return volatility or skew. Returns are inherently more
complex and dynamic than our proxy allows. Second, our measure of financial literacy is based on four
standardised questions, and we define “basic” and “advanced” literacy based on the number of correct re-
sponses. We recognise that this approach cannot fully account for the multidimensional nature of financial
understanding. Moreover, financial literacy data is only available for three countries (Luxembourg, Greece,

                                          36
and Slovakia), reducing our estimation sample to under 5,000 households and thereby limiting cross-country
generalisability. Lastly, our analysis of risk preferences relies on self-reported attitudes toward financial risk,
which, while commonly used in household finance research, remain difficult to quantify. These measures
may be subject to lack of introspective accuracy or inconsistencies between stated and revealed preferences.
Additionally, risk tolerance questions are missing for several countries (Malta, the Czech Republic, and
Ireland), reducing our sample for these regressions to approximately 90,000 households. Despite these lim-
itations, the consistency of our core findings reinforces the study’s empirical contribution and invites further
research to inform policy efforts aimed at promoting equitable financial decision-making within households
and addressing the broader impact of gender norms on economic outcomes.

Long-run Performance of Market-timing
         Initial Public Offerings

           ANDREAS JALLINDER AND PATRIK ESSUNGER

                                    Abstract

         In this paper we examine the long-term performance of firms that conduct their
         IPO in periods of high investor sentiment. We use different measures of returns and
         conclude that the value-weighted risk-adjusted buy-and-hold return is the most
         appropriate measure to use. We find that there is significant underperformance of
          the so called market-timing firms. There is also evidence supporting that market-
         timing firms are more short-term oriented than firms conducting their IPO in other
         periods. Our results are robust after controlling for explanatory variables and after
        controlling for alternative measures of return. We also discuss the possible
        implications of our research within the framework of financial and economic
           theory.

                    I. INTRODUCTION

The cyclicality of the Initial Public Offering (IPO) market is an irrefutable fact. Early research
on the topic (Ibbotson and Jaffe, 1975; Ibbotson, Sindelar and Ritter, 1988; 1994; Ritter,
1984) sparked a large academic and public interest in this seemingly strange phenomenon.
What has come to be called “hot issue markets” is the observed spike in IPO activity
following periods of high returns in both the stock market and in other new issues. Several
different theories have been proposed in response to as why this occurs. The most common
one, springing from fact that IPO activity seems to follow rising markets, is the business
condition hypothesis. This school of thought argues that firms, in order to receive acceptable
valuations for their equity, want to time their IPO with favorable market conditions. By
timing the IPO with market factors such as optimistic expected returns and high expected
aggregate profitability, the issuing companies can benefit as the valuation of their offering
increases (Pástor and Veronesi, 2005).
    Another common explanation, with similarities to the business condition hypothesis,
emphasizes the important role of investors’ sentiment. During some periods (often coinciding
with periods of favorable market conditions), investors become irrationally excited about new
investment opportunities. This irrational exuberance causes mispricing in the equity market
and a higher demand for new equity issues with ensuing high initial returns (Loughran and
Ritter, 1995). The cost of financing the company by equity decreases until it eventually is
cheaper than debt financing. At this point the IPO activity will escalate into a hot issue
market. The classic explanation for why companies go public states that they need new funds
in order to finance investments and future growth. While this certainly is true, there are
undoubtedly other factors in play in regards to determining the timing of the IPO. Some
research has pointed towards the use of IPOs as a tool for an eventual sale of the company by
the initial owners (Rydqvist and Högholm, 1995; Zingales, 1995).
    The purpose of our paper is to look at performance differences between periods of high
and low-to-medium investor sentiment. This is interesting as it might help to bridge gaps
between the research areas of market-timing, financing decision and long-term performance
of IPOs. Our hypothesis suggests that issuing firms that time the markets with their IPO have
short-term motives for using equity financing. Consequently, these firms should have worse
subsequent long-term performance compared to non-market-timers. The hypothesis is based
on the argument stating that if owners use IPOs as an instrument for liquidating their
investment, and thus putting private needs foremost, the mere existence of hot issue markets

                              Long-run Performance of market-timing IPOs

indicates that there are certain beneficial aspects for owners in going public during those
periods. One of these aspects is most likely the fact that there is a higher possibility to receive
the most money for the shares in periods of high investor sentiment. This implies that the
firms going public in other periods of investor sentiment have different motives for doing so;
otherwise they too would have waited for more favorable conditions. There are other apparent
ulterior motives for going public in hot issue markets, such as personal wealth-maximization,
that can be characterized as short-term behavior as it does nothing to ensure improved
performance of the firm in the foreseeable future.
    We test our hypothesis by regressing risk-adjusted return at a four-year horizon on a
dummy variable for high investor sentiment peaks (the top quintile of the investor sentiment
index). The results show a significant larger underperformance by market-timers. The initial
results are robust after controlling for other contributing factors to the additional
underperformance. Furthermore, the results also indicate a positive relationship between R&D
intensity and long-term performance. As expected, improvements in profitability, in our case
measured as return on equity, have a strong positive relationship with the performance.
Despite the strong control variables, market-timing appears to be a strong determinant for
explaining the underperformance of IPOs. We go on to test our results with both buy-and-hold
and cumulative return measures, calculated respectively with value-weighted and equal-
weighted market returns. In total we test ten different return measures. The results show that
the market-timing firms contribute negatively to the four-year performance for all return
measures. In the robustness test, nine out of ten return measures are significant at a 5%
significance level.
    The results indicate that firms that conduct IPOs in hot issue markets periods with high
investor sentiment have less long-term incentives in comparison to firms conducting their IPO
in other periods.

                 II. PREVIOUS RESEARCH

A. Market Timing

The market for IPOs shows a highly cyclical behavior. Research by Ibbotson and Jaffe
(1975), Ibbotson, Sindelar and Ritter (1988, 1994), and Ritter (1984) marked the beginning of
a large academic and public interest in the subject of hot issue markets. These hot issue
markets are characterized by periods of rising stock markets and high initial returns of IPOs

                              Long-run Performance of market-timing IPOs

followed by a spike in the number of IPOs, clustered in relatively few months. Several
theories have emerged in order try to explain why there is cyclicality in the IPO market.
    The business condition hypothesis argues that number of equity issuances reflect the
prevailing economic conditions and would thus be positively correlated with measurements of
business activity. In favorable market conditions, firms are able to issue equity to more
attractive prices. In the context of IPOs, Pástor and Veronesi (2005) introduced a narrowed
down definition of business condition. It includes the time-varying expected market returns,
the expected aggregate profitability and the prior uncertainty about average post-IPO
profitability. Their model predicts that firms go public when equity prices are high, expected
aggregate profitability is high and prior uncertainty is high. According to their findings, many
firms choose to wait for market conditions to improve so that their decision for going public
can be justified by a high price for their equity. These statistical results are supported by a
qualitative study made by Brau and Fawcett (2006), who interviewed 336 CFOs who had
been involved in planning IPOs. According to the majority of the CFOs, they based the timing
of the IPOs on the overall market conditions.
    An alternate explanation for the IPO cyclicality is the investor sentiment theory. During
some periods, investors become abnormally excited about new investment opportunities. The
demand for new equity issues rises and the marginal cost of obtaining new funds decreases
(Loughran, Ritter and Rydqvist, 1994; Lerner, 1994; Loughran and Ritter,
1995). Consequently, by taking advantage of the so called “window of opportunity”, firms’
previously unattractive projects suddenly become lucrative as the investor optimism
intensifies (Ritter, 1991).
    Another explanation is one depending on the costs associated with adverse selection. In
periods when asymmetric information is high, investors require larger price discounts on new
equity issues. For example, during bad macroeconomic conditions it is often difficult for an
investor to identify the risks associated with a specific company. Due to uncertainty of current
and future asset values the demand for larger price discount in new issues increases (Choe,
Masulis and Nanda, 1993).
    These differing theories provide different explanations of the underlying reasons for the
cyclicality in IPO activity. Ivanov and Lewis (2008) explore the impact of these three
explanations. They find that the most significant determinants of the IPO cycle is business
condition and investor sentiment, while adverse selection costs have no significant effect on
IPO activity. Lowry (2003) found similar results with the addition of firms’ demand for
capital as another important determinant. Even though the previously mentioned theories have

                              Long-run Performance of market-timing IPOs

different approaches, they expectedly lead to similar conclusions. The consequence from all
of the aforementioned theories is the clustering of IPOs around the period of a rising stock
market. When the stock market is rising we find favorable business conditions, high investor
sentiment and a relatively low uncertainty regarding the condition of issuing firms. Drawing
upon these conclusions, Colak and Günay (2011) developed a model for strategic waiting in
the IPO market. In their study they find evidence that firms, which are about to go public,
benefits from delaying the IPO process in order to obtain more information about the current
economic condition. The existence of a sharp decline in IPO activity during unfavorable
market conditions supports this theory. It indicates that firms do not solely base the decision
to go public on their own fundamentals, but rather on the aggregate state of the economy.
    Going public during an economic slowdown is costlier since the real or supposed risk of a
failed IPO is higher. This is why the extra information from recent IPOs is of great value: to
maximize the probability of a successful IPO (or minimize the probability of a failure). The
market participants cannot know the exact result of an IPO at any given time; instead they
rely on information gathered through other IPOs. This free-riding nature of information
gathering may cause extended periods of inactivity even as the economy starts to recover. It
also implies a quick increase in activity when a couple of successful IPOs have led the way
(Lowry and Schwert, 2002; Alti, 2005).

B. Reasons for Going Public

The classic explanation for going public is the company’s demand for new capital in order to
finance future growth and investments. Several papers challenge this view. It would also be
odd that there is a rise in IPO activity during economic expansions, when in fact it is during
periods of bad economic conditions that companies are financially constrained and would
need additional funds to continue expanding. Rydqvist and Högholm (1995) provide an
alternate explanation as to why companies go public. They find that companies are taken
public mainly due to the owners’ desire to liquidate their investments. With this explanation,
it makes perfect sense for IPOs to cluster around market peaks since that is when the proceeds
to the initial owners are the greatest. This strategy is supported by Zingales (1995) who argues
that the choice whether or not to go public is made in order to maximize the proceeds from an
eventual sale by the initial owners. Further evidence of personal wealth-maximization can be
found in a study conducted by Ang and Brau (2003). They find evidence of two different
concealment strategies consistent with personal wealth-maximization. Insiders tend to

                              Long-run Performance of market-timing IPOs

underreport the number of personally owned shared in the original prospectus, and later
correcting the numbers in obscure amendments. The number of secondary shares offered is
also being increased in later amendments as to avoid sending any obvious negative signals
regarding the IPO. On a related note are the findings by Teoh, Welch and Wong (1998),
explaining that IPO firms report higher earnings in comparison to their cash flow around the
date of the IPO compared to non-issuers. Such behavior indicates a short-term focus. The
earnings are inflated in unsustainable ways due to an over-emphasis on maximizing the short-
term gains from the IPO.
    According to the previously mentioned strategic waiting model (Colak and Günay, 2011),
every firm benefits by delaying their IPO. If that is the case, why would there be any IPOs at
all? It resembles a game of chicken, where all firms delay their IPO until the point where
some firms desperately need the new funds provided by an equity offering. According to the
reasoning in the strategic waiting model, these should be the firms conducting the IPO
relatively early in the IPO cycle. Also, these firms assumedly conduct the IPO solely to
finance their growing business and future investments. In contrast, when conducting an IPO
during a hot issue market, we would expect that at least some firms do not desperately need
new funds but instead justify their IPO by the prevailing high equity valuations. Since firms
going public during a cold market cycle do it for the sake of funding their future expansion it
is also safe to assume that there is a thorough investment plan for the usage of the new capital.
The amount of equity that is raised is also on average lower, further increasing the efficiency
of the equity employed. Although supposedly not all firms that go public during the
expanding phase of the IPO cycle do so for the sake of receiving a larger amount of cash,
some will. Consequently, these latter firms raise money with a less carefully planned usage of
the new capital. The reported equity would also be larger at higher valuations, causing even
lower equity returns. As a result, we would expect to see worse long-run performance of firms
going public in the hot IPO markets. Consistent with these claims are the findings of
Chemmanuer and He (2011), who provide empirical evidence that firms going public later in
the IPO cycle have lower productivity and profitability but larger cash holdings compared to
those who perform the IPO earlier in the cycle. There is further supporting evidence from
Yung, Colak and Wang (2008) concluding that due to opportunistic issuance of low quality
firms during hot market conditions, the cross-sectional return variance is greater and there is a
higher incidence of delisting for firms going public during this stage of the cycle. On the other
hand, the model developed by Colak and Günay (2011) predicts that there is a higher
probability of low-quality firms being among the first to go public.

C. Long-run Underperformance of IPOs

Over a period of one to five years, newly issued stocks have been found to underperform
compared to non-issuing firms. Loughran and Ritter (1995) found an average yearly
underperformance of 7 percent compared to non-issuing firms with approximately the same
market capitalization. Ritter (1991) showed a mean underperformance over three years of 27
percent (median of 55 percent) for new issues relative to stocks matched on size and industry.
    Certain accounting policies and measures have been proven to influence the long-term
performance of IPOs. Accounting policies stated in the GAAP (Generally Accepted
Accounting Principles) give companies some discretion over how to report earnings. As a
result, companies can choose to report earnings that are far higher than the actual cash flow.
Teoh, Welch and Wong (1998) reports that companies that are aggressively reporting earnings
in excess of cash flow underperform by 20 percent compared to companies with more
conservative policies. Another aspect that has been under scrutiny is the expenditures for
research and development (R&D). Guo, Lev and Shi (2006) found a positive relationship
between R&D intensity (both R&D as a fraction of sales and as a fraction of expected market
value after the IPO) and long-term performance. Jain and Kini (2008) found no consistent
relationship between R&D expenditures and changes in operating performance. They did
however find that managerial commitment to R&D and developing new product lines
improves the IPO firms’ chances of remaining viable for longer periods of time.

joined by monthly stock data from CRSP, as well as quarterly company data from
CRSP/COMPUSTAT Merged. All observations that only contain CRSP data in less than one
year are dropped. Due to the mismatch between the monthly and quarterly data, we compose

data for many of the observations, and consequently we have assumed these R&D values to
be zero as is done in prior research. Chauvin and Hirschey (1993) empirically test the
suitability of this assumption and conclude that it is generally appropriate to set missing R&D
expenditure values to zero. After this, we drop all observations that have missing CRSP or
CRSP/COMPUSTAT Merged data. This creates a final sample of 7156 IPOs. The final
dataset appears to be unbiased in regards to years. As seen in Table I in the results section, the
dropped observations are more or less equally spread out over the entire range of years. There
could however be a slight quality bias. It is not clear whether the firms with missing data in
CRSP or CRSP/COMPUSTAT Merged are of lower quality than those with complete data.

B. Variables for Investor Sentiment

The variable we use to measure investor sentiment is created by Baker and Wurgler (2006)
and is available from Wurgler’s webpage (Wurgler, 2011). This variable is a composite index
of the common variation of six underlying proxies for sentiment. These six proxies are the
closed-end fund discount, NYSE share turnover, the number and average first-day returns on
IPOs, the equity share in new issues, and the dividend premium. The composite index is also
constructed in such a way that it excludes all business cycle specific variation.
    Our paper will focus on a comparison between firms who choose to go public in different
periods in the IPO cycle. In line with the investor sentiment hypothesis and the strategic
waiting hypothesis, we define a hot issue market as when the investor sentiment is in the
highest quintile. We construct a dummy variable for investor sentiment that is one if the
company went public during a month when investor sentiment was in the highest quintile, and
zero otherwise.

C. Return Variables

We use several different measures of returns in order to adjust for risk and control for the
robustness of our results. For all of our return variables we calculate the buy-and-hold return
as well as the cumulative return. By using both methods, we can account for large early

                                         7
                              Long-run Performance of market-timing IPOs

changes in stock price, which would otherwise get an un-proportional influence over longer-
term returns. In order to correct for the usual abnormal initial returns of IPOs, we skip one
month before calculating the returns.
    In addition to using basic raw returns, we construct a market-adjusted return variable.
This variable is simply the return of a stock in a specific month less the return of the market
portfolio for that specific month. To adjust for certain risk characteristics we use a method
where we match the IPO firms with non-issuing firms of similar risk character. We use data
provided by Kenneth R. French on his webpage (French, 2014). The firms are divided into
two portfolios based on size, and three portfolios based on book-to-market ratios. In total,
when intersecting the different portfolios, we get six portfolios, which is consistent with the
division of Fama and French (1993). The monthly returns of the firms are matched by the
corresponding monthly returns of their portfolio, the monthly risk-free rate and the monthly
market risk premium. To further control for robustness in our regressions we construct the
market-adjusted and the risk-adjusted returns based on both value-weighted and equal-
weighted returns.
    Finally we calculate the event-time returns based on the previously calculated monthly
returns. These returns are relative to the IPO event with, as previously mentioned, one-month
lag. Thus, what we describe as the five-year return is the return from approximately one
month to 61 months after going public (depending on when during the month the IPO
happened).

D. Control Variables

In order to check our main regression for robustness we use several other variables to control
for alternative causes of variation. These alternative variations could come from stock
characteristics, accounting performance, or both. To control for stock characteristics, we
construct two variables that have been extensively used for this purpose: market capitalization
and the book-to-market ratio, which is based on the market value and book equity at the time
of the IPO. We also include the age of the company when it goes public. In addition to
controlling for certain stock characteristics we include two accounting measures that have
been found to influence the long-term performance of IPOs. The first such variable is change
in profitability, which we measure as the difference in logarithmic return on equity plus one
between the end-date of the event-time horizon and date of the IPO. This variable is
constructed in a similar fashion as the construction by Pástor, Taylor and Veronesi (2009).

The second accounting variable measure is R&D and is defined as R&D expenditures at the
time of the IPO divided by book asset value at the time of the IPO. This ratio has been shown
to influence the performance of IPOs by Jain and Kini (2008). In aggregate, these control
variables should explain some of the variance not related to market timing.

E. Methodology

We begin by determining a suitable event-time horizon where it is possible to see significant
differences in returns between periods of different levels of investor sentiment. This is done
by simple linear regressions of different measures of return on our dummy variable indicating
high levels of investor sentiment at the time of the IPO. The regression is run using different
horizons from the day the company went public, ranging from one year to five years.
    In the next step we check the robustness in our results by including additional variables
that have been found to influence the returns of IPOs. By adding the new variables in multiple
stages, and by including a correlation matrix, we hope to convince the readers of the lack of
multicollinearity.
    Finally, we run the same regression as above but with different measures of return. This
way we can assess whether or not our results are dependent on the choice of return
measurement. In all of our regression we cluster the standard errors by month of IPO. We
choose 5 percent as the lowest acceptable significance level.

                           IV. RESULTS
The objective of the paper is to examine whether there are any differences in long-term
returns between firms that are timing their IPO with high sentiment periods and firms that are
conducting their IPO in periods of lower sentiment. In order to do this, we need appropriate
measures to study the long-term performance for the two groups. Thus, we use raw return,
market-adjusted returns, and risk-adjusted returns for every year up to five years after the
IPO. In addition to our computed buy-and-hold returns, and in alignment with critique from
Fama (1998), we also compute cumulative returns. For each of the return measurements
(except for the raw return), we adjust by both value-weighted market return and equal-
weighted market return. In most of our cross-sectional regressions we only use the value-
weighted buy-and-hold return since it is the most relevant for investors. All of our tests show
that the market-timing firms have negative long-term performances.

B. Cross-sectional Regressions over Different event-time Horizons

In order to examine whether going public during a peak in investor sentiment have any
significant effect on the future returns, we regress the buy-and-hold returns of different time
horizons on our sentiment dummy variable (see Panel A, B and C in Table III). We find a
relatively large negative sentiment dummy coefficient when regressing on the raw returns.
This is to be expected since the investor sentiment peaks usually corresponds to stock market
peaks. When the market soon reverts back towards the mean, the raw returns would be
considerably lower for the IPOs conducted close to this event.
    Of more interest are the results from the regressions on market-adjusted return and risk-
adjusted return. All of the sentiment dummy coefficients are negative with varying levels of
significance. This result is consistent with our hypothesis stating that companies trying to
exploit market mispricing in aggregate have more short-term reasons for going public, which
translates into lower returns over a longer time period. It should also be noted that all
intercepts are significantly negative, meaning that all IPOs on average underperform relative
to the benchmark.
    When examining the risk-adjusted returns in Panel C, it is apparent that the IPOs during
hot issue markets (market-timers) underperform compared to IPOs during colder issue
markets. The underperformance of these issues, compared to the already underperforming
total IPO market (in parentheses) is 13% (21%) in one year, 13.7% (36.6%) in two years,
8.08% (48.9%) in three years, 10.2% (57.8%) in four years, and 5.74% (66.3%) in five years.
    These results are however not complete and needs to be checked for other contributing
factors. Instead, these results are used as a basis to determine over which time period there is a
significant difference in return between high sentiment IPOs and IPOs during colder periods.
As seen in Table III, there is a lower significance in both the market- and risk-adjusted five-
year return. In order to ensure that potential short-termism will be reflected in the stock price,
we choose to focus the following parts of the paper on the longest possible horizon with
overwhelmingly significant results. Considering the lower significance over the five-year
horizon combined with Ibbotson’s (1975) findings of no underperformance in the fifth year
after going public, we decide that a four-year horizon is the most adequate horizon for further
research (see Panel D in Table III).

C. Cross-sectional regressions over a four-year event-time horizon with controlling variables

As seen in the previous section, there is a significant underperformance by companies going
public during hot issue markets, when sentiment is high, compared to companies that go
public during colder issue markets. It is difficult to ascertain whether this proportionally
higher underperformance is only due to the timing of the hot issue market, or if there are other
factors better explaining the increased underperformance. In order to test this we add the
additional control variables to our regression.
    The result from the robustness test is found in Table IV. The underperformance
contributable to market timing is still significantly negative. Over a four-year period, starting
when going public, the market-timers underperform relative to the risk-adjusted benchmark
by 8.95%. This is a slightly lower underperformance than before we added the control
variables, which were 10.2% (see Panel D in Table III). The control variables thus explains
1.25% of the underperformance that earlier only was contributed by the sentiment dummy.
Still, the underperformance of market-timers is large and appears to be an important
determinant in explaining some of the variation in the long-term returns of IPOs.
    The coefficient for the book-to-market variable is positive and significant. This
relationship was expected considering that high book-to-market stocks, also called value
stocks, have historically outperformed stocks with lower book-to-market ratio (growth
stocks). Our results show that companies with a higher book-to-market ratio at the time of the
IPO tend to perform better in the following four years. Quite surprisingly, the market
capitalization coefficient is positive. It is the opposite of what is known as the small firm
effect, which is based on the historical findings stating that smaller firms tend to outperform
larger ones. Our results present a different relationship in the context of IPOs. We propose
some possible explanations in accordance with our results: (i) Larger firms are less risky, with
subsequent lower volatility. Since the IPO market is characterized by declining trends, the
underperformance should be lower for larger firms. On the other hand, if the IPO market
would theoretically outperform the benchmark, we would expect lower positive returns for
large firms, and thus, a negative coefficient. (ii) Larger firms attract more analyst coverage.
The increased coverage contributes to pricing the new issue more fairly. Future returns would
be less volatile and the coefficient for market capitalization would be positive.

                            V. Conclusion
In this paper we examine whether firms that conduct their IPO during investor sentiment
peaks underperform in the long run. We study different horizons of raw, market-adjusted and
risk-adjusted returns and conclude that the four-year risk-adjusted return is the most
appropriate measure to use in our tests. We find that there are significant results for long-term
underperformance among firms that issue their IPO during sentiment peaks. This result is
robust after controlling for several explanatory variables proven in previous research to have a
significant influence on IPO performance. Furthermore, we find that the market-timing firms
have a considerably lower mean value of R&D expenditures relative to assets compared to
other firms. When testing our regression with this variable in our robustness check, we find
that it is positive for regressions including all control variables and significant at a 5%
significance level. In alignment with this relationship, together with the findings of negative
correlation between the market timing group and the R&D-asset ratio, we identify an
indication insinuating that the market-timing firms’ are more short-term oriented than other
firms.
    Our research ties together three different areas of previous research: one being the
research that challenges the view of long-term incentives as the reason for going public,
another one being the research finding a tendency among firms to delay the time of the IPO to
times with more favorable market conditions, and finally, the third one being the research on
long-term performance of IPOs. Our findings should have implications on investors’
decisions since the market-timing firms have long-term underperformance and seem to care
more about the instant cash flow rather than the sustained well-being of the firm. As the
market intensifies along with the investor sentiment, there should be a point in time where it is
no longer lucrative for investors to participate in IPOs. This point in time should be before the
sentiment reaches its’ peak, and in a window where investors maximizes the profit of their
investment. This reasoning raises several interesting questions. If investors were able to
identify and time this window, how would this affect the market-timing of IPOs? Would the
different periods of investor sentiment be characterized by a more evenly spaced distribution
of IPOs? As this “investor window of opportunity” to a large degree would impact on
financial and economic theory, we suggest for future research in this area to try to identify this
window. As an alternative it would be interesting, considering our findings, to find out where
in the market cycle there is IPO activity initiated by long-term incentives. Finally, we suggest
additional research supporting our theory on market-timing short-term firms.

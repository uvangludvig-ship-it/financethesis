CSR Under Pressure:  An Analysis of CSR and Stock Returns in Europe during
COVID-19
Abstract:

This paper investigates whether Corporate Social Responsibility shields firm value during
and after exogenous shocks, using the COVID-19 crisis as a case study.  The  main focus
is  whether firms with higher CSR scores experienced better stock performance during the
crisis and the  following  recovery period.  The study analyzes a sample of 610  public
European companies, applying  regressions on raw and market-adjusted stock returns for
                            th                 th                                          th             rd
the crisis (February 19  to March 25 , 2020) and post-crisis (March 25  to June 3 , 2020)
periods.  The analysis includes control variables for firm  financials,  Fama-French factor
loadings  and industry  fixed  effects.  During the crisis, CSR is positively associated with
adjusted returns, but the effect disappears once controls are introduced. In the post-crisis
period, CSR is negatively and significantly associated with both raw and adjusted returns.
Idiosyncratic risk  shows a strong positive relationship with post-crisis returns, indicating
that firms with higher risk profiles outperformed in the recovery phase.

Keywords:
CSR,  COVID-19,  Stock  Return,  ESG,  Exogenous  Crisis

1.        Introduction

Financial markets are inherently volatile, with crises periodically testing the resilience of
firms and their investors. Recent global events, such as the COVID-19 pandemic, have
intensified discussions on how companies can mitigate risk and maintain stability in times
of economic distress. One area that has gained increasing attention is the Corporate Social
Responsibility  (CSR)  investing,  which  promotes  sustainable  and  ethical  business
practices,  first  introduced  by  Howard  R.  Bowen  in  1953  in  the  book  “Social
responsibilities of the businessman”. This paper explores how CSR commitments
influence stock price behavior during crises, examining whether firms with strong CSR
profiles exhibit greater resilience and  recovery during and after an exogenous shock.
     The coronavirus disease 2019, also known as COVID-19, was a  pandemic  where
the first case was detected in December 2019 in China.  Since  the start of the pandemic
more than 2 million people  died in Europe as a result of the disease  according to  World
Health Organization  (n.d.). This pandemic led to a financial crisis erasing 35% of the
                                                          th                        rd
equity value in the euro area between the peak  19  of  February  and  23  of  March,  2020
according to  Ampudia,  Baumann  and Fornari (2020).
     The relevance of corporate social responsibility (CSR) in financial markets has
grown  evidenced by  a  growing body of research  which  highlights how investor
preferences have shifted in favor of socially responsible firms and the importance of CSR
metrics. For instance, Hartzmark and Sussman (2019) provide empirical evidence that
investors allocate more capital to sustainable  mutual funds. This heightened emphasis on
CSR makes it a crucial area of study, particularly in the context of evaluating its impact
on firm performance and risk. The topic is  relevant  in the European context,  as it houses
countries at the forefront of sustainable  company practices. Notably,  Scandinavian
countries dominate ESG rankings, with four nations in the region occupying the top
global positions for ESG performance according to Statista (2024). This underscores the
importance of examining CSR within European markets, where investor expectations and
institutional pressures may amplify its financial and reputational consequences.
      The previous research on how CSR affects firms both during crises and in general
has been done extensively as shown in papers such as  Bae, El Ghoul, Gong, and
Guedhami  (2021), Utz (2017), and Albuquerque,  Koskinen  and Zhang  (2019) among
others. Focus has been put on the 2008 financial crisis by Lins, Servaes, and Tamayo
(2017) and Ding, Levine, Lin,  and Xie  (2021) along with Bae et  al.  (2021)  who  have
researched and compared it to the more exogenous impact of Covid-19. These studies,
excluding Utz (2017), have arrived at different conclusions about CSRs effect on stock
performance during and after crises.
      The research gap that we identify is that no study akin to  Bae  et. al  (2021)  have
been done with Europe as the scope of the research. Utz (2017) has European companies
in his study however,  only  looking  at CSRs impact on stock synchronicity and crash risk.
He finds that high CSR score for European companies has a mitigating effect on stock
price crash risk and is a predictor for lower idiosyncratic risk. Thus, the question arises
of whether a high CSR score has a mitigating effect on stock price during a crisis and if
it can explain stock price recovery during the subsequent period. The findings of Utz
(2017) spark the hypothesis that CSR should have a mitigating effect during and after
crisis as it is found to have a mitigating effect on the stock price crash risk.  The
contribution this paper aims to add is an extension of Bae et al. (2021), which is done by

analysis due to our divided full sample. Further challenges may lie in the fact that omitted
variables such as managerial ability or other  factors correlated to CSR may  influence  the
result  instead  of CSR  itself. A  good manager may  be better equipped to handle a crisis
and may also invest more in CSR practices, thus if impact of CSR is found, it may be due
to factors outside of our studies.
      When we look at CSRs effect on stock returns during the crisis, we  first  find  a
significantly positive effect on the market-model adjusted returns, but that effect is lost
when incorporating our control variables in the regression.  Stock performance is more
affected by size, long term debt and idiosyncratic risk during the crisis where size is the
only one with a positive effect, the others having a negative impact on the returns.  When
moving our scope from the crisis period to the post-crisis period we  then  find  that higher
CSR has a significantly negative effect  on stock performance during the post-crisis
period. This  may give some evidence that CSR  scores  are not valued by investors after a
big exogenous crash  but one should be careful drawing to big conclusions due to the
limited sample size and the  effect of control variables.  We find that  variables such as
idiosyncratic risk  and  profitability has a significantly positive effect where  idiosyncratic
risks  have  the biggest effect.  The inclusion of the controls  lowers the negative effect of
CSR from -0,23 to -0,10 and -0,27 to -0,14 for the raw and market-model adjusted returns
respectively.
      We later look at the different industries to see if  there are significant  differences
between different economic sectors. We find that all sectors were affected negatively with
the  mean  raw  crisis return being between  -0,35 to  -0,19 and the mean  raw  post crisis
return for each  sector  being  in the span of  0,13 to 0,31. We did  individual regressions for
each industry as well and found no significant results  for each industry in the market-
model adjusted  crisis return. For the adjusted  post crisis return,  the Real Estate and
Consumer Non-cyclicals show negative returns, significant at the 5 and 10%  level

                                   3
respectively, but due to the limited sample  for the industries (N= 39 & 48)  one should not
draw big conclusions about the results  regarding  CSRs effect on  these individual
industries.
     To assess the robustness of our findings, we constructed an alternative CSR
measure  by equally averaging the Environmental, Social, and Governance pillar scores.
This  new  measure  offers a  balanced view of CSR performance, allowing us to test
whether our results are sensitive to the construction of the CSR variable. When re-
estimating our main regressions using  the new  CSR variable, we find that the magnitude
of the coefficients is generally  weakened. This suggests that the way CSR is measured
can  influence empirical results. The discrepancy highlights a broader challenge in CSR
research,  the lack of standardization in how CSR  ratings  are constructed  and interpreted.
While the direction of the relationship between CSR and stock returns remains broadly
consistent, the sensitivity of effect sizes underscores the need for caution when drawing
conclusions.  Further  differences may exist between rating institutes which is something
one should be aware of  when analyzing CSR  scores.
       Extensive research has been done on the role of ESG and CSR within a company,
both  during  normal and crisis  market conditions. In order for us to effectively analyze the
impact of the social capital on firms' stock performance during crises, we have analyzed
the previous research on the topic. Our study aims to further  investigate  into these effects
in public companies in Europe  more specifically during  the exogenous shock, COVID-
19.
      This paper primarily relates to the literature on the effects of firms' Corporate Social
Responsibility (CSR) during financial crises. Prior studies, such as Lins et al. (2017) and
Bae et al. (2021), have explored whether CSR provides resilience during times of market
distress. Lins et al. (2017)  investigates  the role of social capital during the 2008 financial
crisis by analyzing CSR performance in relation to firm stock returns before, during, and
after the crisis. They find that companies with higher  CSR scores exhibit  better  stock
performance during the crisis, particularly when market trust is low.  In contrast, Bae et
al. (2021)  analyzes  the effects of CSR on firm returns during the COVID-19 crisis using
U.S. company data and two different CSR scores (MSCI and Refinitiv). Their findings
indicate no significant relationship between CSR scores and firm performance during or
after the crisis, even after controlling for market risk factors, firm characteristics, and
industry effects. The authors suggest that this  nonexistent relationship  may stem from
CSR scores not adequately capturing meaningful CSR activity at the firm level. We
extend this literature by demonstrating how CSR relates to firm stock performance in the
European context during the COVID-19 crisis, using Refinitiv CSR data and applying the
empirical framework of Bae et al. (2021).
      Our paper also contributes to the growing body of research on the economic effects
of the COVID-19 pandemic. While existing research, such as Albuquerque et al. (2020),
focuses on how environmental and social (ES) performance influenced U.S. firms’
resilience during the COVID-19 market crash, our study adds to this by analyzing whether
similar effects hold in a European setting. Albuquerque et al. (2020)  find that higher ES
scores are associated with significantly better stock returns, lower volatility, and higher
operating profit margins during the first quarter of 2020. They attribute these outcomes
to customer and investor loyalty and suggest that  loyalty enhances firm resilience during
exogenous shocks.  Additionally, our paper is  influenced  by the theoretical framework
proposed in Albuquerque et al. (2019), which links CSR to reduced systematic risk. Their
study demonstrates that CSR can be used as a product differentiation strategy, allowing

                                   4
firms   to   command   higher   margins   and   ultimately   lowering   their   exposure   to
macroeconomic downturns. We build on this theoretical foundation to interpret our
findings on the performance of high  CSR firms during the COVID-19 shock in Europe.
      Further literature that our paper relates to are Utz (2017). The paper investigates the
relationship between CSR and firm  risk in  different  markets,  particularly through the lens
of crash risk and stock price volatility.  The  paper finds  that companies with higher CSR
scores exhibit lower crash risk and idiosyncratic volatility  in Europe and the U.S. This
suggests that CSR may serve a protective role by reducing extreme negative return events,
consistent with the idea that responsible corporate practices foster investor trust and long-
term stability. Additionally, our work relates to the literature on ESG rating disagreement.
Berg,  Kölbel,  and  Rigobon  (2022)  highlight  the  significant  divergence  in  ESG
assessments across rating agencies, raising concerns about measurement reliability and
the comparability of CSR effects across studies. These discrepancies can introduce noise
or bias into empirical findings, particularly when comparing results across data providers.
In our  study  we  use the  data source,  Refinitiv Eikon,  and  acknowledge  the limitations
inherent in ESG data construction. By including a robustness test that incorporates the
Governance dimension separately, we further explore how CSR measurement decisions
influence observed return patterns, particularly during periods of financial stress.
     There are  conflicting results across studies examining similar measures and time
periods, which  underscores the necessity for deeper investigation into this  area.  While
Lins et al. (2017) discovered evidence that strong CSR performance positively influenced
stock returns during crisis periods, Bae et al. (2021) found no such correlation during the
COVID-19 pandemic.  This notable discrepancy  potentially  stems from  different  factors.
First, methodological variations in research design, statistical approaches, and control
variables may account for these divergent findings.  Second, as Berg  et al.  (2022)
convincingly demonstrate,  divergence exists between different  CSR rating  agencies,
attributable to measurement differences, scope discrepancies, and weight variations. This
"rating divergence" phenomenon may explain why studies using different ESG data
sources reach contradictory conclusions.  Our study addresses these gaps by examining
the relationship through a distinct geographical lens,  focusing on European public
companies,  to detect potential regional variations in how  CSR  influences firm resilience
during crises.  This approach may reveal whether the effectiveness of CSR as a risk
mitigation strategy during market turbulence exhibits meaningful  differences across
markets, potentially reconciling some of the contradictions in existing literature.
      The   remainder   of   this   paper   is   organized   as   follows.  Section   2  outlines
our  methodology and data, detailing the research design, data sources,  and  sample
construction,   including   the   formation   of   our   CSR   metrics.  Section   3  presents
the  empirical results, focusing on the regression analyses that examine the relationship
between CSR and firm stock performance during and after the COVID-19 crisis.  This
section also includes a  robustness test  using  an alternative CSR variable, to assess the
consistency of our findings. In  Section 4, we provide a comprehensive  discussion  of the
results, address potential limitations in the research design, and explore possible
directions   for  future   research.   Finally,  Section   5  offers   a   concise  conclusion,
summarizing    the    main    findings    and    contributions    of    the    paper.

3.6  Robustness

When using CSR as a measure to analyze companies, the way one values the different
components and constructs the final variable can impact the results. Data from different
rating institutes can be divergent from each other for the same company as shown by Berg
et al. (2022).  How authors construct the CSR coefficient can also impact results. As Bae
et al. (2021) and Lins et al. (2017) do we omit the governance aspect of the CSR score.
As a robustness  test,  we thus incorporate the Governance score and test the effect with
the new measure.
     Our robustness test with our new variable named CSR2 is found in  Table  (7).  To
create the new variable, we have incorporated the Governance score into the aggregate
CSR score that previously only contained an average of the environmental and social
score from Refinitiv Eikon. The governance score is now incorporated and the sum of the
three  ESG  metrics  is divided by three and then a hundred to get it in decimal form.
Incorporating the governance score gives us a fuller picture of how the company is run as
well as  including  something that investors value in accordance with the findings of
Cheung, Thomas Connelly, Limpaphayom, and Zhou (2007). The results that we get with
the new CSR2 variable  are interesting. During the crisis, we see that there is a significant
positive effect of the adjusted return  when  omitting the control variables,  although  with a
low  coefficient estimate. We also get a low, negative effect that is significant on the raw
return with control variables. The stand outs here are Log(MV) which has a significant
positive effect during the crisis, suggesting that bigger companies perform better  during
the exogenous shock. Long-term debt has a significantly negative effect according to the
data and idiosyncratic risk is highly negative when dealing with the raw crisis return. In
columns  (5)  to  (8), the post crisis returns are analyzed. We can see a highly significant
effect on all post returns except for column  (7). All coefficient estimates are however
negative and low showing little to no effect on post crisis returns for our sample. Size as
measured by Log(MV) is again significant with a small negative effect for the adjusted
returns and slightly less significantly negative for the raw post crisis returns.
      From the robustness test one can interpret that  adding the governance pillar into the
aggregate CSR score makes  the variable more significant in most columns,  showing that
while  the full CSR variable may be  significant,  the coefficient estimate  suggests  a very
small effect on stock returns.  Instead,  the size  is significant with a bigger  effect  as well,
and we also see the idiosyncratic risk  has  a big negative effect in column  on raw returns
during the crisis period,  with  -1.81  as the coefficient,  and being  highly positive and
significant in column  (7)  &  (8),  with  4.98  and 4.00  as coefficients  respectively.  As
idiosyncratic  risk  is  highly significant and  has  a large effect on stock returns during the
post-crisis period  on can  relate stronger post crisis recovery  to higher idiosyncratic risk
from these results.

4.        Discussion
Our empirical results point towards CSR having limited effect in shielding companies
stock performance from the exogenous shocks such as the COVID-19 crisis. During the
defined crisis period, CSR scores do not exhibit a significant relationship with adjusted
stock returns when controlling for standard financial variables and risk factors. This
suggests that corporate social responsibility did not serve as a buffer against the
immediate market downturn. In contrast, our results on the post crisis period imply that a
higher CSR score, instead of helping companies recover from the shock, negatively
affects stock returns. While this may have different causes, one possible explanation is
that in the aftermath of a crisis, investors may favor firms with more risk  over those
perceived as allocating resources to long term non-financial goals. While definite links to
stock performance are impossible to make, the negative effect of CSR could further imply
a shift in investor preferences during recovery periods. The strong positive and significant
effect of idiosyncratic risk on post crisis returns in  Table  (3)  supports the idea that
investors may become more tolerant of firm risk in pursuit of return opportunities after a
market dip.  As idiosyncratic risk is by far the  coefficient with the largest effect, it
indicates that it is the  variable that  predicts and impacts  stock return, especially in the post
crisis period, the most.
      The results of our research should be considered carefully as our robustness test,
altering the CSR variable, influences and weakens the effect of the coefficients. Thus,
how one constructs the CSR variable can influence the results and impact the research.
Our industry level analysis further indicates no strong effects of CSR across sectors,
though smaller subsample sizes limit conclusion ability. Notably, sectors such as
Healthcare and Consumer Non-Cyclicals display stronger average returns during the
crisis period, consistent with  healthcare’s  relevance during a pandemic and non-cyclicals
resilience towards the economy.
      The underlying mechanisms behind this are hard to explain without larger and more
comprehensive studies being done but we believe that our results could be due to investor
preferences shifting during the crisis.  While  CSR may be favored  during normal  market
conditions, the crisis may induce new behaviors for investors that alters the  strategies and
their value of different company metrics.  Thus, CSR  may  be one of the metrics that are
deemed less important during market crashes. Our post crisis results also point towards
CSR not being favored. During the post crisis period, other variables such as idiosyncratic
risk  seem to drive  stock performance instead,  which could  suggest that investors  prefer
to take on larger risks after a  big  market dip.
      Limitations for this paper may include, but  are  not limited to,  the limited sample
size of  610 European companies.  This sample size resulted from our requirement that
firms have a  market capitalization of  at least $250,000,000 as of December 2019,
combined  with  the  geographical  constraint that firms be headquartered  in  Europe.  A
larger sample could have affected the outcome of the study, either by strengthening the
results or producing different patterns, or it might have yielded similar findings. However,
a  broader  dataset  could  have  provided  more  certainty  and  clearer  conclusions.
Furthermore, there is always a risk  that  the data  used may  be skewed,  incorrect, or
misinterpreted.
      In addition,  several data  providers offer  similar  categories of  information,  for
example, both  MSCI and Refinitiv Eikon  supply  CSR scores.  If  MSCI had been used
instead  of  Refinitiv  Eikon,  the  results  could  have  differed  due  to  variations  in

                                  20
methodology. Lastly, the choice of control variables and regression techniques may also
have influenced the results. Using alternative specifications could have led to different
outcomes.  Another limitation of this paper is the time frame of the study, which focuses
specifically on the COVID-19 crisis and the following post-crisis period. This provides a
relevant and timely context, but the results may not be applicable to other types of crises,
such as financial crashes or political instability. The impact  of CSR on firm performance
could vary depending on the nature of the crisis, meaning that the conclusions drawn here
may not hold in different circumstances.
      Further research may  investigate  the issue deeper, looking into country specific
effects of CSR, especially during the COVID-19 crisis  where different country policies
affected  company performance.  Thus,  further controls for such factors may help isolate
the effect of CSR  even more.  Other things that one could  investigate  to isolate CSR
further is indigenous variables such  as managerial ability which could potentially be
linked to CSR performance within a company. A good and experienced manager may
value CSR  more and run a company better which could  provide a possible connection
between CSR and stock performance.  Therefore,  it may be relevant to  try and isolate the
managerial ability in order to  enhance the analysis of CSR.
      One important caveat  to also consider  is the issue of potential endogeneity between
CSR and firm performance. As mentioned, managerial ability may play a role in how well
a company both performs  in  general,  but  it  may  also  have an impact on a firms  CSR
commitments  and  the actions  a firm takes to achieve good ratings.  Firms that  weather
crises  well may  also  have more resources to invest in CSR, making it unclear whether
CSR  influences stock  performance or vice versa. While our  cross-sectional  approach
provides evidence of association  in the post crisis period, it does not identify causality.
Future studies could  use other  variables  or incorporate further fixed effects such as
country specific ones to more effectively isolate the  effects.

                                  21
    5.  Conclusion
In conclusion,  our findings indicate  that  during  a  crisis  period, higher  CSR  scores  are
associated with  better  adjusted  returns. However, in the  post-crisis  period,  CSR is
correlated with worse  adjusted  returns,  an effect that is  even more  negative than  the
positive impact observed  during  the  crisis. Notably, the  raw return  coefficient  for
idiosyncratic risk  during the crisis is  -1.82  and statistically significant at the 1%  level,
suggesting that idiosyncratic risk is not associated with favorable returns in times of crisis.
Conversely, in  the post-crisis period, the relationship between  idiosyncratic risk  and
return becomes strongly  positive,  with a coefficient of  4.95,  also  significant at  the  1%
level.
     When comparing the individual components of CSR, the Environmental and Social
scores, we find no statistically significant results during the crisis period. However, in the
post-crisis  period, the Environmental score exhibits a  positive  relationship with  both raw
and adjusted  returns. In contrast, the Social score shows a  slightly negative and
statistically significant association with returns, suggesting that its impact may differ from
that of  the Environmental  dimension in the post-crisis context.
      To  test the robustness of our results and data,  we added a new variable to the CSR
measure, namely the governance factor from ESG. This expanded measure, referred to as
CSR2, continues to show a positive and statistically significant relationship with adjusted
returns, supporting our earlier findings. However, when raw returns are considered, the
results become weaker or even slightly  negative once firm-level control variables are
included. This indicates that while the inclusion of governance reinforces the link between
CSR and risk-adjusted performance, it may also introduce additional variation that affects
the relationship with raw returns. Overall, the robustness test confirms the core patterns
observed in the main analysis, while also pointing to the more complex role governance
may play in how CSR influences firm performance.
      The findings of this study may have been influenced by differences in managerial
ability across firms. For example, forward-looking managers who anticipated the
potential benefits of CSR during a crisis may have proactively increased their firms’ CSR
engagement at the onset of the pandemic to mitigate negative impacts. Additionally, the
analysis of CSR’s effect on performance during a crisis is inherently complex, with many
contributing factors that could not be fully controlled for. Future research could explore
country-specific differences in CSR impacts during crises, as institutional contexts and
regulatory  environments  may  moderate  the  relationship  between  CSR  and  firm
performance  differently, especially during a crisis such as COVID-19.

When does payment method lose its signaling power in M&A? Evidence from
market reaction.

Abstract:

   This paper examines whether payment method still conveys bidder negative
   information in modern M&A. Using a European M&A sample of 1716 observations
   from 2014 to 2024, we focus on short-term acquirer announcement return using [-1;
   +1] deal announcement date window to compare the value associated with cash- and
   equity-financed deals. We identify two opposing drivers of equity financing: a
   synergies-enhancing instrument and a negative signal of bidder overvaluation. Our
   univariate result shows equity-financed M&A is no longer underperforming cash-
   financed M&A, thus generating higher marginal CAR. Additionally, we interact the
   payment method with target listing status, deal geography, and industry relatedness to
   clarify the associated strategic motives behind cash/stock payment. We find that stock
   is rewarded in private/subsidiary, cross-border, and cross-industry deals, where it
   functions as a risk-sharing mechanism for synergies alignment. Meanwhile, in public,
   domestic, and intra-industry deals, equity-financing still plays its traditional role in
   signaling bidder misvaluation.

Keywords:

   Merger and Acquisition, Payment Method, Value Creation, Abnormal Returns,
   Signaling

1. Introduction

   1.1. Background and motivation

Mergers and acquisitions are popular ways aimed at value creation for shareholders,
fundamentally achieved through synergies – or the incremental operational profitability
generated by the combination of the businesses. Multiple characteristics of the acquirer,
the target, and the deal itself are taken into consideration by the market to assess the future
direction and magnitude of synergies. This assessment is reflected in the equity
performance (if public) of both the sell and buy side by the time of announcement, thereby
realizing the value enhanced or destroyed.

However, a different strand of research points to the parallel existence of another value
driver in addition to forward-looking synergies: the retrospective market re-valuation of
the acquirer. This is based on the theory of information asymmetry and signaling effect
in an inefficient market. Essentially, some characteristics of the deal disclosed at the point
of announcement not only reveal information about collaboration success likelihood, but
also tell the market whether its judgment of the acquirer’s worth up until that point has
been accurate (Fuller, Netter & Stegemoller, 2002). One prominent deal characteristic
that serves this function is the choice of payment method: cash or equity.

To explain, when the acquirer is undervalued due to information asymmetry, management
prefers to pay with cash and avoid issuing underpriced shares; conversely, when the
acquirer’s shares are overvalued, paying with their own stock is cheaper, thus preferable
(Myers and Majluf, 1984; Hansen, 1987; Faccio and Masulis, 2005; etc.). As a result, the
market interprets stock-financed deals as a signal of overvaluation and reacts negatively
to correct, while cash-financed deals typically generate positive abnormal returns, holding
other factors constant (Travlos, 1987; Fuller, Netter, and Stegemoller, 2002). It is also
necessary to note that the ‘value’ referred to in these studies is are acquirers’ stakeholders’
return in the short-term, around the announcement date window.

We do not touch upon the drivers behind payment method choice themselves; however,
we assume that the acquirer’s choice of either cash or stocks is ultimately an effort to
benefit its stakeholders. Thus, our question of interest is rather whether the later equity
price develops in their favor, given that the market has its separate interpretations, and in
particular, we focus on the market's interpretation of the payment method.

Furthermore, it is interesting to understand the interaction between the parallel manners
in which payment method is perceived: through its impact on future profitability, and by
correction of past market (mis)valuation. In other words, the strength of the misvaluation
signaling effect could differ depending on whether the choice of payment method is
strategically justified. For example, Fuller, Netter & Stegemoller (2002) found that stock-

                                   4
financed takeovers are more popular when acquiring private and subsidiary targets, as
equity ownership serves as a risk-sharing tool between the acquirer and the bidder. Huang,
Officer & Powell (2016) found that in cross-border deals, bidders were increasingly
opting for stock to hedge uncertainty about the target value and political & institutional
risks. In these various contexts, stock payment is perceived as a value-enhancing feature
by the market, potentially cancelling out negative signals of acquirer overvaluation or
even creating positive value for shareholders.

In summary, our study uses data of 1716 EU mergers and acquisitions from 2014 to 2024
to study two main questions:

    (1) In European M&A between 2014 and 2024, does the choice of payment method
       (cash versus stock) lead to systematically different short-term announcement
        returns for acquirers, in a way that is consistent with the misvaluation-signaling
       theory?
    (2) How does the short-term value effect of stock-financed versus cash-financed
        acquisitions vary across deal environments where stock/cash also act as a synergy-
        enhancing tool, specifically with respect to (i) target public status (public vs.
        private/subsidiary), (ii) geographic scope (cross-border vs. domestic deals), and
        (iii) industry relatedness (cross-industry vs. within-industry)?

   1.2. Contribution

Our first contribution is to provide up‑to‑date EU evidence on whether payment methods
still carry the same informational content as in the classic US studies, by using a new,
comprehensive panel of EU acquirers over 2014-2024.

Foundational US work from the 1980s and 1990s (e.g., Travlos; Amihud, Lev & Travlos;
Martin, Hansen) established that stock-financed offers are met with significantly lower
acquirer announcement returns than cash offers, consistent with equity issuance signaling
overvaluation and cash signaling bidder confidence. However, Alexandridis, Mavrovitis
& Travlos (2012) document that sixth‑wave deals (2003-2007) remained
value‑destroying for acquirers despite becoming more cash intensive, and Alexandridis,
Antypas & Travlos (2017) report that in the post‑2009 US environment (data from 1981
to 2017), stock‑for‑stock deals no longer destroy value, suggesting a structural change in
how markets interpret stock consideration. Against this background, we expect that the
strategic role of stock has evolved to become more dominant compared to its signaling
role; a flip in the payment effect could be observed in our data period.

Our second contribution is to integrate the signaling and strategic synergy roles of stock
payment in a single empirical framework. Existing empirical studies typically
acknowledge these competing mechanisms but treat them separately: they set of firm and
deal‑level characteristics (public vs private/subsidiary targets, cross‑border status,

                                   5
relative deal size, etc.) and payment method as additive controls or subsample splits,
rather than modeling how the valuation effect of stock systematically varies with the
contexts in which its strategic role is most important (e.g. Fuller, Netter & Stegemoller,
2002; Bessler, Kruizenga & Westerman, 2020; Auguets-Pratsobrerroca, Martinez-Blasco
& García-Blandón, 2017). We contribute by explicitly interacting payment method in that
set of contexts. This design allows us to test whether the (potentially negative)
misvaluation‑signaling component of stock payment is attenuated, neutralized, or even
reversed in transactions where stock serves as a risk‑sharing or synergy‑facilitating
instrument.

Our final contribution is the exploration of the role that horizontal industry-relatedness
plays in moderating the payment method effect on short-term acquirer value, under a
similar spirit as that of target public status and cross-border status. Papers that study the
effect of industry-relatedness itself on acquirer value often report mixed results, with
some of them insignificant (Sudarsanam, Holl & Salami, 1996; Tuch & O’Sullivan, 2007;
Liu, Lu, Su & Wei, 2022). Meanwhile, papers that study whether cross-industry status
drives the choice of payment method rarely make a further step to connect to M&A deal
value creation. However, similar to the dynamics motivated in the target public status
context and cross-border context, we expect the payment method’s role to shift between
signaling and strategic among cross-industry and intra-industry environments. Hence, we
will test this moderation effect in our last hypothesis.

2. Literature review

   2.1. Classic payment-method signaling

       2.1.1. Information asymmetry in takeovers and the signaling theory framework
Our starting point is the classic lemon problem suggested by Akerlof (1970) in which one
side of a transaction has more information than the other, leading to mispricing of assets.
In corporate finance, managers are usually better informed than outside investors about
true firm value. If investors cannot distinguish high-quality from low-quality firms, they
set prices based on the average, which is known as adverse selection.

Since investors themselves are aware of this problem, they constantly monitor new
information and interpret any observable corporate actions as potential signals about the
unobservable state. In Spence-type signaling games (Spence, 1973), there are three core
ingredients:

    • Hidden type: The firm knows whether itself is high- or low-quality type.
    • Signal choice: The firm chooses an observable action.
    • Cost structure: Signals are costly by type, hence eliminating signal mimicking.

       2.1.2. Method of payment as a misvaluation signal
Applying the theoretical framework in the context of mergers and acquisitions, the
payment method satisfies all three pillars: management, being aware of the true valuation
of their own stock, and knowing whether issuing equity is costly or efficient. The market
infers the valuation from the firm’s chosen signal, and prices react. Myers-Majluf (1984)
developed this canonical asymmetric‑information model underlying virtually all later
work on payment choice. Their core findings are:

    • Issuing stock in general is attractive when the acquirers’ shares are overvalued,
       since existing shareholders can sell an expensive currency to buy real assets.
       Conversely, for an undervalued acquirer, issuing stock transfers a large share of
         future surplus to target shareholders at a discounted price.
    • Paying in cash is, by itself, a positive confidence signal. It not only sends a
       confirmation of current equity value, but also reflects strong financial health and
       high expectations of positive future performance.

To confirm the theory, Rhodes-Kropf, Robinson & Viswanathan (2005) develop a
decomposition of bidders’ and targets’ market-to-book ratios into (i) long-run value-to-
book, (ii) industry-level misvaluation, and (iii) firm-specific misvaluation, and show that
the misvaluation components strongly shape takeover activity. Using a large US sample,
they find that overvalued firms are more likely to become acquirers, to pay with stock

                                   7
rather than cash, and to buy less-overvalued targets, while undervalued firms are more
likely to be targets. Misvaluation also helps explain merger waves, as periods of high
industry-level overvaluation coincide with high M&A volumes. Overall, their evidence
supports the view that stock-financed acquisitions are often vehicles for exploiting
temporary overpricing, rather than purely synergy-driven investment decisions.

In a similar spirit, Dong, Hirshleifer, Richardson & Teoh (2006) test misvaluation versus
Q-theory using pre-offer book-to-price and residual-income value-to-price ratios as
proxies for bidder and target mispricing in US takeovers (1978–2000). They show that
overvalued bidders (low B/P or low V/P) are more likely to initiate bids, more likely to
offer stock, and tend to pay higher premia.

       2.1.3. Signaling effect of stock-financing on acquirer value
Foundational US papers provide the first direct statistical evidence for acquirer value
destruction due to stock-financing:

       Travlos (1987) documents the systematic difference across returns for choice of
       payment methods in acquisitions, using a market-model event study of 167
       successful US takeover bids from 1972 to 1981. They defined overvaluation using
       trading multiples as a benchmark. The results show a significant cumulative
        abnormal return (CAR), [-1; +1] days around the announcement, of -1.47% for
        stock-financed deals, versus an average 0% for cash offers.

        Similarly, Wansley, Lane & Yang (1987); and Franks, Harris & Mayer (1987-
        1988) find systematically better post-merger share price performance for cash
        acquirers than for equity acquirers, and in some specifications, negative CAR for
        equity offers.

Several early US research studies empirically attributed this difference directly to the
misvaluation signal sent by the stock:

       Shleifer and Vishny (2003) studied early 2000s US data, which further confirms
       that (i) acquirers exploit temporary stock market misvaluation, observed by the
       abnormally high market-to-book of acquirers, and (ii) announcement returns for
         stock biddings are negative.

       Moeller, Schlingemann & Stulz (2005) instead use “glamour” bidder as a proxy
       for overvaluation: those acquirers that are high-profile and involved in massive
       money value deals. They find a higher concentration of negative announcement
       returns in stock-financed mega deals than in cash-financed mega deals.

More recent US re‑examinations present results aligning with the acquirer
overvaluation signal sent by stock financing:

                                   8
       Alexandridis et al. (2012) re‑examine the classic “stock‑financed acquisitions
       destroy value” result in a large US sample and confirm that stock bidders have
       substantially lower CARs than cash bidders. Decomposing bidder returns into an
       acquisition and a financing component, they show that most of this
       underperformance reflects the misvaluation effect of announcing a stock offer
       (Myers-Majluf adverse selection), rather than the takeover itself, reinforcing the
        view that stock use is a signal of overvaluation.

      Eckbo, Makaew & Thorburn (2017) model bidder returns as a function of the
       continuous stock fraction and find a monotonic decline in CARs with the fraction
       of stock, concentrated in acquisitions of public targets and largely absent for
        private targets. Using valuation‑error proxies and IV methods, they link stock use
        to overvaluation but show that, after controlling for selection and risk‑sharing,
       there is no pervasive long‑run underperformance, implying that the key
       informational content of stock payment lies in a short‑run announcement‑window
        signal, especially in public‑target deals.

   2.2. Stock as a synergy-enhancing instrument

As presented, many M&A works largely treat stock financing as a negative signal, with
bidders paying in stock earning lower announcement returns than cash bidders. However,
Alexandridis, Antypas, and Travlos (2017) shift this narrative. Using a very large global
sample of 23,553 deals (1981-2017), they document that:

    (i) Post‑2009, bidder announcement returns, and total synergistic gains are
            substantially higher than in earlier decades.
    (ii) Crucially, stock‑for‑stock deals “no longer destroy value”; mega
           stock‑financed transactions (≥$500m) now generate large positive synergies
           and bidder wealth gains.

Their inference is that, in the modern era, acquirer equity is increasingly used as a
strategic instrument: to share risk, align incentives, and support value‑creating (often
complex) transactions, rather than merely as a vehicle for exploiting mispricing.

After reviewing the literature landscape, we focus on three contexts where stock can be
synergy‑enhancing, thus laying the motivation for later empirical design:

       2.2.1. Risk‑sharing over valuation and synergy uncertainty
Hansen’s (1987) “earnings uncertainty” model is the standard theoretical starting point:
when the target’s stand‑alone value and synergies are highly uncertain from the acquirer’s
perspective, stock allows both sides to share the ex-post risk, whereas cash loads all that
risk onto the acquirer.

                                   9
       Fuller, Netter & Stegemoller (2002) provide empirical evidence for this model
       using US data of frequent acquirers (>5 takeovers over the 1990s). They found
       significantly higher acquirer returns when private or subsidiary targets are bought
       with stock. They motivate that public targets have market‑verified prices and
       richer disclosure, while, conversely, private or subsidiary targets are much more
       opaque. Hence, stocks could be strategically used to make target sellers retain
       exposure to the combined firm, explaining the favorable interpretation from the
       market. Boone and Mulherin (2007) and Bargeron, Schlingemann, Stulz & Zutter
       (2008) empirically substantiate this argument as they find that public targets
       operate in a more transparent information environment due to regulatory
       disclosure and ongoing public scrutiny, implying lower information asymmetry
        than for private targets.

       Eckbo, Makaew & Thorburn (2017) studied 6,200 US takeovers by public,
       non‑financial acquirers (1980–2014) and found bidder announcement returns to
       decline monotonically as the stock fraction increases. Crucially, this negative
        slope is concentrated in acquisitions of public targets; for private targets, the
       stock‑fraction effect is much weaker. Zhang (2014), focusing on US banking
       M&As (1985–2006), sharpens the same point as the market’s dislike of stock
        offers concentrated in a public target.

        2.2.2. Mitigating country-related risks in cross‑border deals
In cross-border M&A, acquirers face greater uncertainty regarding the information about
the target, regulations, and future cross-border developments such as currency volatility.
Consistent with risk-sharing theory, from the acquirer’s point of view, it should be
preferable to pay the target’s shareholders with equity to share post-deal risks and realize
synergies under uncertainty.

       To confirm this theory, using 47 481 domestic and cross-border deals in 46
       countries (1990-2010), Huang, Officer & Powell (2016) study how country-level
       governance risk shapes the method of payment. They show that cross-border
        acquisitions are still more likely to be all-cash than domestic ones, but this gap
       has narrowed over time as mixed and equity-financed deals have become more
        common. Crucially, when the target’s country exhibits weaker investor protection,
        poorer transparency, or higher political and institutional risk than the acquirer’s
        country, bidders are significantly less likely to use all-cash and more likely to
        include stock in the consideration. They interpret this as acquirers using equity as
        a risk-sharing instrument to mitigate overpayment risk in opaque cross-border
        environments. Additionally, De La Bruslerie (2013), studying European M&As
        over 2000-2010, found that the premiums paid by acquirers to targets in cash-
       financed takeovers are significantly higher than those by stock. He suggests that

                                  10
       this is due to targets demanding to be compensated for the immediate capital-gain
         tax effect incurred by cash transfers.

       Linking the choice of stock to acquirer value creation in cross-border M&A, Cho
        & Ahn (2017) analyze 4,720 cross-border deals worldwide (1997-2012) with a
       standard event-study approach, finding that stock-financed cross-border
       acquisitions normally generate negative announcement CAR due to overvaluation
        signaling. Yet in environments with high institutional or cultural distance, their
        data find that stock becomes value-enhancing, directly supporting our hypothesis.

       However, from the target’s point of view, foreign equity is unattractive because
        of other types of cross-border friction such as tradability, legal complexities, and
        unclear foreign investors’ protection policy (Faccio and Masulis, 2005). In line
       with this, De La Bruslerie (2013) documents that while greater information
        asymmetry regarding the target increases the probability of all-share rather than
        all-cash payment, the actual proportion of cash deals dominates that of stock deals
       in cross-border takeovers, suggesting a much higher likelihood of successful
       negotiation between the sell and buy side if cash is offered.

In other words, stock offers, although preferred by acquirers as a strategic instrument to
manage country‑level risk, avoid overpayment and capture synergies in foreign
expansions, are rarely accepted by targets. However, that challenge itself can be the driver
for large acquirer upside potential if stock offers are pulled off, as confirmed by Cho &
Ahn (2017).

       2.2.3. Accommodating industry distance when synergies are more uncertain
There is little empirical evidence regarding the moderating effect of cross-industry status
on the relationship between payment method and acquirer value. Regarding whether the
choice of payment method is driven by cross-industry status in the first place, the most
prominent paper that emerges is Faccio and Masulis (2005). Using a large sample of
European acquisitions by public bidders in 1997–2000, they estimate probit/logit models
for cash versus equity offers, conditioning on ownership structure, investor protection,
and financial constraints. A key implication is that bidders are more likely to use stock in
related‑industry deals, where targets view bidder shares as a natural continuation of their
existing industry exposure.

Nevertheless, when solely examining the impact of payment method on value creation in
takeovers, the findings up to date are unclear:

       Tuch and O’Sullivan (2007) synthesize multiple event-study and post-merger
       performance papers that compare related versus diversifying acquisitions. Their
       synthesis is that findings are mixed: some classic US and UK studies show

                                  11
       significantly higher announcement or operating performance for related
       acquisitions, while others find little or no systematic difference, and a few
       document underperformance mainly in specific waves of conglomerate
        diversification.

       Liu, Lu, Su & Wei (2022), however, construct a “unique bidder–target
       relatedness” based on conditional stock‑return linkages instead of a simple SIC-
        based measure. They find that higher unique relatedness is strongly and positively
       associated with combined bidder-target announcement CARs and post‑merger
       efficiency gains, and that this measure outperforms standard industry or
         correlation‑based relatedness proxies in explaining value creation.

Therefore, while the observation by Faccio and Masulis (2005) that the stock-to-cash
volume ratio is higher in an intra-industry than cross-industry environment aligns with
our descriptive data, the implication of stock/cash on value creation remains open for
more research.

   2.3. Other confounding effects on M&A value creation

Other evidence also presents the influence of some other firm and deal characteristics that
induce the negative market reaction to stock-financing, besides misvaluation signaling.
Acknowledging these confounding factors serves as a basis for the later design of our
empirical methodology:

       Amihud et al. (1990) extend the announcement CAR event study to include
        managerial control and find a strong negative correlation: high insider ownership
        significantly reduces the probability of stock offers, which is interpreted as to
       avoid control dilution. While their baseline model confirms negative acquirer
        CAR for stock-financed deals relative to cash, but crucially, this negative reaction
       to stock occurs only in firms with low managerial ownership (<5%), reflecting the
       market’s concern over agency problems.

       Martin (1996) investigates a large set of payment method determinants. Acquirers
       with high leverage and low cash availability, or high exposure to uncertainty about
        the target, are significantly more likely to use stock-financing.

We aim to control for these payment method determinants in our model to separate the
effect of overvaluation signaling on bidder announcement abnormal return, as much as
data access allows. Specifically, our study includes the deal size ratio, tangible asset ratio,
and cash holding ratio, whose details will be specified under part 3 – data.

   2.4. Hypotheses

Our sample period (2014-2024) lies within the modern M&A era, during which the
strategic use of equity as a risk-sharing mechanism has regained its power over its
overvaluation signaling role, according to Alexandridis et al. (2017). Therefore, we
expect the following hypothesis:

H1: Stock-financed deals are no longer underperforming compared to cash-financed
deals regarding short-term abnormal returns.

Beyond the average base effect, earlier research suggests that the deal contexts strongly
shape the post-deal market reactions. First of all, equity is worth considering when
acquiring private or subsidiary targets with a high degree of uncertainty (Hansen, 1987;
Fuller et al., 2002). We are interested in how strategic use of stock may overweight the
traditional signaling theory and expect stock deals to offset the negative impacts
associated with private/subsidiary acquisitions:

H2: Target public status moderates the effect of payment method on short-term
acquirer cumulative abnormal returns (CAR), in that it attenuates the negative effect
of stock offers relative to cash offers in the private/subsidiary target acquisitions
context.

In cross-border settings, Huang et al. (2016) find that stock is served as a tool to manage
cross-border risks; however, is not as attractive as cash offers to the targets. We believe
that in foreign transactions, stock deals play double strategic roles in both risk-sharing
and incentive aligning mechanisms. As a result, we anticipate the market to react
favorably to equity financing in cross-border acquisitions:

H3: Cross-border status moderates the effect of payment method on short-term
acquirer cumulative abnormal returns (CAR), in that it attenuates the negative effect
of stock offers relative to cash offers in the cross-border context.

Lastly, for cross-industry takeover, the context is similar to cross-border acquisitions,
where stock-financing is expected to enhance the incentives and share risks associated
with an unfamiliar market from bidder’s perspective:

H4: Industry relatedness moderates the effect of payment method on short-term
acquirer cumulative abnormal returns (CAR), in that it attenuates the negative effect
of stock offers relative to cash offers in a cross-industry context.

3. Data

   3.1. Sample selection

The sample comprises EU-incorporated public acquirers announcing control acquisitions
during 2014 and 2024. The sample period captures a decade of M&A activities across the
volatility and varying macroeconomics of European markets. For the main models, data
only include M&A deals with either cash-only or stock-only considerations, and the
robust model deals with combined cash and stock considerations. The United Kingdom
is classified based on its legal domicile to ensure consistency.

   3.2. Deal filtering

Deals are retrieved from SDC Platinum with the following filters:

   ● The deal value is at least 10 million Euros, with all foreign currency being
       converted to Euros using the announcement date exchange rate.

   ● The post-deal ownership of acquirers is at least 50%, ensuring that the sample
        only includes control acquisitions.

   ● Acquirer with SICs from 6000 to 6999 (financial institutions) and 4900 to 4999
        (utilities) are excluded due to their distinct regulatory environments and capital
        structures, leading to bias.

   ● Deals with unknown or contingent-only considerations are excluded to maintain
        integrity.

   3.3. Market data & event date definition

Acquirer stock returns and industry benchmarks are obtained from LSEG Datastream. In
particular, daily close-to-close return indices from acquirer stocks are used instead of
actual stock returns to capture the effects of reinvestment of capital gains and dividends,
ensuring a comprehensive total return of shareholder return around the announcement
window. The date is based on the acquirer's listing calendar. For the industry benchmark,
the return indices are based on the acquirer’s economic sector, matched with the S&P
Europe 350 Industry classification, and with the same date, to control for sector-specific
market movements.

                                  14
   3.4. Subsamples

The final dataset consists of 1716 observations in total. In order to examine the signaling
role of cash financing under different conditions, we deal with 3 dimensions:

        3.4.1. Target listing status
Based on the listing status of the target firms, a 𝑃𝑢𝑏𝑙𝑖𝑐𝐷𝑢𝑚𝑚𝑦𝑖 is created, where 1
indicates the public targets and 0 represents non-public (private or subsidiary) firms. In
total, there are 128 public deals and 1588 non-public deals, resulting in an unbalanced
distribution of our subsample. This imbalance introduces a degree of bias toward public
acquisitions, which is acknowledged and further discussed in our analysis.

        3.4.2. Cross-border status
To identify cross-border and domestic transactions, we based on the acquirer’s country
of incorporation to see whether it differs from that of the target. A dummy variable, called
𝐶𝑟𝑜𝑠𝑠𝐵𝑜𝑟𝑑𝑒𝑟𝑖 , is equal to 1 if the acquisition is cross-border and 0 for domestic deals.
The sample consists of 920 domestic deals and 796 cross-border deals. This
categorization will enable us to analyze how geographical differences modify the
signaling role of payment methods.

        3.4.3. Industry relatedness
  In order to extract the target and acquirer industry, we flagged whether the acquirer
economic sector is different from the target economic sector: 1 is assigned for the

   3.5. Control variables

We employ a set of six firm-level control variables commonly used in M&A literature to
isolate the effects of confounding factors on the independent variables. All controls are
in the form of ratios, constructed from raw balance sheet figures from the most recent
fiscal year-end prior to the deal announcement.

Acquirer leverage ratio is used as a determinant of payment methods, where higher
leverage suggests a higher likelihood of stock-financing deals (Faccio & Masulis, 2005).
The ratio is computed by dividing the acquirer’s total debt by total assets over a period of
12 months. Leverage ratio also appears as an explicit acquirer variable to explain how
financing structure affects market reactions (Ismail & Krause, 2010).

                                  15
Acquirer tangible assets ratio (collateral) reflects the capacity of debt and signals the
feasibility of cash payment. Faccio & Masulis (2005) show that firms with more tangible
assets tend to use cash to finance M&A. Net Property Plant & Equipment (PPE) is divided
by total assets over a timeframe of 12 months to compute this ratio.

Acquirer’s cash holdings ratio measures the liquidity position and the capability to
finance the acquirer. It is constructed by taking the acquirer’s cash and cash equivalents
over total assets. The higher ratio shows a greater possibility of the acquirer being able to
finance the acquisition using internal funds, with less reliance on external capital (Martin,
1996; Faccio & Masulis, 2005).

Deal size ratio is standard in both payment-choice and return models. Previous papers
show that the relative size between acquirer value of market capitalization and deal value
is one of the key regressors for CAR, as larger deals are associated with stronger market
reactions and higher uncertainty (Faccio & Masulis, 2005; Fuller et al., 2002; Seth, Song
& Pettit, 2002). Deal size ratio is computed by dividing deal value by acquirer market
value, with foreign currency being converted to Euro using the exchange rate from the
announcement date.

Hostile dummy is included in Campa & Hermando (2004) to indicate the deal dynamics
in EU announcement-return models. In our study, firms with hostile attitudes are marked
as 1, and 0 for friendly deals. Furthermore, Faccio & Masulis (2005) also incorporated
hostile dummies in their sensitivity checks for the model, supporting its relevance within
the relationship between cash financing and post-deal abnormal returns.

Power Distance Index (PDI) difference, taken from Hofstede’s cultural dimensions,
measures the degree to which people in a culture are comfortable with the hierarchy of
power and wealth. As a proxy, the PDI difference is the absolute value of the difference
between the acquirer’s and target’s PDI. Dang, Henry, Thai, Vo & Mazur (2022) show
that power-distance differences moderate the effect between the likelihood of cash-
financing and political/economic uncertainty, such that when the target culture is more
hierarchical, the negative uncertainty-cash relationship weakens. Furthermore, this
control variable is solely included as a control variable in the interaction model between
CashDummyi x CrossBorderi.

    3.6. Fixed effects

To account for unobserved heterogeneity across industries, time, and jurisdictions, we
incorporate industry, time, and country fixed effects in all of our regression models. These
temporal, institutional, and structural differences are believed to systematically influence
the financing decisions and market reactions.

                                  16
Industry fixed effects control for cross-industry variations. The effects are captured by
categorizing firms into 7 industries using the TRBC Economic sector (excluding utilities,
financial services, and real estate). Controlling for industry differences helps to isolate
the effects of industry-specific norms and characteristics, namely acquisition intensity
and growth prospects.

Year fixed effects are included to control for time-varying macroeconomics and market
conditions over the studied period that can affect M&A activities. By holding all external
shocks, such as interest rates, economic growth, and investor sentiment, constant, we can
mitigate potential biases that can confound our results.

Country fixed effects are used to account for the heterogeneity in regulations and
institutions across jurisdictions are accounted for using country fixed effects. Differences
in governance quality, shareholder protection laws, and takeover regulations can affect a
firm's perception of financing choice and investors’ reactions to the announcement.

This section presents the results and the findings of our study. Building on the proposed
methodology, we examine how payment structures in M&A deals are related to the
acquirer announcement abnormal returns and how the relationship varies across
contextual factors.

We begin with the descriptive statistics obtained from our data sample to provide an
overview of the composition as well as the distributions, mean values, and standard
deviations. We then report the regression results from the baseline model: cash vs stock

solid foundation for the interpretation of the signaling effects of payment structures in
different environments.

The analysis is extended with interactive regression models, where we investigate how
target listing status, cross-border deal status, and industry-relatedness alter the signaling
role of cash financing to predict the acquirer’s short-term returns. These regressions focus
on how the decision of payment methods reflects information asymmetry and valuation
uncertainty, as we proposed in our research motivation.

This order indirectly supports the hypothesis that equity issuance is justified by the market
as a strategic instrument in an environment where information about the target is less
certain. While we expect higher information asymmetry to exist in cross-industry deals,
encouraging bidders to use stock financing to offload risks, past papers suggest that the
acceptance likelihood from the target side for equity in cross-industry deals is lower
(Faccio & Masulis, 2005). This lower success rate is consistently reflected in our data.
Given the target opposition, we speculate that the rarely pulled-off stock offers would
enjoy a boost in acquirer’s CAR relative to the common cash offers, which is confirmed
to be a marginal 2.8% in our model (Table 11).

On the other hand, one of the main purposes of intra‑industry (horizontal) acquisitions is
to expand the bidder’s market share and strengthen its market power in its existing
product markets (see Ghosh, 2004; Bhattacharyya and Nain, 2011; Fathollahi, Harford &
Klasa, 2022). We conjecture that, since growth prospects and positive synergies are the
goals, intra-industry bidders would rationally prefer cash financing to avoid equity
dilution and having to share long‑term gains with new shareholders. Hence, we argue that
equity offers are more likely to send the message that the acquirer firm’s stock is currently
overvalued, supported by the observed negative marginal effect of 0.5% observed in
stock-financed M&A. In other words, there is evidence that the market still punishes stock
financing in an intra-industry context, where it sees few reasons to use equity as a strategic
tool.

                                  32
6. Robustness checks

To further ensure the reliability of our regressions, we implement two robustness checks
across all models: (i) percentage of cash as a moderator and (ii) estimation of CAR over
a [-2; +2] window.

   6.1. Percentage of Cash

Our main analysis simplifies the share of cash used to close the deals, similar to prior
foundational literature. For example, according to the signaling theory by Travlos (1987),
the binary concept implies the acquirer firm’s value and its managerial confidence. The
latter study, by Fuller et al. (2002), shows that the types of payment methods are
categorical, which are directly linked to the market consideration. Therefore, we believe
that the use of a cash dummy creates a sense of comparability between our research and
previous studies.

On the other hand, in practice, not all payment types are categorized into cash or equity
financing. Some deals are completed using mixed payment methods. To account for the
heterogeneity across the degree of cash financing, we follow Faccio & Masulis’ (2005)
logic to conduct a robustness check using the percentage of cash, aiming for more nuance
in our study.

The results in Appendix 3 suggest that the overall effect of cash payment on CAR remains
significant as our main base regression, confirming that our main findings are not driven
by variable constructions. When interacting the percentage of cash with different settings
on the full sample, the cross-border deal characteristics became insignificant across all
main variables: cash dummy, cross-border flag, and cash dummy – cross-border flag
interaction. However, for target listing status and industry relatedness, the significance
level of our robustness check models does not deviate from our main regressions, albeit
the coefficients’ magnitude reduces marginally. As a result, we believe that our base
regressions are relatively stable and theoretically be able to support our interpretations
and discussions.

   6.2. CAR estimations using [-2;+2] window

In our main regressions, a narrow window [-1; +1] is used for the estimation of CAR as
we want to capture the immediate market effects of the announcement and avoid the noise
in the surroundings. In contrast, there are some issues linked to this choice of the window,
such as the earlier leakage of deals announcements due to rumors, the time-zone
difference associated with cross-border deals, and the slow processing of information.
Fama, Fisher, Jensen, and Roll (1969) also support the fact that event day might be

                                  33
uncertain, so by slightly extending our window to [-2; +2], we aim for a small buffer
around possibly missed record days.

Appendix 4 exhibits the outputs for our robustness check models. It can be seen that there
are no major differences between the [-1; +1] and [-2; +2] windows, showing the
concentrated effects of the payment methods, deal characteristics, and circumstances on
the short-term CAR. The robustness checks further suggest that our findings are aligned
with the classic signaling theory, which means that financing signals managerial private
information immediately. It confirms that our main regression models do not strongly
depend on the choice of little changes in the CAR window definition, and there are no
confounding factors centered around the announcement day.

7. Discussions

   7.1. Implications of results

To reiterate the purpose of our thesis, we study whether payment method still carries
significant informational value in the modern European M&A landscape, and how that
choice affects acquirers’ short-term returns through market reaction. Classic US studies
consistently report negative abnormal returns associated with stock-financed takeovers
around the announcement date (Travlos, 1987; Wansley, Lane & Yang, 1987; Franks,
Harris & Mayer, 1987-1988). They attribute the effect to the market interpreting stock
payment as an overvaluation of the acquirer, and cash payment as the acquirer’s
confidence and commitment to the deal, which are later empirically confirmed by
Rhodes-Kropf et al. (2005) and Dong et al. (2006).

In contrast, our 2014-2024 evidence shows that equity-financed acquisitions are not
penalized in the same way, and in many cases are rewarded. This finding aligns more
closely with more recent research that highlights the growing recognition of equity as a
flexible contracting mechanism (Faccio and Masulis, 2005; Alexandridis et al., 2017).

to stock financing in M&A, using the interpretation suggested by Brambor, Clark, and

Specifically, on the aggregate level, our cash effect model found a 2.5% marginal
underperformance of cash-financed takeovers’ CAR at 1% significant level, also
confirmed by the statistically significant t-test of the difference in mean CAR between
cash and stock deals. This is translated to the positive value creation of stock-for-stock
deals from our sample since our independent variable is binary, only considering 100%
equity- or 100% cash-financed deals. The finding leads us to accept H1 and explore some
environments where the role of equity as a strategic value-enhancing tool is perceived to
be stronger.

First, when separating deals based on target public status, the classic literature is partly
confirmed, and we accept H2. For acquisitions of public targets, there were weaker
market reactions to stock offers, consistent with earlier evidence that equity issuance is
interpreted most skeptically when target valuation is well understood (Fuller et al., 2002).

                                  35
On the other side of that same coin, acquiring a public target with cash signals enough
confidence to largely offset the univariate underperformance of cash offers. Conversely,
equity-financed acquisitions of private or subsidiary firms perform significantly better
than cash. This reinforces the idea that stock becomes more valuable when uncertainty
about the target favors contractual flexibility rather than signaling concerns.

Second, findings are largely similar across geographic and industry dimensions, leading
us to accept H3 and H4. Foreign acquisitions paid in cash generate significantly lower
announcement effects, enough to reverse the positive returns expected from a general
cross-border deal. Cash payment is perceived to heighten uncertainty and integration
risks. When financed with stock, however, cross-border deals perform more favorably.
The same holds for cross-industry transactions: equity financing yields stronger
announcement returns, while cash destroys value. In both settings, the results are
consistent with stock being used to share uncertainty rather than broadcast misvaluation,
therefore creating positive marginal CAR. Meanwhile, in intra-industry deals, where
there is little justification for issuing equity to pay targets, the signaling power prevails
and leads to negative marginal CAR. Domestic context, as discussed before, does not
perfectly represent an environment where the acquirer cannot justify its choice for equity;
hence, market interpretation is mixed.

In addition, the aggregate superior performance of stock financing relative to cash
financing could also be supported by the improved requirements for disclosure and the
governance standards across EU jurisdictions. The changes have improved the
availability of information, thus reducing the signaling power of payment to convey
private information. Moreover, the gap among investor perceptions about domestic and
foreign targets has become narrower within the European zone. Therefore, the existing
information asymmetry has been gradually eroded. This outcome draws an important
temporal dimension of the signaling theory, that the explanatory power of traditional
sources of information asymmetry has been weakened as the markets become more
transparent and standardized. However, further statistical analysis is necessary to
substantiate this argument.

   7.2. Limitations

This paper is subject to several limitations. First of all, we do not account for any
structural changes, such as the COVID-19 crisis and the Brexit referendum, throughout
our study period. During these major events, investors and markets tend to change their
perceptions of financing structures and risk exposure. On the bidder side, the possibility
and the attractiveness of cash/stock financing may alter during the periods of higher
information uncertainty, as both acquirers and targets may face constraints of liquidity,
valuation volatility, and access to debt financing. Hence, we believe that the absence of
dummy variables for these shocks may influence our practical research validity.

                                  36
Second, we treat transactions between UK and UK-domiciled firms as cross-border
transactions, thus not perfectly distinguishing the actual distances of legal systems,
cultural heterogeneity, and informational environments between countries. As a result,
our results for the study on the interactive effect of cross-border and cash on CAR are
biased. In other words, the cross-border and interaction term coefficients appear to be
closer to zero than the actual effects, which are often due to firms choosing domiciles
based on tax structural purposes. Our estimations remain valid but conservative, as long
as acquirer/target nations are defined as legal domiciles.

Third, there are concerns about data bias. One concern is that, given the large proportion
of private/subsidiary acquisitions in our collected sample, the regressions could be
overridden partly by the strategic synergy-enhancing role prominent in the acquisition of
these types of targets. However, the coefficients of cross-border flag and cross-industry
flag were at 1% significant level across both the raw and winsorized samples. Another
concern is with the possible influence of outliers, since there exist deals that are observed
to have abnormally high CAR. Regardless, the proportion of stock-financing deals is
substantially minor overall, and significant results were reported in the winsorized model
in which these anomalies were normalized, suggesting that the observed superior effect
of stock financing is systematic.

Lastly, earlier research from Fuller et al. (2002) and Faccio & Masulis (2005) shows that
managerial ownership is one of the key drivers for the decision-making process on
whether the acquisitions should be financed by equity or cash. They suggest that higher
internal control leads to a higher likelihood of cash-financing, avoiding share dilution.
Additionally, the choice between cash and stock also determines the bidder’s post-deal
CAR. However, in our study, due to the lack of data availability, we could not address
information on insider-controlled stakes and did not account for it as a control variable.

Overall, these limitations suggest that our research should be analyzed with caution.
Nevertheless, we believe that the overall patterns remain valid and informative, directly
answering our research question on how market reactions vary across different
environments. Further addressing our limitations on the macro shocks, domicile
classifications, and data quality would give a more comprehensive aspect of our study.

                                  37
8. Conclusion

Following the foundational work of earlier US papers on whether the post-deal value
creation is influenced by the informational signal of payment methods, this paper
provides an update relative to the question using data on EU acquisitions from 2014 to
2024. In addition to that, our study further investigates whether different informational
and strategic contexts moderate the magnitude and direction of that relationship.

In our univariate model, we answer our first question: acquisitions financed by equity are
no longer destroying value, suggesting the misvaluation signaling role of stock is
weakening on the aggregate level. Instead, stock payment is increasingly acting as a
synergies-enhancing tool nowadays, recognized by the market. By using three moderating
regression models to study the role of stock-financing under the three mentioned
circumstances, we answer our second question:

    When examining (i) public target, (ii) domestic M&A, and (iii) intra-industry M&A,
    the misvaluation signaling role of stock and the confidence signaling role of cash
    remain strong and dictate market reaction in corresponding directions.

     When examining: (i) private/subsidiary target, (ii) cross-border M&A, and (iii) cross-
    industry setting in Europe, the evidence implies that today stock-financing is
    primarily seen as a tool to elevate the post-deal synergies, mainly by mitigating risks
    and aligning incentives.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the economic meaning of payment choice in
European M&A has become conditional rather than universal. While the classical
misvaluation-signaling mechanism persists in transparent, low-uncertainty settings, it is
increasingly superseded by the strategic and synergistic role of stock in deals
characterised by greater informational frictions. This dual interpretation provides a
coherent explanation for the diminishing unconditional payment effect observed in recent
years, and highlights the importance of analyzing payment method within the specific
informational and strategic context of each acquisition.

Green minus Brown: A comparative Analysis of the Financial Performance of the
ESG factor

Abstract:

This thesis investigates the performance of a Green-minus-Brown portfolio in the US
public stock market and whether the GMB portfolio provides significant results for
investors. The study also aims to investigate if the returns of the GMB portfolio are
already explained by other existing factors. The research collected data from 2003 to
2022. Moreover, our findings indicate that there is no significant effect of the GMB
portfolio over a longer period of time. Nevertheless, during economic expansions, there
are effects in the GMB portfolio that are not explained by existing factors. The findings
of the research point at the fact that ESG might not be an ideal and unique factor for
investors looking to predict their returns. In light of these results found for the GMB
factor, it is fundamental to note that  further research  in this area is fundamental to better
understand the role of the GMB portfolio, especially over longer periods of time and
different economic scenarios and markets.

   1.  Introduction
       The significant risks of global warming and climate change to the world
economy are becoming increasingly relevant to investors,  governments,  and different
stakeholders. The impact and risk posed by global warming and pollution-driven climate
change are expected to have implications for financial markets, firm profitability, and
household wealth (Hong, Karolyi, & Scheinkman, 2020).  Addressing climate changes
can be considered one of the most challenging undertakings humanity is forced to face,
which requires global cooperation, yet is riddled with agency problems, third  party
externalities and financing constraints, and whose risks are underestimated (Stroebel &
Wurgler, 2021).
       Increasing   investor   preferences   for   green   assets   reflects   a   view   that
environmental,  social,  and corporate governance matters have the potential  to influence
long-term financial performance, and thus are to be considered in investment decisions
(Boffo & R. Patalano, 2020).
        To reduce risks and increase long-term profits, an increasing number of investors
are attempting to invest sustainably by taking environmental, social, and governance
concerns into account where, as of 2020, it’s estimated that more than $35 trillion have
been funneled into sustainable investments (Global Sustainable Investment Review
2020).
        One of the asset management industry's fastest-growing subsectors is sustainable
investment, with some figures indicating 15% annual growth in assets over the last two
years (Global Sustainable Investment Review 2020). Sustainable investing entails
considering  non-pecuniary benefits while making investment decisions, which is an
approach that aims to incorporate environmental, social and governance factors into
realizing sustainable financial returns (Hong et al., 2020).
       Green assets in the past have outperformed brown assets.  Pástor, Stambaugh,
and Taylor developed an equilibrium model which suggests that since investors have
developed green tastes and because greener assets are a better buffer against climate
risk, it’s expected that green assets will have lower anticipated returns  than brown ones
(Taylor, Stambaugh, &  Pástor, 2019). Therefore, the lower projected returns of greener
assets represent both a hedge premium and a preference premium.
       First, the demand from investors for green assets may rise, directly increasing
the prices of green assets. Second, increasing environmental regulations, and customer

                                      3
demand for green products may increase green company revenues thereby leading to
increases in share prices. Parallel to this process is the declining investor demand for
brown assets which causes green assets to perform better relatively (Taylor, Stambaugh
et al. 2019).
       Taking strong inspiration from the paper “Dissecting green returns” by Pástor,
Stambaugh, & Taylor  (2022), we aim to further understand if the green stocks
outperform   brown   stocks   in   the   American   and   European   markets.   Since   the
aforementioned paper dissects green returns from many different points of entry, such
as German twin bonds, stocks in the American market and equity wise, we aim to
replicate such paper focusing solely on the public equity markets. Considering the
empirical method, this is extremely similar to as done in Pástor, Stambaugh et al. (2022),
in terms of how we intend to create a model for a long short GMB portfolio. Our research
centers on the assumption that ESG ratings provided by Refinitiv Eikon are a proxy for
the greenness of a financial asset, however we try to reformulate the way in which a
given stock’s “greenness” is defined by an overall ESG score. Furthermore, we look into
how the existing factors in literature are able to capture the effect of the GMB portfolio
on the returns, that is how the GMB factor is already priced in by the other factors. This,
therefore,  leads to the following research question.

Research question:

“Is there a  higher performance rate for green stocks compared to brown stocks?”

       Our main findings are that we have not found significant outperformance of green
stocks over brown stocks, by the creation of such a GMB portfolio and regressing the
returns of this factor against already existing factors relevant to literature. Moreover, we
replicated the experiment for European stock exchange data and found similar results. It
is however noteworthy to mention that regressing the GMB portfolio on the existing
factors, during good economic scenarios, increases the statistical significance of our
regressions, indicating that the novel GMB factor might be present during economic
expansions.

   2.  Literature review

       Firstly, the study by  Pástor  et al.  (2022)  aims to examine the historical
performance of ESG-characterized and sustainable assets, and its implications for future
investing. The study utilizes an equilibrium model developed by Pástor, Stambaugh, &
Taylor  (2021), referred to as PST, which assumes that green assets have lower expected
returns compared to brown assets. This is due to investors’ increasing preference for
green assets, and such assets’ inherent hedging quality against climate change risks. The
model also assumes that green assets may exhibit high realized returns due to unexpected
changes in demand for these assets.

       Additionally, the study focuses on the U.S. financial market and uses MSCI’s
environmental rating data to observe that during the period  November  2012 – December
2020, green assets had outperformed brown assets. The study asserts that this past
outperformance isn’t necessarily a predictor of future outperformance, but likely to be
the result of an unexpected increase in investor environmental awareness and green asset
demand.

       The study analyzes the impact of public perception of climate change on the
performance of green assets by using a media index developed by Ardia, Bluteau, Boudt,
& Inghelbrecht (2021). The researchers found out that green assets outperformed brown
assets when negative news regarding climate change were communicated. This is in line
with the suggestion that green assets provide a hedge in periods of negative climate
events. It was also concluded that in absence of the effects of unforeseen increases in
climate  change  awareness,  the  outperformance  of  the  green-minus-brown  ,GMB,
portfolio disappears to a large extent.

        Another finding of the study is that stocks of smaller companies generally reacted
more gradually to negative climate  change news, which is relevant for investors
considering portfolio adjustments based on news pertaining to climate risks. The
researchers constructed a two-factor asset pricing model, of which one is a green factor.

        This study has implications for investing; it underlines the notion that investors
should be mindful of both expected and realized returns, instead of basing investment
decisions on recent performance of green assets as a predictor of future performance, and

                                      5
is relevant for our study since we look into a different set of data available for investors,
with a similar approach.

       Another relevant source of literature for our study is  Climate Change Concerns
and the Performance of Green Versus Brown Stocks, by Ardia et al.  (2021), in which it
explores the connections between unforeseen transitions in the climate risk concerns, and
how green and brown stocks perform. The PST model used in this study predicts that
green stocks would outperform brown stocks when public and investor concerns about
climate  change elevate. This prediction is examined by analyzing daily returns of
S&P500 stocks and utilizing a new proxy for unexpected shifts in climate change
concerns based on news articles released on the same day.

       The findings of this study align with the prediction laid by PST, suggesting the
green assets perform better than brown assets upon sudden shifts in climate change
concerns. Additionally, the paper determines that investors were more likely to divest
from brown assets than to place investment in green assets when the severity of climate
change perception was elevated.

       The paper delves into driving mechanisms underlying the relationship between
climate change risk perception, and financial asset performance. The researchers discuss
two channels through which perception of climate change affects asset prices. Namely,
the  customer  channel  through  increased  demand  and  regulatory  facilitations  for
sustainable solutions, and the investor channel whereby the discount rate for green assets
is lower due to utility from financially enabling green companies.

        Furthermore, the study analyzes how the emissions intensity of an individual firm
affects the sensitivity of the individual stock’s returns when climate change concerns
increase. The findings show that firms characterized by lower emissions intensity exhibit
more positive value returns on days when climate change concerns increase, aligning
with the findings of Ilhan, Sautner, & Vilkov  (2020). The effect was observed at industry
level for a minority of industries.

       The study’s findings yield some implications for firm managers, as it shows
climate change concern in the media and its fluctuations can affect firm value. This
implies that firms stand to gain from improving their climate risk profile, in terms of
growing  preferences on the side of investors and lower discount rates. Moreover, the

                                      6
implications for our study are such that, given the higher attention given to ESG in recent
years, there are stronger and more direct impacts on the behavior of a GMB portfolio.

       Lastly, the research  Are Carbon Emissions Associated with Stock Returns?  by
Aswani  (2021)  investigates  the  relationship  between  CO2  emissions,  company
operational and stock performance and Tobin’s Q by examining 2,729 U.S. firms for the
period  2005-2019.  The  study  highlights  that  similar  studies  such  as  Bolton  &
Kacperczyk,  (2021) rely on changes in  a measure  of unscaled CO2 emissions as the
independent variable, whereas Aswani  (2021) show that there exists a strong correlation
between unscaled CO2 emissions and firm size, industry compositions, and time. Aswani
(2021) also  shows  that CO2 emissions to sales, also known as carbon intensity, doesn’t
correlate with stock returns.

       The study also points out that prior research investigating the relationship
between stock returns and CO2 emissions relied completely on vendor-estimated data
rather than firm-disclosed emissions data. Vendor-estimated data comprises roughly 80%
of emissions figures in databases. The researchers bring forth empirical evidence that
shows systematic differences between data disclosed by firms, and vendor-estimated
data, which casts doubt on the validity and robustness of prior research. They show that
vendor-estimated CO2 as a function of size, revenue growth, industry and time, function
as a proxy of within industry heterogeneity in business models. This further implies that
prior research investigates the relationship between operating performance and stock
returns.

       The study shows that upon controlling for confounding factors such as  the size of
firms and industry categorization, the correlations suggested in previous research cease
to show and disappear. The authors further investigate the relationship in a European
context where disclosure is more common practice and investors’ preferences place more
weight on ESG-metrics, without finding a correlation between CO2 emissions and stock
performance.

       The study’s findings  invite  caution on part of ESG-oriented investors and
academics when interpreting and investigating the relationship between CO2 and
emissions. The study underlines the role of omitted variable bias, in terms of size,
industry and time, and vendor-estimated CO2 emissions data in showing correlations that

                                      7
invite scrutiny. Implications involving policymakers arise, in terms  of the importance of
developing regulatory and compliance oriented CO2 emission disclosure standards,
which would enhance the quality of data used in academic research. For our case, it would
provide a benchmark for comparing CO2 intensive firms and their  greener counterparts
in terms of financial performance.

   3.  Data and  Institutional  Background

        The major focus of study in this research is the US stock market, however we also
gathered data from European stock exchanges to study potential disparities in ESG
preferences between the markets, which could be reflected differently in the GMB
portfolios.

       We collected data from all companies listed in Nasdaq and NYSE, which results
in 6389 companies. European data consist of 7928 companies listed in Austria, Belgium,
Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Great Britain, Ireland, Italy, Netherlands,
Norway, Portugal, Spain,  Sweden,  and Switzerland. Out of the companies included in
the analysis, European data tends to have a somewhat similar return distribution to US
stock data, but instead seems to have somewhat higher ESG Scores as the European mean
and median are 54.25 and 52.91 respectively, while the American ESG scores have mean
and median of  40.58  and  37.25,  respectively.

       The gathered data consists of ESG ratings, and 52 week returns for the past 20
years for publicly listed companies in the aforementioned stock exchanges. Contrasting
to Pástor et al.  (2022), we use the singular annual ESG score for the basis of our research,
instead of extending back the ESG score up to 12 months until the prior rating.

       Data was mostly retrieved from the Refinitiv  EIKON database using the
Screening App, due to its high accessibility and data integration. Refinitiv EIKON is one
of the world’s largest and most well-renowned ESG data gathering and analysis
providing databases with plenty of key financial metrics for both public and private
companies around the world. One important distinction that our raw data provides is that
it only includes existing companies; which can therefore lead to survivorship bias in our
regressions  and analysis. The issue arises as companies that are no longer listed, due to
bankruptcy, delisting, or acquisitions are not included.

                                      8
        Another important aspect of the data used is that it encompasses the last 20 years,
as Refinitiv EIKON began covering ESG ratings in 2003 (Refinitiv). The importance of
such data is that it allows us to study the ESG effect on stock prices across different
economic scenarios such as economic booms and busts. Despite larger coverage in the
last years, due to the inclusion of MSCI ESG rating in 2019 (Refinitiv), our  aim was to
study the overall effect, and if needed broken down into specific economic scenarios.

        Refinitiv EIKON ESG rating relies on over 630 metrics, divided into the 3 pillars,
Environmental, Social and Governance, to create a holistic ESG score for  companies
(Refinitiv). Since our goal is to understand the overall effect of ESG on stock returns we
used the combined weighted score, despite that the effect of each individual pillar is, in
and of itself, an area for further research.

       In order to argue  that the ESG factor, proxied by the construction of a GMB
portfolio, is relevant for stock returns, we look into if this new factor is not explained by
other well accepted factors in academia. For such we use the Fama French 3 Factors,
Fama  French  5  Factors,  Fama  French’s  Momentum  Factor  and  lastly  the  Size,
Investment,  profitability,  and Expected Growth factors from Hou, Mo, Xue, &  Zhang
(2020).

   4.  Methodology and Main  Results

       When considering US stock data, we remove the companies that have fewer than
4  ESG ratings, which reduces the number of companies included from 6389 to 2487.
These companies are now used to create the Green-Minus-Brown Portfolio, whose
creation is quite straightforward. We create panel data with a given company’s return and
ESG rating for a given year. The companies in the bottom 20 percentile of ESG ratings
are allocated to a Low ESG portfolio, while the top 20 percentile are allocated to a High
ESG portfolio. Subsequently, at each time interval, we subtract the returns of the low
ESG portfolio from the High ESG portfolio to create the GMB portfolio.

       The data used in  Dissecting Green Returns  only spans from 2012 to 2020, which
happens to be only during an economic expansion, apart  from 2020. Therefore, we have
also decided to look into recessions and economic expansions, which might help us to
understand the possible heterogeneous effects in the relationship between ESG rating and
excess returns. To help identify periods where the American economy is undergoing
periods of recessions, our paper uses data from the Federal Reserve Economic Data.
FRED  is  an  online  database  comprising  economic  data  time  series  from  U.S.,
international, public, and private sources. The dates of U.S. recessions that we employ in
our study are inferred by a GDP-based recession indicator whereby an index value above
67% means  that the economy is determined to be in a recession (The  Econbrowser
recession indicator index). For the scope of our study, we approximated the years 2008,
2009, and 2020 as recessionary years (FRED).

       Another factor in which we have arbitrarily chosen is the number of observations
per company. Since ESG has had a sharp increase in interest over the past years, both
from the investors’ and the managers’ point of view (Welch and Yoon, 2022), there is an
expectation that the companies will report their ESG data as of a  certain time and this
will be thus used by Refinitiv EIKON to produce an ESG rating. Therefore, in our
universe of returns and ESG ratings gathered, we observed that, for instance, some
companies had only ESG ratings in the last 2 years, resulting in only  2 observations for
such companies. For the result of the estimations to be as close as possible to the actual
market we could have included companies with a single or two observations, however
this adds additional noise to our regressions. On the other hand, we could have stricter
requirements, such as companies having a total of 7+ ESG ratings for instance, which in
theory would lead to a cleaner and more reliable data analysis, as it would capture the
long-term effect of the ESG on returns. However, this also drastically reduces the number
of companies in our portfolios. Therefore, in total, we have replicated the experiment
with companies with at least 1 ESG rating up to a minimum of 6 ESG ratings and also
found similar results. In table 7 we have the summary of this robustness check, where no
sizable statistical difference is found depending on the number of ESG ratings issued for
a company.

   5.  Discussion

        Our findings in Table 1, point at the fact that there is a significant correlation
between the GMB factor and already existing and well accepted factors in current
literature. Since we did not find statistical significance in Table 2, of the GMB factor in
combination with existing factors, our results could be aggregated resulting in the
absence of a significant relation between the GMB factor and excess returns over the
period between 2003 and 2022.

       However, since we are replicating the experiment in section 4 of Pástor et al.
(2022) one could expect to have similar results, which is not the case. There are several
factors that can lead to such a difference, most of them are related to our data sources and
the difference in timeline adjustments. The study  by Pástor, Stambaugh et al. (2022)
creates a “greenness” measure by using the green score as well as the green pillar weight
of data available at MSCI, while we decided to focus on the overall ESG score for
companies from Refinitiv. We did not utilize an environmental weight, or social and
governance related weights for that matter, in our data as these are already accounted for
in the calculation of the overall ESG Score by Refinitiv EIKON. The aforementioned
“greenness” measure indicates how far away a company is from the perfect score. On the
other hand, our rating from Refinitiv EIKON represents the overall ESG score of a
company, where the most sustainable companies score the highest. Therefore, this gives
rise to the difference in sign of the constant terms in our results compared to the replicated
paper.

       Despite somewhat expected correlation between these data  sources,  they are
fundamentally different, which is shown in our results. Moreover, Pástor, Stambaugh et
al. (2022) uses  return data from CRPS.  CRPS data includes companies that are no longer
listed, which is a feature that our data source for returns, Refinitiv EIKON, does not
include. In that sense, our data is not as ‘clean’ as the one in  Dissecting Green returns,
because our data sample suffers from survivorship bias. Our results, columns (2), (3) and

                                     14
(4) in both Table 1 and Table 4 respectively, show that the GMB portfolio tilts towards
larger companies, much like the findings in  Pástor’s paper. However, the comparatively
higher magnitude of our findings could be a consequence of a survivorship bias
mechanism, whereby smaller companies are more likely to be delisted than larger
companies (Gilbert & Strugnell, 2010).

         The other main difference between our analysis and the one conducted by Pástor,
Stambaugh et al. (2022) is that we did not extend the ESG rating back to the observation
from a year prior. Instead, we used only annual data for the returns to match the reporting
of ESG scores, which drastically reduces the creation of a GMB portfolio,  to only 20
observations, however it was carried out to better reflect the effect of ESG ratings as this
extension would’ve implied “stickier” relationship between the variables, and perhaps
even lagged effects in the regressions.

        Another difference in methodology lies in that we did not use the same percentiles
and had stricter requirements for the amount of ESG scores per company, which as
already mentioned does not impact the significance of our results. Nevertheless, when
analyzing the behavior of the GMB portfolio in economic expansions, we obtain much
similar results to Pástor, Stambaugh et al. (2022). This points at heterogeneous effects
since the GMB factor is more significant in good economic scenarios, which is more
similar to the period used in the replicated paper.

       Moreover, by studying the effect of a GMB portfolio in European stock data, we
are able to draw two very interesting insights. The first of which is that it reinstates the
statistically significant effect of the Small-Minus-Big factors in the GMB portfolio,
which could be a consequence of lower trading volumes creating information asymmetry
for smaller companies first discussed by Roll  (1981). The second consequence of this is
that the difference in sentiment towards ESG between American and European markets
is not as pronounced, which is in line with the findings from West and Polychronopoulos,
(2020).

       The practical implications of this research are somewhat limited, as by using
granular annual data we limit ourselves to a more theoretical framework by looking into
the direct relationship between ESG ratings and annual returns for a given time period.
Investors will base their decisions on the existing information in the market, whether or

                                     15
not it represents the ‘complete’ information for a statistical model. The use of existing
information is, on the other hand, better captured by methodology in Pástor, Stambaugh
et al. (2022).

       Nevertheless, there are noteworthy implications in a theoretical framework. By
extending the time horizon in which we look into sustainability, we have less significant
results. Another important aspect is the different outcome of the analysis under non-
recessionary   economics   periods.   The   GMB   factor   has   statistically   significant
characteristics not explained by existing factors, implying that it aligns with the results
from Pástor, Stambaugh et al. (2022) and Ardia et al.  (2021).

       The study by Ardia et al.  (2021) reaches  a conclusion  that  is  interestingly in
partial harmony with our findings. This study provides empirical support to the recent
return outperformance of the green assets, and attributes this phenomenon to the higher
attention given to ESG upon sudden shifts in climate change concerns, which the
researchers claim have been on the rise in recent years. Similar to the  Pástor’s  study
which we replicated, our results upon the inclusion of the full dataset, don’t align with
this study. However, upon excluding recessionary years from our  dataset our results lend
support to their findings. The focus of Ardia’s paper on investor attention to ESG-related
news, invites a straightforward explanation as to why GMB return outperformance is not
observed under recessionary years. Financial and corporate news related to an economic
recession, which is a major global event, with ESG-related news for investor and public
attention. The loss aversion tendency in investing observed in Ardia’s study, could be at
play during recessionary period whereby news directly relevant to  an  asset’s financial
performance gain more investor traction than ESG-related news which might indirectly
affect financial performance.

       Aswani’s study focuses on scrutinizing a great deal of financial literature that
bases conclusions on unsuitable metrics or invalid methods. The study underpins  the
impact  of omitted variable bias in showing or nullifying correlations. The sensitivity of
our results and subsequent conclusions to including or excluding recessionary years
provides a case-study example for the call of caution laid forth by Aswani et al. (2021).
Arguably, their problematization of research characterizes our study, whereby the
‘omitted variable’ of state of economy-level distress drives variation in the data, and
ultimately  conclusions.

                                     16
       In our study, the decrease  in  significance of the effect of GMB on excess returns
during recessions, which relating to the findings of  Pástor, could be driven by two main
aspects. The first of which is that during financial crises there is a higher focus on returns,
which leads to an increased attractiveness of value companies, the majority of which are
not considered green companies. Secondly, the importance of negative environmental
news diminishes in face of news covering the broader financial crisis, which leads to
lower exposure to news evoking environmental concern. The second mechanism
reconciles with the mechanism driving green asset outperformance in the paper we
replicate.  Pástor  provides argumentation suggesting that recent outperformance of green
assets is news-driven, and shouldn’t be expected to last, as investors grow accustomed to
negative environmental news and ESG-investing becomes less novel over time.

       Therefore, we are able to conclude that ESG investing presents a similar behavior
to luxury investing, where it is strongly present when capital markets are favorable and
news coverage of environmental issues isn’t overridden by major economic news.

5.1 Limitations & Extensions

       The major limitation in our research was  access to more detailed data. By having
more observations for returns we would have been able to better replicate the paper by
Pástor, Stambaugh et al. (2022), which could provide us with more in depth insight into
the effect of sustainability on a company’s performance, over a longer time span.
Secondly, the larger number of time periods would allow us to create better and more
statistically reliable regressions, as even in the case with the complete time horizon,
namely 2003 to 2022, we had a limited number of observations on which we ran our
regressions. Lastly, we would be able to better adapt our data to the economic expansions
and recessions as defined by FRED and thus better capture the heterogeneity in our data.

       Despite Refinitv Eikon serving as a credible database for ESG ratings for the
world of academia, there are however certain limitations in terms of such data. There’s
subjectivity in the chosen method that is used to quantify a qualitative concept such as
sustainability bringing forth the biases of the designer (Calvert 2021).

        Publicly reported data by firms forms the basis of Refinitiv ESG scores, implying
that corporate discretion and disclosure willingness may present a distorted image of

                                     17
reality. The resulting information asymmetry and opacity diminish the research value of
our data (Refinitiv ESG company scores). Lastly, comparing companies across different
industries and regions is challenging in terms of differences in information availability,
reporting standards and regulations (Build Custom ESG Scores using Refinitiv ESG Data
in Eikon).

      A  simple extension  to our study  would be to replicate Pástor, Stambaugh et al.
(2022) and our regressions for different economic scenarios with more specific time data.
Another clear extension for this paper would be creating an event study that analyzes the
differences between the European and American preferences for sustainability in a more
detailed manner, by having some measure for the difference in the preferences. Lastly,
further relevant research could include a more detailed analysis of the effect of the
different components of ESG scores in the returns, such as a High-minus-Low Social or
Governance scores portfolio factors.

   6.  Conclusion

       Sustainability is an area of study which has faced an increased amount of research
and significance in the last decade. Its effect on the financial market has not been an
exception and therefore there is a plethora of literature not solely focusing on its
consequences on financial markets and economies, but also on how investors can drive
sustainability in companies and how ESG related issues are associated with returns. In
our study, we conclude that for the time period between 2003 and 2022 there are no major
significant effects of a Green-minus-Brown portfolio on the excess return of stocks.
Nevertheless, we found the effects of GMB on excess returns to be significant when we
excluded recessionary years from the 2003-2022 time period.

       In order to arrive at such a conclusion, we regressed the GMB portfolio on
existing portfolio factors and found no significant effect that was not already explained
by such factors. Moreover, when regressing returns on the GMB and existing factors, we
found no significant effect of ESG on excess returns. The same results also apply to
European stock data accordingly. Nevertheless, amidst economic expansions, the GMB
factor does carry a larger significance as the constant term is significant and captures
effects that are not explained by existing factors in literature.

                                     18
       Relating our findings to existing literature we are able to understand the
intricacies and differences between our findings and existing research in the area. The
main point of interest is that the clear difference in methodology generates different
results, that neither prove our findings nor debase previous ones. They instead point at
the intrinsic limitations of the study of portfolio factors and their usefulness in real life
investment strategies as well as the reality that there is the need for plenty of methods
and research areas to be further explored in order to fully grasp the effects of ESG on
investments.

       Finally, future research on the matter could involve delving deeper into the
different behaviors of the GMB portfolio in economic recessions, as well as further
exploring the potential differences between the European and American stock market
preferences  for and against sustainability, and the environment, social, and governance
factors separately.

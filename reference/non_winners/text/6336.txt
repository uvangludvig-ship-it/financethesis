Beyond Speculation:  Can Fundamental Blockchain Metrics Predict Token
Returns?

Abstract:
    We construct a three-factor model based on the cryptocurrency market, momen-
     tum, and transactions per user, which captures the cross-sectional returns of Layer 1
    blockchains’ native tokens. We replicate Liu, Wu, and Tsyvinski’s (2022) methodol-
     ogy on a more recent and delineated sample, and extend it to fundamental blockchain
     metrics. We construct zero-investment long-short portfolio strategies based on these
     characteristics, of which 26 are found to deliver statistically significant excess weekly
     returns. We show that all but two out of these strategies are explained by our three-
     factor model, and discuss potential explanations for the significance of a fundamental-
     based factor. Finally, we construct a market regime analysis and find a bull market
    e↵ect, which magnifies the excess weekly returns of all previously significant strate-
     gies.

Keywords:

                       I.    Introduction

The area of asset pricing is well-researched. In the literature, a fundamental question is
how di↵erent risk factors explain the returns of various asset classes. The Capital Asset
Pricing Model, commonly known as CAPM, argues for systematic risk exposure as the
driver of returns(Sharpe, (1965), Lintner, (1965)).   Fama and French (1992) critically
examine this model, and later expand this view by finding the additional significant risk
factors, size and value, and constructing a three-factor model(Fama and French, 1993).
Further research has broadened this area, with examples like Carhart (1997) adding the
factor of momentum as a return predictor, and Fama and French (2015) adding the factors
profitability and investment pattern to better predict stock returns. Pricing models for
other asset classes have been explored by the likes of Lustig, Roussanov, and Verdelhan
(2011) for currencies, and Szymanowska, De Roon, Nijman, and Van den Goorbergh
(2014) for commodities.
     Over the past decade, the emergence of public blockchains has given rise to a novel
class of digital-native assets that are fundamentally distinct from traditional equities,
bonds, or commodities, as discussed in the working paper by Krueckeberg and Scholzl
(2019). A blockchain is a distributed and cryptographically secured ledger that maintains
consensus over state transitions without the need for central intermediaries (Nakamoto,
2008).  Each public blockchain supports a native digital asset, such as Bitcoin (BTC),
Ethereum (ETH), or Solana (SOL), which serves multiple economic roles, from securing
the network to acting as the medium of exchange for execution and settlement (Buterin,
2014).  They represent real-time, permissionless financial systems built on open-source
infrastructure, capable of facilitating capital formation, value exchange, coordination,
and incentive alignment at internet scale (Nakamoto, 2008).
    Unlike traditional cash flow generating assets such as stocks or bonds, the valua-
tion of cryptocurrency assets has historically been approached di↵erently. Some models
emphasize network e↵ects and transactional utility (Cong, Li, and Wang, 2021), while
others focus on market risk factors and momentum.  Building on equity-based models,
Liu, Tsyvinski, and Wu (2022) apply a Fama-French-style framework to the cryptocur-
rency market.  Using 24 price-, volume-, and market-based factors derived from Chen
and Zimmermann (2019) and Feng, Giglio, and Xiu (2020), they found that 10 were
statistically significant, driven largely by size, momentum, and market risk, suggesting
a parallel to traditional equity asset pricing.   Liu et al.   (2022)’s selection methodol-
ogy, and basing their analysis on equity factors, safeguards against biases. However, in
only investigating factors based on price-, volume-, and market information, they neglect
the possible predictive power of fundamental cryptocurrency-specific metrics. Liu et al.
(2022) write that “The reason we focus on the market-based return predictors is that
financial and accounting data for the cross section of coins are either not readily available
or not applicable.”.
     Despite the growing significance and importance of this asset class, there is yet lim-
ited literature on the predictive power of fundamental metrics on cryptocurrency pricing,
reinforcing the rationale of our study.   Since Liu et al.   (2022) was published, crypto-

     Our data set ranges from January 2, 2015, to December 31, 2024, consisting of panel
data of 3 to 36 L1 tokens and a total of 65,820 observations.   With our delineation
of required available fundamental metric data, analysis e↵ectively starts on October 9,
2018, providing just over six years of useful observations. Our main data source is Token
Terminal, which provides the fundamental token metrics used in our analysis. Because of
our delineations, we have a smaller and more homogeneous data set than used in papers
such as Liu et al. (2022). However, the breadth of new data possibly provides for deeper
insights into the available observations and a chance to explore our research question.
     We replicate the methodology of Liu et al. (2022), largely based on Fama and French
(1993), and extend it to include our fundamental metrics. For each week, we sort tokens
cross-sectionally into quintiles based on the distribution of the selected metric. For each
quintile, we compute excess weekly returns using value-weighted averages, where weights
are determined by beginning-of-week market capitalization.   For each metric, we then
construct a zero-investment long-short portfolio that shorts the first quintile and longs
the fifth, to find strategies with significant excess weekly returns.  We then regress the
strategies on our risk factor models composed of one-, two-, and three factors to try to
explain their excess weekly return and exposure to each risk factor.  Also, we conduct
a market regime analysis to assess potential e↵ects on strategy excess weekly returns in
di↵erent market conditions, as well as a Fama-MacBeth regression to assess the robustness
of our significant strategies. The layout of our paper and formatting of tables is inspired
by Liu et al. (2022).
    Our first result confirms the strong contribution of price momentum in predicting
returns shown by Liu et al.   (2022), even when applied to our more delineated data
set.  The strategies based on one-, two-, three-, four-, one-to-four-, eight-, and 16-week
price momentum are highly statistically significant and yield a positive excess weekly
return. We see no predictive power in the size-related strategies, most likely due to size
homogeneity in our data set, and instead find significance in a volatility metric strat-

     This paper mainly relates to the asset pricing literature on factor zoos.   Earlier
work, particularly by Fama and French (1993) and, more recently, Feng et al.  (2020)
and Chen and Zimmermann (2019), has developed selection methodologies and tested a
large number of factors to explain equity returns. We build on this tradition by applying
similar factor logic to L1 blockchains’ native tokens.  While replicating parts of Liu et
al. (2022), we extend their approach by introducing fundamental blockchain variables as
return predictors. With a more delineated and up-to-date data set, we show that certain
momentum and volatility-based factors, and selected novel factors, also exhibit predictive
power.
    Our paper also contributes to the growing cryptocurrency asset pricing literature.
While prior studies, such as Liu et al. (2022), and extensions with their paper as foun-

Metelski and Sobieraj (2022) investigate the role of certain fundamentals in decentral-

of these fundamental metrics, particularly those capturing user engagement and activ-
ity momentum, have significant explanatory power and help increase the performance of
factor-based return models for cryptocurrencies.

for our fundamental metrics. In Section IV we describe our methodology in constructing
portfolios and regressions. In Section V we report our results, including those from the
market regime analysis and Fama-MacBeth regressions. In Section VI we discuss these
findings, and in Section Section VII we draw conclusions from our findings and discussion.

B.   Data Cleaning
Our data cleaning and adjustment process follows the same steps outlined in Liu et
al.   (2022).   For our extended analysis, we combine the market information data set
derived from CoinGecko, through Token Terminal, with the set of fundamental metrics
from Token Terminal.  For the data set, we solely choose Layer 1 blockchains’ tokens,
which have fundamental metrics available on Token Terminal, given that testing for their
significance is central to our main research questions.   We remove all entries with no
price, market capitalization, or volume data to ensure data completeness. Furthermore,
consistent with Liu et al.   (2022), we require a minimum market capitalization of  $1
million.   However, given the maturity of the L1 tokens included, no observations are
removed based on this criterion.
    To standardise our weekly returns, we create custom weeks, consistent with Liu et
al. (2022), dividing the year into 52 weeks, with seven-day weeks starting from January
                                                                                               18
1, where week 52 has eight days. In leap years, the final week of the year has nine days   .
For the risk-free rate, we use the daily one-month U.S. Treasury bill rate. On days when
the markets are closed, when daily one-month U.S. Treasury bill rates are unavailable,
we forward-fill using the most recent available rate, similarly to Liu et al. (2022). If a
token starts trading on a day without an available daily one-month U.S. Treasury bill
rate, we backfill using the next available observation. This adjustment accounts for the
fact that cryptocurrency markets operate continuously, 24 hours per day and seven days
per week.

C.   Data Limitations
The main limitation of our data set is the narrow selection of data used, compared to
studies such as Liu et al. (2022). Although our delineation is rationalised by data rel-
evance, availability, and comparability, a broader sample would imply a more robust
empirical foundation. With a small sample size, any outliers exert more influence, poten-
tially distorting summary measures, and the test has low power, so the chance of a Type
                   19
II error is higher   The data selection poses some additional downsides.  For example,
direct comparability with the publication that inspired this study (Liu et al., 2022) is
greatly reduced, as their sample stretches from 2014 to 2020 and includes a maximum of
1827 coins. With this, we risk not representing all L1 blockchains, nor the cryptocurrency
market as a whole, and thus being unable to draw significantly applicable conclusions.
We have low variable coverage for some fundamental metrics of our data set, displayed
in Appendix I, due to the previously discussed lack of requirements for reporting and
standardizing this data. We consider the e↵ects of this fact more in Section VI.
    Certain biases are potentially present in the data selection process.   For one, we
choose a data set for which we logically expect compatibility with our intended analysis,

        III.    Cross-sectional Return Predictors

A.   Price-, Volume-, and Market-based Return Predictors
Our price-, volume-, and market-based return predictors are inspired by Liu et al. (2022),
which in turn were derived from Chen and Zimmermann (2019) and Feng et al. (2020).
The rationale for using these factors as return predictors for cryptocurrencies is that,
while originally applied in equity pricing models, they rely solely on price-, volume-, and
market data, and can therefore be e↵ectively applied to cryptocurrencies as well.  The
factors and their respective categories and definitions can be found in Appendix III.

B.   Price-, Volume-, and Market Variable Construction
For the first part of our analysis, we replicate the methodology of Liu et al.   (2022).
Like Liu et al. (2022), we define the daily excess return by adjusting the daily return of
a token to the daily risk-free rate, for which we use the one-month US Treasury yield.
We calculate the excess weekly return by summing the daily returns over each custom
week.   We also define a cryptocurrency excess market return (CMKT) as the market
capitalization-weighted average excess return across all tokens.
     For the size-factors market capitalization and last-day price, we use the final values
of the custom week, whereas for the maximum daily price, we use the maximum price
during that week. We then transform these factors logarithmically. With the age factor,
we count the days from the first appearance, indicating the maturity of the blockchain.
This means that the blockchains older than our data set get their age factor determined
from the first day of the data set. In our data set, this only applies to Bitcoin, Litecoin,
and Dogecoin, which were launched prior to January 1, 2015.
     Likewise, we transform the volume factors logarithmically. The volume is calculated
as the mean daily number of trades during the custom week. We define price volume as
the weekly average of the daily USD volume.  We expand this with volume scaled, by
dividing the daily USD volume by the daily market capitalization, before calculating the
weekly average, further standardizing the volume analysis.
    We base the volatility factors on daily price movements and calculate them over a
seven-day rolling window.   With the return volatility-factor, we measure the standard
deviation of the daily logarithmic returns, and with the maximum return-factor, we
capture the maximum single-day log return during each rolling window. We compute our

                                      8
beta-metric and idiosyncratic volatility by using rolling one-year windows of daily returns.
For each token, we regress daily returns on CMKT over the past 365 days. This means
that the first 365 days that the token exists in our data set, it does not have the beta
and idiosyncratic volatility factors. The beta-metric is calculated as the estimated slope
coe cient, capturing its systematic market exposure.  We use the standard deviation
of the regression residuals to compute the idiosyncratic volatility. This method follows
Liu et al.  (2022) to isolate common market risk from idiosyncratic noise.  To measure
di↵erences in the lagged returns, we construct a delay variable.   For each token, we
estimate two regressions over a rolling 365-day window:  (i) a simple model regressing
daily excess returns on CMKT, and (ii) a full model regressing daily excess returns on
                                                                                   2
CMKT and lagged market returns. We define delay as the di↵erence in  R  between the
full and simple models.   Delay indicates that lagged market returns explain a greater
share of current returns, implying slower price adjustment and potential informational
frictions.   Furthermore, with the standard deviation of price volume, we measure the
standard deviation of the daily USD volume during the week.  We construct Amihud
illiquidity, which works as a proxy for illiquidity, by dividing the absolute values of the
daily excess return by the daily trading volume in USD, then averaging across the custom
week. We do not logarithmically transform it, in line with how Liu et al. (2022) define
the factor, based on Amihud (2002).
    We construct the price momentum factors by measuring past cumulative logarithmic
returns over di↵erent horizons, as defined by Liu et al.  (2022).  One-week momentum
is defined as the logarithmic return over the past 7 days, two-week momentum over 14
days, three-week momentum over 21 days, four-week momentum over 28 days, eight-week
momentum over 56 days, 16-week momentum over 112 days, 50-week momentum over
350 days, and up to 100-week momentum over 700 days.   We define one-to-four-week
momentum as the return from weeks 2-4 before the current week. This excludes the most
recent week, meaning analyzing the logarithmic returns between day 28 and 7 prior to
the day in question, following how Liu et al. (2022) define the factor. The momentum
signals allow us to understand patterns and sort the tokens into portfolios based on past
performance patterns.

C.   Fundamental Cryptocurrency Return Predictors
When evaluating what fundamental variables to include in our extended analysis, our
starting point is assessing what data is readily available.   For some metrics, we can
draw direct parallels to existing and demonstrably significant equity asset pricing factors,
much like when applying the price-, volume-, and market-based metrics. For our absolute
fundamental metrics, we can compare the fee, revenue, supply-side fee, and incentive of a
protocol to the gross income, gross profit, (part of) operating expense, and issuance of an
equity, respectively. The daily-, weekly-, and monthly active users of a blockchain, that
is, how many unique wallet addresses transact in each respective time period, serve as
proxies for network activity. Cong et al. (2021) develop a model in which the equilibrium
token price is determined by aggregated transactional demand, which is inherently tied
to the level of user adoption and network activity. Transaction count and transactions
per second also tie into network usage and adoption.

an analogue to the return on equity of a stock.   Our incentive ratio, defined as token
incentives divided by user fees, parallels the strategy used by early-stage marketplace
companies, which provide financial incentives to one or more stakeholder groups to accel-
erate network growth (Howcroft and Leaver, 2024). The number of transactions per daily,
weekly, or monthly active user on a blockchain can serve as a proxy for user engagement.
Higher engagement may reflect greater utility, usage intensity, and possibly stronger user
retention. These metrics o↵er a way to quantify platform activity in ways that investors
might interpret as indicators of quality. This connects to the idea put forward by Asness,
Frazzini, and Pedersen (2019), who argue that investors are willing to pay more for assets
with quality characteristics.  Similarly, Cong et al.  (2021) emphasize that token value

                                      10
is driven by aggregate transactional demand, which is directly linked to user activity on
the network. Revenue per daily-, weekly-, and monthly user is profitability-related and
indicates value added.
    Our fundamental momentum metrics aim to capture how the market responds to
changes in cryptocurrency fundamentals. This approach draws on the parallel with stan-
dardized unexpected earnings (SUE) in equity markets, as discussed by Hou, Xue, and
Zhang (2015), who show that earnings momentum is reflected in a return on equity fac-
tor. Given that fundamental data in the cryptocurrency market is reported at a much
higher frequency than in equities(real-time versus quarterly), we adapt the traditional
framework to slightly shorter time horizons more appropriate for the crypto domain. All
chosen fundamental metric-based return predictor definitions are reported in Table II.

D.   Fundamental Variable Construction
We construct the fundamental variables by aggregating the data for each custom week.
We summarize the logarithmic amount for transaction count, fees, supply-side fees, rev-
enue, and incentives, respectively, for the entire custom week, to get the weekly total
logarithmic value for each factor.   Average fee, median fee, daily active users, weekly
active users, and monthly active users, on the other hand, are defined as the average
logarithmic value for the week, as these factors cannot be cumulative. The transactions
per second factor is also constructed as the mean value for the week, however, without
transforming it logarithmically due to the nature of the already derived metric.
    To further analyze the fundamental metrics, we introduce the fundamental momen-
tum factors that capture short- to medium-term changes in fundamentals, analogous to
the price momentum we have analyzed previously.  For each fundamental variable, we
compute logarithmic di↵erences over one-, two-, three-, four-, eight-, and 16 weeks. As
an illustration, one-week transaction count momentum measures the one-week logarith-
mic change in transaction count, and four-week fees momentum measures the four-week
change in fees collected.
    To better understand the potential economic drivers of fundamental factor valua-
tions, we construct derived metrics based on the underlying fundamental variables. We
calculate the revenue yield as the weekly logarithmic revenue divided by the logarithmic
end-of-week market capitalization. The incentive ratio is calculated as the weekly incen-
tives divided by the weekly fees. An incentive ratio of over 1 indicates that the blockchain
issues more incentives to the miners or validators who validate the blockchain than users
are willing to pay in fees to interact with the blockchain. Furthermore, we analyze the
transactions and revenue relative to the users, both factors on the average daily-, weekly-,
and monthly average active users during the week. We winsorize all derived metrics to
reduce the influence of potential outliers.

                  IV.   Methodology

A.   Portfolio Construction
Following the methodology of Liu et al. (2022), we construct long-short portfolios based
on the return predictors described in Section III. At the beginning of each custom week,

                                      11
we extract the relevant characteristic signals for each token. We then sort tokens cross-
sectionally into quintiles based on the distribution of the selected signal. We perform the
sorting in ascending order: the first quintile (Q1) contains tokens with the lowest values
of the characteristic, and the fifth quintile (Q5) those with the highest.
    For each quintile, we compute excess weekly returns using value-weighted averages,
where weights are determined by market capitalization at the beginning of the week. This
weighting approach ensures that larger tokens, which carry greater economic significance,
have a proportionally larger impact on the portfolio returns within the quintile portfolio.
We then construct long-short portfolios by taking the return di↵erence between the high-
est and lowest quintiles. For most factors, the long-short return is defined as the return
for tokens in the fifth quintile minus the return of the tokens in the first quintile, that
is, we long the tokens in the fifth quintile and short the tokens in the first quintile. We
compute all long-short returns on a weekly basis, matched to the custom calendar week
structure described in Section II.
     For variables based on rolling volatility measures, such as return volatility and max-
imum return, we separately construct portfolios to properly align the rolling daily mea-
sures with the custom weekly calendar. This ensures that volatility-based portfolios reflect
stable week-level information and are not unduly a↵ected by daily noise. Also, liquidity-
related variables, such as Amihud illiquidity, where lower values represent higher liquidity,
the interpretation is inverted to maintain economic consistency in long-short construction.
Hence, we then define the long-short portfolio as the return for tokens in the first quintile
minus the return of the tokens in the fifth quintile to align with theoretical expectations
of return patterns.

B.   Summary Statistics of Long-short Portfolios
After constructing the zero-investment long-short portfolios, we compute descriptive
statistics to assess their performance and underlying characteristics.   For each portfo-
lio, we calculate the mean excess weekly return and standard deviation of returns.
     To account for serial correlation in weekly returns, we estimate the standard error of
the mean return using the Newey and West (1987) heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-
consistent estimator. We choose a lag length of 21 days (equivalent to three weeks) to
reflect the fact that the crypto market operates seven days a week, making it roughly
comparable to a standard trading month in traditional markets. Based on this adjust-
ment, we perform a one-sample t-test to evaluate whether the average long-short return
is statistically di↵erent from zero.  We report the Newey-West adjusted t-statistic and
corresponding significance levels for each portfolio.   The summary statistics are based
on the full sample of available weeks after portfolio construction, with missing weeks
dropped to maintain a consistent time series across all portfolios.

C.   Fama-MacBeth Cross-sectional Regression
To evaluate the cross-sectional predictive power of our return predictors, in addition to the
time series and long-short portfolio analysis, we implement the two-step Fama-MacBeth
(1973) regression methodology. This approach estimates the average relationship between
weekly token returns and lagged predictor variables, while accounting for both cross-
sectional and time series variation in returns.

                                      12
     First, we run cross-sectional regressions of excess weekly returns on predictor vari-
ables for each custom week. To improve robustness and comparability across assets, we
transform all predictor variables into ranked quintiles (1-5) within each week, following
the approach used by Liu et al. (2022). For each predictor and week, we include observa-
tions only when the weekly return and the predictor value are non-missing, the number of
valid tokens is at least ten, and there is more than one unique rank within the week. This
gives a weekly series of betas for each predictor, capturing its cross-sectional relationship
with weekly returns.
     We then analyze the time series of the estimated betas from the cross-sectional re-
gressions. The average of these betas represents the overall sensitivity of returns to the
predictor variable across the sample period.   To account for autocorrelation and het-
eroskedasticity in the beta series, we apply Newey and West (1987) standard errors with
a lag length of three weeks, consistent with previous sections.   We apply this proce-
dure to 26 variables previously identified as statistically significant at the 5% level in
our long-short portfolio analysis. In addition to the univariate regressions, we also run
joint multivariate Fama-MacBeth regressions within each panel to test whether the set
of signals, considered jointly, has explanatory power. These joint regressions use the full
vector of ranked predictors in the panel and follow the same two-step structure with
Newey-West inference.

factor (CSMB) introduced by Liu et al. (2022), because we did not find significance in

F.   Market Regime Analysis
As an additional analysis, to evaluate the possible e↵ects on factor portfolio returns across
di↵erent market states, we classify each week into one of three market regimes: bull, bear,
or neutral. This regime-based analysis allows us to investigate whether the performance
of cryptocurrency return factors is state-dependent, much like Cooper, Gutierrez, and
Hameed (2004) do for momentum strategies for equities.  While Cooper et al.  (2004)
examine a 66-year horizon in traditional asset classes, we suggest that similar patterns
could appear over much shorter periods in the highly volatile and fast-moving cryptocur-
rency market. With this as a starting point, we attempt to construct a set of rules that
adequately classify market regimes that capture the trends in the market, while also
keeping the volatile nature of cryptocurrencies in mind.
    To begin, we construct a daily cryptocurrency market index as the value-weighted
average of individual token returns, using beginning-of-day market capitalizations as
weights.  We initialize the index at a base value of 100 at the start of the sample, and
it evolves according to the cumulative product of daily returns.  To smooth short-term
fluctuations and identify underlying trends, we compute a 100-day moving average of the
market index. We determine the daily regime by comparing the level of the market index
to its moving average.

more than 5%, while a bear market occurs when the index falls more than 5% below the
moving average.  We define neutral markets as periods when the index remains within
±5% of its moving average.
    Given the high volatility of cryptocurrency markets, temporary noise could other-
wise cause excessive regime switching.   To address this, we apply a persistence filter,
where a regime label must persist for at least 30 consecutive days to be considered valid.
Short-lived regime labels are reassigned to the previous valid regime, ensuring stability
in classification and better capturing true market trends.
    We then map daily regimes to our custom weeks through a modal approach, by
taking the most frequent regime observed during each week. As a result, we assign each
unique custom week to one of the three regimes:  bull, bear, or neutral.   The division
between the three regimes is visualized in Appendix V.
     After categorizing each week in a regime, we analyze the behavior of each constructed
long-short portfolio within each market regime separately. Importantly, we construct the
portfolios themselves independently of the regime definition. We do not re-sort tokens or
reconstruct the portfolios based on regime. Rather, we condition the already constructed
long-short returns on the prevailing market regime in each week.

    Furthermore, during bull markets, all price-, volume-, and market-based strategies
see increases in mean excess weekly return, ranging from 0.02% to 0.82%, all significant at

                     VI.   Discussion

Similarly to Liu et al. (2022), we use 24 equity return predictors as the basis for our cryp-
tocurrency model, but with a considerably more delineated data set consisting only of
Layer 1 native tokens. Our major finding with this comparison is the lack of significance
in the size-related strategies. Micro- and small-cap coins are a driving force in Liu et al.
(2022), and small coins’ excess return can thus be categorized by a CSMB(cryptocurrency
small-minus-big)-factor. By excluding the smaller tokens in our sample, we compress our
cross-sectional spread, minimize the illiquidity and delisting risk usually present in that
segment, and essentially take the size factor out of our equation.   This is not an in-
tended outcome in our process, but rather an expected and necessary choice to allow for
other analyses. The absence of significant size e↵ects may also help explain the weaker
performance of absolute fundamental strategies in our study, as larger tokens, typically

                                      27
associated with higher fees, revenues, and activity.  A clear finding is that momentum
strategies are still an important aspect in explaining excess returns, mostly in line with
the replicated study.   Contrastingly, we also find the one-to-four-, eight, and 16-week
momentum strategies significant, indicating a more persistent momentum e↵ect for the
larger tokens in our data set.  However, in our Fama-MacBeth regression, we see that
the momentum strategies are individually statistically significant, but not jointly for the
eight- and 16-week portfolios, showing that these factors are correlated.  This is partly
in line with Liu et al. (2022) finding that strategies for coins with a market cap over 10
million USD are individually significant(with the exception of one-week momentum), but
none of their momentum strategies are jointly significant. The significance of the maxi-
mum return strategy, also confirmed in the Fama-MacBeth analysis, shows the influence
of volatility in predicting expected returns. We see this strategy highly significantly ex-
posed to market-, momentum-, and transactions per user betas in our three-factor model.
While it may seem intuitive that higher transactions per user lead to network congestion
and thus rising fees, potentially dampening usage and negatively impacting returns, this
relationship does not appear to hold for Layer 1 blockchains.  Instead, our results sug-
gest that a higher transaction count per user is associated with positive future returns.
One possible explanation is that this metric may proxy for deeper user engagement or
greater application-level utility. Additionally, since each transaction must be paid in the
native token, increased activity could raise transactional demand for the asset, poten-
tially supporting its price. These factors may outweigh the frictional costs of higher fees,
particularly in networks with scalable infrastructure or dynamic fee markets. As such, we
interpret the observed positive returns not as an anomaly, but as potentially reflecting
demand-driven network value and investor recognition of long-term utility.
    As expected, our zero-investment long-short strategies based on absolute fundamen-
tal metrics do not yield significant results. An equity equivalent example would be using
revenue as a stock return predictor, which inherently does not make sense. However, for
the relative metrics, we see transaction count per daily-, weekly-, and monthly user as
significantly yielding positive returns.  A possible explanation for this could be a high
transaction count per user, meaning high user-intensity, being regarded as a quality eq-
uity factor, as discussed by Asness et al.  (2019), and thus being priced at a premium.
However, our Fama-Macbeth regression findings show that these strategies are neither
individually nor jointly significant. This could be a result of poor variable coverage, as
shown in Appendix I and Appendix II. Our other fundamental relative metric strategies
also yield insignificant results, pointing against their potential usage in token pricing as
parallels to equity pricing models. For our strategies based on fundamental momentum
metrics, we see some indicating possible predictive power.  Testing robustness through
Fama-Macbeth regression, we see nine strategies being independently significant and only
three jointly, the two- and three-week monthly active users momentum strategies, and
the 16-week supply-side fee momentum strategy.   These are all captured best by our
three-factor model, being explained mostly by the momentum- and transactions per user
factors. Intuitively, monthly active users momentum strategies overlap and should thus
correlate heavily, but this finding somewhat disproves that.  As previously mentioned,
the successful fundamental momentum strategies could be analogous to equities’ post-
earnings announcement drift e↵ect, as Hou, Xue, and Zhang (2015) describe. However,
with no clear pattern in the type of factor momentum, or time horizon, no such conclu-
sions can e↵ectively be drawn.

                                      28
     Our market regime results show that bull markets enhance the excess weekly return of
price momentum strategies. In bear markets, we see significance in short-term momentum
strategies as well, but with lower excess returns compared to bull markets, with the
exception of one-week momentum.  These findings underscore the significance of short-
term momentum e↵ects in both bull- and bear markets. In both bull- and bear markets,
we find significance-anomalies in absolute- and relative fundamental metrics, which we
do not see in the previous analysis.  As we do not perform the Fama-MacBeth test on
these variables, nor regress them on our factor model, we cannot explain these findings
e↵ectively.   In practice, regime-dependent heterogeneity cannot be e↵ectively proven,
apart from the conclusion that some strategies’ significance and returns increase in bull
markets.   This can be interpreted as meaning that bull market movements are more
predictable. However, our definition of market regimes categorizes a majority of observed
weeks as in bull markets, which could explain its superior significance.
    Our three-factor model increases the explanatory power compared to the one- and
two-factor models and captures all significant strategies’ alphas except for the one- and
two-week price momentum. It is unclear what prevents CMOM from capturing this short-
term price momentum.  With some strategies only being incrementally more explained
than by the other models, we have to consider the aspect of overfitting. Further analysis
is required to fully understand the drivers of predictive power through the CTPU factor.
     As a result of our narrow delineation to a relatively homogenous data set, there is
little external validity in applying these conclusions to a broader cryptocurrency context
than large L1s. Also, poor variable coverage for some fundamental metrics weakens our
ability to draw broader, definitive conclusions. Being that these metrics have not been
available for very long, our analyzed time period is shorter than desired.   To better
understand the correlation between factors and assess the robustness of our model, we
could have also performed a principal component analysis, as done by Liu et al. (2022).
To guard against omitted variable bias due to selection mistakes, we could expand by
adopting a double-selection LASSO strategy, as proposed by Feng et al.   (2022), and
introduced by Belloni, Chernozhukov, and Hansen (2014). Furthermore, an aspect Liu et
al. (2022) bring up is that not all tokens might be available to sell short, thus decreasing
the real-life applicability of the long-short strategies. With our smaller data set and larger
tokens, we think that this argument is less prevalent. However, we could strengthen our
findings by replacing short-selling the relevant quintile portfolio with shorting a more
publicly available token, such as Bitcoin.

                    VII.   Conclusion

This paper investigates the pricing of Layer 1 blockchain tokens by applying equity-
style factor models alongside novel fundamental blockchain metrics.   Replicating the
methodology of Liu et al. (2022) with a more delineated data set, we find that momentum
remains a key token return predictor, with especially strong returns in short-term horizons
and during bull markets.   In contrast, we find no evidence of a size premium, likely
attributable to our exclusion of smaller tokens. Our analysis also highlights the predictive
power of select fundamental metric strategies, particularly those related to user activity,
user activity momentum, and transaction count momentum, though results are mixed
across methodologies.  We conclude that a higher transaction count per user correlates
with higher returns, which we explain as a potential e↵ect of higher transactional demand,

                                      29
and deeper user engagement and utility. The significance of the maximum return strategy
further underscores the relevance of volatility in explaining cross-sectional returns.
    While our three-factor model, incorporating market-, momentum-, and transaction
per user factors, captures a large part of the excess returns, some short-term momentum
strategies remain partially unexplained. Overall, our findings contribute to the growing
literature on cryptocurrency asset pricing by emphasizing the relevance of select funda-
mental metrics and market conditions.   However, limitations in variable coverage and
sample size restrict the generalizability of our conclusions beyond large L1s. Future re-
search would benefit from broader data sets, improved coverage of fundamental variables,
and complementary techniques such as principal component analysis to capture under-
lying patterns in return predictors. With the current development and expansion of the
blockchain- and cryptocurrency markets, this is something we expect to see soon.

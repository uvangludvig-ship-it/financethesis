  Short Selling Bans: An Effective Tool in

              Times of Crisis?

                  Raymond Hedberg*  and  Tim Paulsson**

                             Bachelor Thesis  in Finance
                         Stockholm School of Economics

                                May 15, 2013

                   ABSTRACT

Short selling has been heavily debated and subject to controversy throughout  history. Today, one of
the most common ways to handle market turmoil is to ban short selling, even though it is doubtful
that the desired effects are achieved.  In our study we investigate the effectiveness of the  recent  ban on
covered  short selling in Spain.  By using matching techniques and comparing Spanish  stocks to other
European  stocks  we find that the ban  (i) supported stock prices and (ii) was detrimental for market
quality.  We do not find evidence that the negative effects of the inception of the ban are  reversed
when the ban is lifted.  Therefore regulators should  carefully consider the trade-off between supporting
prices and sustaining market quality.

Key Words:  short selling, ban, crisis, regulation

1. Introduction

                                  th
In  the beginning of the 17  century  a group of investors,  led by merchant Isaac Le

Maire,  contracted  to  short  sell  shares  of  the  Dutch  East  India  Company.  The

investors speculated in a substantial price decline and  to help put pressure on the

stock price  they  started  spreading  negative rumours about the company.  Once  other

investors and the Dutch regulator discovered Le Maire’s practice  they were  outraged

and there were calls for regulation, which  ultimately lead  to  the world’s first  ban on

short selling  being  put in place  a year later  (van Dillen, J.G., Poitras,  G. and

Majithia, A. (2006)). Since Le Maire’s  short selling scheme  in 1609  short sellers have

been   under   scrutiny   by   regulators   and   repeatedly   accused   for   creating   or

exacerbating financial crises and  times of  panic.

      Throughout history and  particularly  during the  years following the  financial

crisis  in  2008-2009,  there  has  been  a  persistent  debate  about  short  selling  and

whether  or  not  short  sellers  are  harmful  to  other  investors,  if  short  sellers  can

contribute to  causing  financial crises  or if short sellers actually have a justifiable role

in the market. Despite  a number of  studies having been made on the  subject,  results

and interpretations  of the studies  are somewhat unclear.  Even though  studies,  like

the  US government  Pilot Study in  2007,  recommend  a relaxation of short selling

restrictions,  regulators  around the world  still implement  bans on short selling  to deal

with financial crises and stock market crashes.  In the light of a statement that  the

former SEC  chairman,  Christopher Cox,  made after the  financial crisis in 2008, this

is particularly  interesting:

”’While the actual effects of this temporary action will not be fully understood for

many more months, if not years, knowing what we know now, I believe on balance

the commission would not do it  again," Cox told Reuters in a telephone interview

                                                                                                   3
from the SEC's Los Angeles office late on Tuesday. "The costs appear to outweigh

the benefits.’” (Younglai, R. (2008))

Evidently  the  subject  of  short  selling  is  a  practice  that  is  heavily  debated  and

politically sensitive. As we shall see in the following sections, even among academics

there are some discrepancies  in the views of what the actual effects of short selling

are.  By studying the ban on short selling in Spain,  put in place on July 23  2012  and

lifted on January 31 2013,  we hope to shed some light on matters  where previous

studies’ results have been somewhat  ambiguous.

       In our study we analyse the  effects  on returns and market quality  caused by

the short selling ban  on  Spanish equities.  We have  chosen  a set  of  stocks  from the

Spanish,  French,  Portuguese  and  the  Italian  markets.  The  Spanish  stocks  are

matched  with similar  stocks from the other  countries  so that  we have a treatment

group of 34  Spanish  stocks and a control group  consisting of  34  stocks from the other

markets.  By comparing the two groups, before, during and after the ban, we analyse

the effectiveness of the short selling ban as a government intervention.

       Several of the previous studies on  short selling bans  have dealt with bans that

were put in place during the financial crisis in 2008-2009, mainly concerning  financial

stocks.  The previous research shows similar results regarding the effects on market

quality. However,  when it comes to the effect on returns there  are  not so  clear

conclusions. Some of the studies point to no or little impact on stock returns,  while

others point to an  initial impact that is later reversed.  Several  studies  concern  the

financial stocks in the USA during the financial crisis in 2008, and they  point to a

positive impact on stock returns.  One of the main difficulties in the  studies  that deal

with financial stocks during the crisis  is to  isolate the effects of the ban. Bans on

short selling of financial stocks  have  often coincided  with  other  interventions  such as

TARP  and QE.  This makes it difficult to sort out what is actually driving returns in

                                                                                                   4
these stocks; is it the ban itself or is it other  government interventions?  Furthermore,

if all the financial stocks are banned and if they are the ones who benefit the most

from government interventions,  it may be difficult to find adequate stocks to use as

control group.

       In contrast to  previous studies, our research deals with data from a recent

short selling ban on all types of stocks in Spain, i.e. not only financial stocks. The

observed time period  should  be less distorted by government interventions  and in

combination with a  treatment group  that consists of different types of stocks we

believe that  the impact of the short selling ban can be effectively isolated.

       Our study  presents  evidence that the ban on short selling in Spain  supports

stock  prices, both in the short-term and in the long-term.  This finding  confirms the

results of some of the  previous  empirical studies  (see e.g. Chang, Cheng and Yu

(2007)  and  Bris,  Goetzmann  &  Zhu  (2007)).  At  the  same  time  we  have  found

evidence  that  points  to  a  clear  loss  of  market  quality  when  incepting  the  ban.

Trading volume, both measured in number of shares and  in  turnover in  euro,  is lost

and  doesn’t recover  even after the ban is lifted. These findings differ from what has

been seen in previous studies.  The implication of  our study  is that there is some

merit to the argument that one wants to support prices by implementing a ban on

short selling. However, the supportive effect on prices comes with high cost and

regulators should therefore carefully consider the trade-off between supporting prices

and sustaining market quality.  Especially since we do not find evidence that the

negative effects of the inception of the ban are reversed when the ban is lifted.

2. Historical Background

As mentioned in the introduction, short selling is a practice that has been questioned

and  lively debated throughout history. To fully understand the  context of short

selling  it is necessary to make a short review of the history of short selling  bans  and

other  government interventions.

2.1 A History of Controversy

To continue from Le Maire and the first ban on short selling,  we  now  turn to the US,

where  there has been a long history of debate and controversy regarding short selling.

By the time of the financial crisis in 1929 short sellers were blamed for causing the

stock market crash, and in 1932 president Herbert Hoover condemned short sellers,

saying that stock prices did not represent “true values”:

“The pounding of prices to a basis of earnings by obvious manipulation of the

market and propaganda that values should be based on earnings at the bottom of a

depression is an injury to the country  and to the investing public”  (Rothbard, M. N.

(2000)).

A couple of years later, after Hoover’s critique of short sellers and after a rapid stock

market decline in 1937, the SEC decided to take action and hereby adopted the so-

called “Uptick Rule” or Rule  10a-1. The rule explicitly prohibited investors from

entering into short positions when stock prices were declining; you were only allowed

to short when prices were rising.

      Since   1938   congress   and   the   SEC   have   had   continuous   debates   and

investigations regarding the usefulness  of short selling and the effectiveness  of bans

on short selling.  In 1976 e.g., an investigation by congress concluded that it would

probably be necessary to temporary suspend Rule 10a-1 to be able to gather enough

                                                                                                   6
statistical evidence  of the long-  and short-term effects of short selling. Therefore

congress proposed a temporary suspension of Rule 10a-1.  However,  this initiative was

met with scepticism and negative comments by third parties that were invited to

discuss the idea.  The NYSE  voiced some potentially negative effects of a suspension

of the Rule 10a-1:

(1) wider  day-to-day price fluctuations;

(2)  disadvantages  for  public  customers  who  could  not  withdraw  limit  orders  to

purchase before market professionals sold short;

(3) accelerated price declines and increased volatility;

(4) distortions in the markets for secondary and tertiary stocks; and

(5) impaired market liquidity because block positioners would be discouraged from

taking positions.  (Katz, G. (1999))

Due to the negative sentiments expressed in the comments, neither congress nor the

SEC saw any need to take action regarding short selling at the time being. However,

since 1976, as trading strategies evolved and technical leaps forward were made,

there was a steadily growing concern that the Rule 10a-1 was becoming out-dated

and that the purpose it once served was no longer fulfilled. In 2004 congress therefore

decided it was time to do a proper study of  the effects of short selling  to see if the

Rule 10a-1 still had the intended effect on stock markets. The Office for Economic

Analysis was asked to perform a Pilot Study, analysing the effects of a temporary

suspension  of  the  Rule  10a-1.  In  short,  the  report  found  that  stocks  that  were

exempted from the short selling restriction were no more likely to suffer from rapid

price declines (or “bear raids”) than the other control stocks.  In general the Pilot

Study supported a removal of the Rule 10a-1, thereby prompting the SEC to remove

the rule in 2007  (Taylor, J. L. (2007)).

2.2 The Financial Crisis in 2008

During  the  financial  crisis  in  2008-2009  global  stock  markets  experienced  high

volatility and rapid price declines. After the deterioration of the subprime mortgage

industry in the USA in 2007, more  and more people began to question the valuations

on the stock market and the stability of the financial institutions that were involved

in the mortgage business.  The loss of investor confidence and the rapid stock price

declines led regulators around the  world to take measures to try to stem the crisis.

Despite the relatively recent Pilot Study and suspension of the Rule 10a-1, the SEC

and its chairman, Christopher Cox, took action in September 2008 and declared a

ban on short selling of financial stocks  (Harmon, F. E. (2008)).  At the same time

similar measures where taken in several other countries such as: Britain, Australia,

Canada, Germany, Taiwan, Portugal and Ireland  (Trotta, D. (2008)).  Christopher

Cox and the SEC commented on the ban on short selling:

“Under normal market conditions, short selling contributes to price efficiency and

adds liquidity to the markets. At present, it appears that unbridled short selling is

contributing   to   the   recent,   sudden   price   declines   in   the   securities   of   financial

institutions unrelated to true price valuation. Financial institutions are particularly

vulnerable to this crisis of confidence and panic selling because they depend on the

confidence of their trading counterparties in the conduct of their core business.”

(SEC Press Release (2008))

Just like Herbert Hoover in 1932, Christopher Cox and the SEC asserted that the

valuations by the market were incorrect and that stocks should be valued higher.

While mentioning some positive aspects of short selling, i.e. added liquidity and

higher price efficiency, the SEC concluded that the potential benefits of a ban on

short selling of financial stocks would outweigh the potential negative side-effects.

                                                                                                   8
This is an interesting statement, given that no such official market study  of a

complete ban on short selling had been done before and particularly since the Pilot

Study in 2007 supported a removal of the Rule 10a-1. What if the SEC and other

regulators were wrong in their analysis that short sellers contributed to the price

declines? What if the price declines were actually taking place regardless of short

selling constraints, but because of a fundamental change in the value of the

securities? Another question that arises is how the SEC, and Herbert Hoover for that

matter, could  see it fit to judge the lower asset prices to be “unrelated to true price

valuation”, when the whole market was implying otherwise.

3. Previous Literature

3.1 Theory

3.1.1  Effect on  Returns

According to the theories  by Miller (1977) a ban on short  selling  would lead to

higher prices. By putting a constraint on short  selling,  bearish traders who do not

own the stock are excluded from the market. This will lead to a bias towards buying

and the asset prices will rise. This means that, given that  assets  were priced correctly

before the ban, assets will be overpriced when short  selling is banned. However,

prices  are predicted to  go down again when the ban is lifted. Miller’s theories are

based on the assumption that the beliefs of the investors are heterogeneous.

      The theories of Diamond and Verrecchia (1987) predict a different result.

Their theories are based on rational expectations instead of heterogeneous beliefs.

This means that when short  sellers are excluded from the market, investors  will take

this into account and therefore adjust their positions to compensate for short  sellers

negative information not being incorporated. As a result, in average assets will not

be overpriced  when prices reach equilibrium.

       More recent theories by Bai,  Chang and Wang (2006) do not assume risk-

neutrality as Diamond and Verrecchia did. Instead they base their theories on risk-

averse   investors.   However,   Bai,   Chang   and   Wang   also   consider   a   rational

expectations equilibrium model. Due to their theories, excluding the short  sellers

increases  the risk  for investors since the short  sellers’  private negative information  is

no longer incorporated in prices. Thereby  uninformed investors will require a higher

risk premium,  subsequently  causing  lower prices. However,  restricting short  sales for

hedging purposes decreases the selling side of the market without excluding any

negative information.  Consequently, this  is predicted to result  in increasing prices.

                                                                                                   10
The net effect of Bai, Chang and Wang’s theories depends on the mix of short sales

driven by private information and short sales  driven by  risk sharing.

3.1.2 Effect on  Market Quality

When a ban is implemented, some  of the traders are  sidelined, and the intuitive

effect  would  be  a  decreased  trading  volume.  However,  due  to  the  theories  of

Diamond and  Verrecchia  (1987) the investors will need to reallocate their asset

portfolio. This could provide the market with an initial boost in liquidity.  Diamond

and  Verrecchia  also find that restrictions on short  selling slow  down price discovery

(other  examples  of  studies  supporting  this  finding:  Saffi  and  Sigurdsson  (2010),

Boehmer and Wu  (2010)).  Revealing “true” prices  will take  longer time  and  the

uncertainty will lead to higher bid-ask spreads. Their theories predict that  the largest

possible spread will be direct after the restriction and will slowly decrease as trading

goes on and more  information gets incorporated.

       Beber  and  Pagano  (2013)  emphasize  that  market  makers  will  have  an

increased inventory cost when they are not allowed to  sell  short  and  thereby are

forced to  hold a larger number of shares. Increased bid-ask spreads will be a way for

them to compensate for the higher costs. If market makers get an exception from the

ban restriction this will lead to an advantage over  other liquidity suppliers, which

will allow them to increase the bid-ask spreads.

       The  effect  of  higher  bid-ask  spreads  when  short  selling  is  banned  is  also

supported by the theories of Bai, Chang and Wang (2006). Like  Beber and Pagano

they  also  base  this  on  the  idea  of  higher  holding  costs  for  the  market  maker.

However, they assign the increased cost to the loss of negative information instead of

the increased number of shares they need to hold.

3.2  Previous Empirical Work

As  seen in the introduction, a common view of short selling  among investors and

regulators  is that  it can be a malicious practice.  Even though short selling has often

been blamed for causing stock market crashes and plunging asset prices, it is also

acknowledged to have several  positive  effects on market quality, like added  liquidity

and more efficient price  discovery.  Like the public views on short selling,  the  findings

in the  empirical field of finance  are  not  always  coherent and clear-cut.  As we shall

see  the  empirical  conclusions  regarding  the  effects  on  stock  returns  often  differ

between studies,  making it unclear  how effective  regulatory action  really is.  In the

following sections we briefly summarize some of the  previous empirical findings.

3.2.1  Effect on  Returns

While Beber and Pagano (2013) conclude that short selling bans have no positive

effect on stock returns, except perhaps on American financial stocks,  Chang, Cheng

and Yu (2007) found  that constraints on short selling in the Hong Kong market were

associated  with  stock  overvaluations.  Similarly  to  Chang,  Cheng  and  Yu,  other

authors also find that constraints on short selling can lead to overvaluations  and that

short   sellers   therefore   play   an   important   role   in   moving   prices   toward   their

fundamental values (see e.g. Jones & Lamont (2001) and Cohen, Diether & Malloy

(2007)). In  line with the overvaluation evidence,  Bris, Goetzmann & Zhu (2007) find

strong  evidence  that  in  markets  where  short  selling  is  either  prohibited  or  not

practiced, market returns display significantly less negative skewness.

       The previously mentioned study by the Office  of Economic Analysis presents

results   showing   that   control   stocks   (which   were   still   subject   to   short   selling

restrictions) on average outperformed Listed Pilot Stocks (i.e.  stocks allowed for

short selling) by approximately 24 basis points on the first day of trading.  This

suggests   that   short   selling   restrictions   may   cause   stock   overvaluations.   When

                                                                                                   12
analysing returns over a period of six months, the Office of Economic Analysis found

that control and Pilot stocks had  similar returns. However,  some tests still presented

weak   evidence   of   stock   over-pricing   when   restricting   short   sales.   Like   Bris,

Goetzmann &  Zhu, the Office for Economic Analysis also tested the return skewness

of  the  stocks  and  how  it  was  affected  by  the  suspension  of  the  short  selling

restriction. In contrast to the previous mentioned authors, the results indicated no

statistically significant impact  on  return skewness,  measured at five-minute, thirty-

minute, and  one-day return intervals.

3.2.2  Effect on  Market Quality

Christophe, Ferri and Angel (2004) show that abnormal short selling is significantly

linked to post earnings-announcement  stock returns, thereby building on the view

that   short   sellers   are   often   well   informed   investors   who   incorporate   valuable

information in stock prices and help facilitate the price discovery process. On a

similar  note,  Beber  and  Pagano  (2013)  find  that  short  selling  restrictions  are

detrimental to the price discovery process and that market liquidity is reduced when

short  selling  bans  are  implemented.  Another  study  that  confirms  the  view  that

restrictions on short selling hampers  price discovery is presented  by Diamond and

Verrecchia (1986). They conclude that bans on short selling reduce the adjustment

speed  of  prices  to  private  information,  especially  to  bad  news  (see  also  Cohen,

Diether & Malloy (2007) for  similar evidence). Boehmer and Wu (2009) further

reinforce these findings by showing that intraday informational efficiency of prices

improves with greater shorting flow and that at monthly and annual horizons, more

shorting flow accelerates the incorporation of public information into prices. Like

Christophe, Ferri and Angel, Boehmer and Wu also show that greater shorting flow

reduces    post-earnings-announcement    drift    for    negative    earnings    surprises.

Furthermore, Karpoff and Lou (2010) present results that indicate that short sellers

                                                                                                   13
often can anticipate and help uncover financial misconduct and thereby contribute to

incorporating important information in stock prices. Another noteworthy empirical

finding, presented by Boehmer and Wu (2009), reveals that short sellers change their

trading around extreme return events in a way that aids price discovery and reduces

divergence from fundamental value. This is an interesting finding since it questions

the short seller critics who often contend that short selling drives down stock prices

below true price valuation.

4.3  Time Period

Choosing   time   frame   for   our   analysis   required   some   considerations   since   the

regulations of short  selling differed between the countries over time. Sometimes bans

affected all stocks.  Countries  have also had  different start and end dates to  their

we   are   not   interested   in   examining   the   sole   effect   of   naked   short   selling.

government interventions and therefore  they  might  be subject to  biases  that could be

hard to adjust for.

4.4  Method

To analyse the effects of  the ban we use  event study methodology and difference in

difference  OLS  regressions  (“diff-in-diff”).  The  Spanish  stocks  are  the  treatment

group and as control group we use a selection of our French, Italian and Portuguese

stocks. This selection is picked  using a matching technique based on beta  values.

Matching on beta could be a good way to sort out and eliminate macroeconomic

noise.    Since  we  only  have  stocks  from  the  four  countries’  largest  indices  the

difference in size will be relatively small across  stocks, thereby reducing the risk of

effects caused by size factors. It appeared that average betas were quite similar for

the treatment group and control group even before the matching process, but after

the  matching  they  were  almost  identical.  In  addition,  matching  stocks  almost

eliminated the gap in beta volatility between the two groups.  The disadvantage with

In our regression analysis we use three dichotomous variables to  measure the effect of

the short  selling ban. The first one, dt, equals one if the stock is included in the

treatment group and zero otherwise. The second variable, d2, equals one after the

inception of the ban and zero before the inception. The last variable, dt2, is an

interaction term,  which equals the value of dt times the value of d2. In other words,

it equals one if the stock belongs to the treatment group and the date is after the

ban inception. The basic level of the regression looks as following:

                 1   !  = ! + !!× !" +  !! × !2 +  !! × !"2 +  ε

To analyse the full effect of the ban we do not only look  at  the inception of the  ban,

we investigate  the effect of the lift of the ban as well. This is done with a regression

similar  to  equation  1,  used  at  the  inception.  The  difference  is  that  variable  d3

replaces variable d2. The variable d3 is also a dichotomous variable and equals one

after the ban is lifted and zero otherwise. The basic level of the regression looks as

following:

                 2   !  = ! + !!× !" +  !! × !3 +  !! × !"3  +  ε

Stock-specific characteristics like the number of market makers and restrictions in

minimum bid-ask spreads may affect our liquidity measures. Consequently, when

running regressions for our market quality related variables we also add firm fixed

effects. In addition, all our regressions include day fixed effects to eliminate common

changes over time. In  several other studies of short  selling bans  it is common to

control  for the possibility of taking short positions through derivatives. This is  not

needed in our study since  all kinds  of short positions in equity are prohibited during

this ban  (CNMV).  In all regressions we correct for heteroscedasticity by using robust

standard errors.

       In  Figure  X  we  plot  the  five  days  moving  average  percentage  spreads,

treatment group and control group separated. Between  April  13 and  April  17 2012

the treatment group curve makes a jump, as a result of  a peak in the Spanish

banking crisis,  increasing the percentage spread about ten times  (Smith, A. (2012)).

                                                                                                   19
A similar  drastic change occurs for  the control group between  June  22  and June 25

2012,  halving  the  five  days  average  percentage  spread  during  the  following  five

trading days.  This is due to a systematic drop in Italian spreads, however we have

been unable to identify  the underlying reason for this.  To control for these events we

make an exception in our regression analysis of the percentage spread and add two

new dichotomous variables, dx_t and dy_c. The variable dx_t equals one for the

treatment group after  April  13 2012 and zero otherwise while the variable dy_c

equals one for the control group after  June  22 2012 and zero otherwise.  Except for

these events, which we from now on will refer to as Event X and Event Y, we are

also controlling for the ban extension  on  October 19 2012 (extension_t) and the

disclosure regulation by ESMA  on  November 1 2012 (ESMA_t).

       In all our  regressions we study the immediate, short-term and the long-term

effects of the ban, since there might  be differences depending on which perspective is

chosen. To study the difference between long and short-term effects we are setting

up our regression analysis on varying time frames. Final regressions with all variables

included are provided  in Tables V  -  XIV.

5. Results

5.1  Descriptive Results

5.1.1  Returns

Figure II  plots the cumulative abnormal returns for the treatment group and the

control group respectively. During the period before the inception of the ban one can

see that all stocks are in a declining trend, however, one can note that the control

group outperforms the treatment group. The treatment group stocks sink almost

30% up until the ban while the control group loses approximately 15%.

      By the inception of the short selling ban, both the control group and the

treatment group display a reversal of their negative return trends. As seen in  Figure

III, the differential between the two groups narrows notably in the first few days of

trading after the inception of the ban, meaning that the initial gain during the ban is

larger   for   the   treatment   group   than   for   the   control   group.   However,   after

approximately a month of trading the differential between the two groups starts to

trend  lower  again,  indicating  that  the  initial  positive  effect  on  returns  for  the

treatment group is smoothed out over time.

       By comparing the  period before the ban and the period during the ban we can

see that the underperformance of the treatment group before the ban is considerably

reduced with the announcement of the short selling ban. From the implementation of

the short selling ban and forward, the cumulative abnormal return of the treatment

group more closely follows the cumulative abnormal return of the control group. This

is  seen  in  Figure  III,  where  one  can  note  that  the  trend  of  the  differential  in

cumulative abnormal returns is much less negative (or even flat) after the short

selling ban is adopted.

                                                                                                   21
       As seen in  Figure III, the lift of the ban is associated with a large drop in

relative abnormal returns. However, after the initial drop,  abnormal returns  rebound

and one can see a flat trend in the cumulative abnormal returns differential.

       By looking at  Table III  one can see that the difference in average abnormal

returns between the two groups is much less negative during the ban than before the

ban. After the lift of the ban, the difference in average abnormal return is even

positive.

5.1.2  Market Quality

I. Volume

By studying Figures IV  and  V  one can note that trading volume for treatment stocks

significantly declines after the ban on short selling is announced. As measured by the

volume index, trading in treatment group stocks decreases by approximately 50% in

the first month of trading during the ban, compared to a more moderate fall in

trading volume for control stocks. The most clear evidence of the loss of trading

volume can be seen in Figure  V, where the trading volume index ratio goes from

being slightly above one before the ban to significantly below one after the ban is

introduced.  Even  when  looking  at  Figures  VI  and  VII,  one  can  see  an  initial

detrimental effect on turnover for treatment group stocks.

      By the time of the lift of the ban volume index ratio temporary increases.

However,  the fluctuations in volume index ratio after the lift of the ban do not

appear to be skewed in any way. The turnover ratio, though, seems to indicate the

beginning of an upward trend. Looking at Table III one can see that the difference in

average volume index between the treatment group and control group decreases over

time.

                                                                                                   22
II. Volatility

Figure  VIII  displays the ten-day moving average volatility for the control group and

the treatment group. Before the ban inception both control group and treatment

group   volatility   seem   to   be   in   an   upward   trend.   By   the   time   of   the   ban

announcement   volatility   spikes   and   thereafter   takes   a   downward   trend.   By

comparing  figure VIII and IX  one can see that the initial increase in volatility is

larger for the treatment group  than for the control group, indicating that the ban

itself might have caused a temporary increase in volatility. By the lift of the short

selling ban one can see a similar spike in volatility as for the inception of the ban.

However, after the lift of the  ban the long-term effect on volatility is ambiguous.

       By analysing the volatility ratio during the ban period it is difficult to discern

any clear trends in the volatility ratio, making it difficult to discover the more long-

term  effects  of  the  short  selling  ban.  Nevertheless,  the  volatility  ratio  provides

supporting evidence of the fact that the ban increases volatility at inception and lift

dates. In  Table III  one can also see that the ban causes higher average volatility.

III. Bid-Ask Spreads

Figure X  presents the bid-ask spread as a percentage of stock prices for the control

group and the treatment group. From April 16 2012 the bid-ask spread percentage

increases dramatically for the treatment group and for the control group the bid-ask

spread percentage decreases dramatically from June 26 2012.

       Disregarding  the  extreme  changes  in  percentage  spreads  before  the  ban

inception one can note that the immediate effect of the announcement of the short

selling ban seems to be an increase in percentage spreads  for the treatment group.

Before  the  ban  it  seems  as  the  treatment  group  was  in  a  declining  trend  for

percentage spreads, and after the initial boost the treatment group re-enters the

same  downward  trend.  However,  by  looking  at  Figure  XI,  displaying  the  ratio

between the percentage spreads for the treatment group and the control group, we

                                                                                                   23
can  see  that  the  percentage  spread  ratio  trends  higher  during  the  ban  period,

indicating that the ban taken by itself may have caused an increase in percentage

spreads.

    When announcing the lift of the short selling ban the effect on percentage

spreads is at first glance unclear. The immediate effect seems to be an increase in the

percentage spreads, which can be seen in both Figure X and XI. After the initial

boost there seems to be a short reversal that subsequently transitions to what seems

to be the beginning of a flat trend, even though the trend is volatile.

5.2 Statistical Testing

Results from the statistical tests are presented in Tables V  –  XIV and Table IV

describes the interpretation of the diff-in-diff regressions.

5.2.1  Returns

Ban Inception

The  diff-in-diff  regression  analysis  to  some  extent  confirms  what  the  descriptive

statistics indicated. As seen in  Table V  the immediate  effect of the ban is not clear.

However, when looking at a short-term window of ten trading days the analysis

confirms an increase in abnormal returns due to the ban of approximately 0.64% per

day, with a t-value of 2.71.  These results are also maintained in the long-run, though

the increase in abnormal returns due to the ban inception is lowered to 0.15% per

day, with a t-value of 1.97.

Lift of Ban

The regression in table  Table VI  confirms  the initial drop in abnormal returns  that

was clearly seen  in the descriptive statistics. During the first day of trading after the

lift of the ban, the  effect on abnormal returns is approximately  -1.64%, with a t-

                                                                                                   24
stastic of  -2.13.  Regarding the short-term effect, however, one can see a  statistically

significant  effect of  0.47% per day  –  which is represented by the reversal of abnormal

returns in  Figure II.  The long-term effects on abnormal returns are unclear.

5.2.2 Market Quality

I. Volume

Ban Inception

The  detrimental  effect  on  trading  volume  that  can  be  seen  in  the  descriptive

statistics is further  supplemented  by the  regression  statistics in  Table VII. While the

immediate effect on  trading  volume is not statistically significant, both the short-

term   and   long-term   effects   point   to   a   substantial   loss   of   trading   volume   of

approximately 30%, as measured by the volume index, with t-statistics of  -3.47 and  -

10.15 respectively.  This finding is further reinforced by the statistics on turnover,

presented in  Table IX,  pointing to clear decline in turnover for both the short-term

and the long-term.

Lift of Ban

Contrary to the effects of the inception of the ban, the statistics shown in the  Table

VIII  point to an immediate effect of increase in trading volume following the lift of

the ban. Even in the short-term there is a statistically significant positive effect on

trading volume, though the long-term effects are more dubious. The initial effect on

trading volume is also supported  by the regression on turnover  in  Table X,  which

shows a statistically significant positive effect on turnover over the first day of

trading. However,  the  short-term effects on turnover are  non-significant and the

long-term effects are negative.

                                                                                                   25
II. Volatility

Ban Inception

The regression analysis in  Table XI  presents evidence of an effect of immediately

increased volatility, confirming what we observed in the descriptive statistics where

there was an initial spike after the ban announcement. However, this effect is not

significant in the short-term, where the t-value is only  -0.16. Regarding the long-term

effect  the regression presents strong evidence  of increased volatility, which confirms

the indication seen in Table III.

Lift of Ban

Table XII provides statistics that point to an immediate effect of increased volatility,

which was seen as a jump in volatility in the descriptive results. This effect is

sustained in the short-term but subsequently reversed when the  changing perspective

to the long-term. In the long-term the regression points to a decrease in volatility

with a t-value of  -3.73, thereby confirming the indication that was seen in Table III.

III. Percentage Spreads

Ban Inception

In  the  descriptive  statistics  we  saw  an  immediate  boost  in  percentage  spreads,

however this effect is not confirmed by the regression statistics in  Table XIII,  where

the immediate effect is non-significant. Instead, the regression statistics show an

effect of increased spreads in the  short-term.  In the long-term  the effect is opposite,

showing strong evidence of a  decrease in percentage spreads, as could also be seen in

the descriptive figures.  Important to note is that both the EU Disclosure Interaction

Term (ESMA_t) and the Extension of Ban Interaction Term (extension_t) are

negative with a high statistical significance.  Regarding percentage spreads it is  also

worthy to note that the average statistics in Table III are confounded by Event X

and Event Y, which distort percentage spreads before the ban is introduced.

                                                                                                   26
Lift of Ban

The diff-in-diff regression on the lift of the  ban, Table XIV,  shows that the effect is a

statistically significant increase in the percentage spreads, both in the  short-term and

in the long-term. What we thought was a flat long-term trend  in the descriptive

statistics  actually  proved  to  be  slightly  increasing.  The  immediate  increase  in

percentage spreads that  we saw in the descriptive section was not supported by the

statistical tests.

5.3 Robustness

A potential problem with our regression  analysis concerns the percentage spreads. As

seen in the descriptive analysis, the percentage spreads for the treatment group

jumps during Event X, before the inception of the ban. In combination with the drop

for control group percentage spreads, Event Y, this creates a problem since it has to

be   accounted   for   using   dichotomous   variables.   Even   though   the   dichotomous

variables neutralise the jump and drop in spreads, there may be a later reversal of

these events that may now be attributed to the ban, thereby making it look like the

ban decreases percentage spreads more than what actually is the case.  Therefore, the

results regarding the percentage spreads should perhaps be taken with a grain of salt.

Our  results  provide  significant  evidence  that  the  inception  of  the  ban  has  a

supporting effect on stock prices. The effect does not occur directly, but when it

appears in the short-term it is maintained in the long run as well. This is in line with

several other studies, such as Chang, Cheng and Yu (2007), and it gives support to

Miller’s (1977) overpricing theories.  However, this supporting effect on prices comes

with  a high cost in market quality. The loss of trading volume, turnover and  the

                                                                                                   28
increase in stock volatility is  detrimental for market participants,  especially in times

of crisis when market liquidity in general tends to dry up.

       The lift of the ban is associated with an instant drop in stock prices,  however,

prices recover fairly soon.  In the long-term the effect on  prices is not clear. Trading

volume increases immediately after the ban is lifted, but in the long-term the effect

on volume is ambiguous. This could be explained by the theories by Diamond and

Verrecchia  (1987), which predict market participants to adjust  their portfolio weights

to  the  new  conditions.  In  the  long-term  the  negative  effects  on  market  quality

associated with the inception of the ban persist or even worsen.

       Taking together the effects of the inception and the lift of the ban,  evidence

from  our study  shows that the ban supports prices without creating any long-term

reversal effects that  would  otherwise undermine the effectiveness of the ban.  At the

same time evidence points to a clear loss of market quality, which  does not reverse

even after the ban  is lifted.  The  implication  of these findings is that there is some

merit to the argument that one wants to  support prices by implementing  a  ban  on

short selling. However, the supportive effect on prices comes with  high cost and

regulators should therefore carefully consider the trade-off between supporting prices

and sustaining market quality.  Especially since we do not find evidence that the

negative effects of the inception of the ban are reversed when the ban is lifted.

       On a more critical note, when financial crises occur and prices decline rapidly,

the  root  of  the  problem  may  not  be  the  short  sellers  themselves  but  rather

fundamental  economic  factors  that  cause  the  loss  of  investor  confidence.  Paul

Asquith, a finance professor at the MIT who specializes in the study of short selling,

said the following in an interview with Reuters on September 26 2008:

”There is no academic evidence that short sellers by themselves brought a company

down without other underlying reasons,” // ”Lehman was having problems and the

                                                                                                   29
shorts were there. But once Lehman started looking for a buyer, what happened?

Nobody wanted to buy Lehman when they started looking at their books very closely.

That tells you the shorts  were right.” (Trotta, D. (2008))

If  fundamental  economic  factors  cause  the  loss  of  investor  confidence,  then  the

positive effect of a short selling ban would mainly come from the signal it sends to

the market,  namely  that the government is not happy with  the current situation and

that  there may be more  interventions  coming until the negative trend  in stock prices

is reversed. If this is true, then  there might be some better way  to send these signals,

without  causing  the side effects on market quality that  short selling  restrictions have

been proven to have.  Therefore  we think  it would be very interesting to see  examples

of  short selling bans  implemented  by  stock exchanges themselves, since  they in this

case  would come completely without market expectations  on further government aid.

However, a scenario like this seems unlikely, but we hope that future research finds a

way to sort out the sole effect of a short selling ban.

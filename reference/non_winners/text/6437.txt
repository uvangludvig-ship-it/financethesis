  Do Environmental Leaders Perform Better During Disasters?
                  Evidence from the 2025 California Wildfires

                                     Abstract

On the 7th of January 2025, multiple wildfires broke out in the US state of California. Like
other severe disasters, these fires affected society in many aspects. This study investigates one
such effect: its impact on the American stock market. More specifically, we look at whether the
environmental performance of S&P 500 firms played a deciding role in how the market treated
firms’ stocks in the time following the wildfires. The study conducted mean comparison tests
and multiple linear regressions to identify whether a significant relationship existed between
environmental performance and Cumulative Abnormal Returns (CARs). We investigated three
separate time frames to account for differing reactions in different stages of the post-disaster
period. We find no significant relationship in the short- or medium-term. Long-term results are
mixed, with some models showing no effect and others showing a weak positive relationship.
The long-term effect remains uncertain and requires further research to be fully understood.

Keywords: Event Study, Cumulative Abnormal Return, Natural Disasters, Environmental
Performance, Stock Market Reaction

1. Introduction

1.1 Background

On January 7th, 2025, multiple wildfires broke out in southern California, the most notable
being the Palisades and the Eaton fires. These fires, along with others that started on later dates,
kept on burning and heavily affected people in southern California throughout January until
they were finally contained towards the end of the month. Though wildfires are not uncommon
in California, with multiple fires each year during the 10 years preceding 2025, even though
these fires did not cover as many acres as some in previous years, they still received extensive
media coverage. The media coverage was likely due to the fires largely affecting residential
areas, whereas previous fires mainly affected less densely populated areas. Therefore, the
January 2025 wildfires caused a disproportionate amount of destruction to structures compared
to previous fires, which led to more people being directly affected than in many previous cases
(California Department of Forestry and Fire Protection, 2025).

Impactful events such as these fires usually affect a broad range of societal areas in various
ways, which makes it interesting to look beyond the direct impacts of the event. We have
decided to look closer at the economy and how these fires affected the companies included in
the Standard and Poor’s 500 index (S&P 500). The S&P 500 includes the 500 biggest
companies listed on American stock markets, encompassing about 80% of the available market
capitalisation (S&P Global, 2025). The S&P 500 companies are listed on some of the most
active markets on the planet. These include the New York Stock Exchange and the Nasdaq (US)
which are the markets that average the highest monthly value of shares traded for 2024 (World
Federation of Exchanges, 2025). As a result of the high activity in these markets, they also
become highly reactive, taking all societal events into account. Thus, the assumption is that the
markets will consider a significant event such as the January 2025 California wildfires. The
question then becomes how the markets will react to this event, whether they will deem it
relevant enough to influence investment decisions, and whether it will positively or negatively
impact the market. In addition to the market as a whole being affected in a certain way, each
company will also be affected in its own way, which might coincide with or differ from how
the market is affected.

The performance of a company’s stock, post a major event, is dependent on a countless number
of factors. However, in most cases, certain factors can be pointed out as having a larger impact

                                                                           3
than most others, depending on the event. In the specific case of the January 2025 California
wildfires, as it is an environmental disaster, we believe that a factor which would be interesting
to look closer at is the environmental performance of companies. Though the term
environmental performance is used loosely, we believe that the definition provided by Mendes
et al. (2011), which states that environmental performance is “measurable results of the ability
of an organization to handle environmental factors which can be, defined as those elements of
an organization’s activities, products or services that can interact with the environment, and
have an impact on it”, is an accurate definition. It is the one that will be used in this paper. How
strong a company's environmental performance is can vary vastly, and a strong performance is
usually achieved through conscious decisions and initiatives to improve this specific
performance. Considering this, it could be argued that strong environmental performance is a
strategy that can be pursued for multiple reasons. With this in mind, it becomes interesting to
see how this strategy affects stock market performance in the event of a wildfire.

1.2 Purpose & Research question

This study aims to investigate the effect of the California 2025 wildfires on the stock market
performance of companies included in the S&P 500 index, which have strong environmental
performance, and compare their performance to that of companies in the same index with weak
environmental performance. Therefore, our research question is formulated as follows:
    - How did the California 2025 wildfires affect the stock market performance of S&P 500
       companies with high versus low environmental performance?

1.3 Relevance of the chosen subject

As section 2 previous literature will expand on, there already exists studies which cover similar
areas to what we will investigate. Multiple studies have examined how natural disasters impact
the markets, and many studies have examined how environmental performance can be
connected to market performance. However, few studies have combined both, with the studies
by Huynh et al. (2021) and by Malik et al. (2023) being part of the limited selection to actively
discuss the connection between natural disasters, environmental performance, and stock market
performance.

This thesis makes two main contributions. First, we isolate environmental performance
specifically, rather than broad ESG or CSR scores, and compare firms with different
performance, something earlier studies have not done. Second, we study a new and high-profile
disaster (the 2025 California Wildfires), to test if previous findings still hold in a recent context.

1.4 Delimitations

We have made a couple of main delimitations in this study. First, we have chosen only to look
at S&P 500 stocks. The reason why we choose not to look at all American stocks is mainly due
to the data availability. The popularity of the S&P 500 means that data about it is readily
available, while finding data about smaller US equities can be more challenging. Additionally,
as the S&P 500 makes up the majority of the US market capitalisation, it becomes one of the
most interesting indices to look at when trying to understand the dynamics of the American
market (S&P Global, 2025).

The second delimitation for this study is that we are only using Refinitiv Eikon’s environmental
scores, even though many other well-renowned environmental score providers exist, such as
MSCI, Bloomberg, and S&P. The main reason for only using one provider is that we wanted a
clear and non-contradicting dataset. If we were to use environmental scores from multiple
providers, then it is possible that the scores would differ, making the data hard to handle. The
reason we chose Refinitiv Eikon specifically was its strong reputation and availability.

1.5 The study

To test our research question, we have conducted both mean comparison tests and multiple
linear regressions to investigate whether a relationship between firms’ environmental
performance and their Cumulative Abnormal Returns (CAR) can be identified. In these tests,
we have looked at three different time frames: short-term ([-1, +1] day), medium-term ([-5, +5]
days), and long-term ([-30, +30] days). Our results indicate no significant relationship in the
short- and medium-term. However, the long-term results are inconclusive, indicating further
research is needed.

2. Previous Literature

2.1 Linking Wildfires and Environmental Sustainability

Wildfires often occur in arid areas that experience a lot of sunlight and heat; these circumstances
can result from purely natural coincidence. However, previous literature points towards the fact
that non-natural factors, such as anthropogenic climate change, can also increase the likelihood
of these circumstances occurring and the severity of the resulting wildfires. A study by
Diffenbaugh et al. (2014) investigates the effect of anthropogenic warming on California's
drought risk. Using a combination of historical data analysis, climate model experiments, and
statistical analyses, they found that anthropogenic warming does lead to an increase in the
probability of drought in California. As an increase in drought also increases the risk of
wildfires, this study indicates a relationship between increased anthropogenic warming and
wildfires.

This relationship is more thoroughly explored in a study by Abatzoglou et al. (2016) that
highlights this specific relationship. The study investigates to what extent anthropogenic
climate change contributes to the rise of wildfires in the western US by using empirical data
and combining it with statistical modelling and climate model simulations. Through these
methods, the study finds that anthropogenic climate change has contributed to more than 50%
of the increase in fuel aridity since the 1970s. Furthermore, in the last 30 years, this
anthropogenic fuel aridity has approximately doubled the forest fire area beyond what was
expected by natural climate variability. This further indicates a clear relationship between
wildfires and anthropogenic climate change.

2.2 The Relationship Between Environmental and Market Performance

The environmental performance of companies is commonly captured under ESG or CSR
measures. Therefore, most previous studies look at these as a whole, rather than focusing only
on environmental performance. Both ESG and CSR include multiple factors, but are primarily
made up of environmental performance. Therefore, they can reasonably be used as proxies for
environmental performance. So, to ensure the highest quality of previous literature, studies
related to ESG and CSR have also been included in this part of the review.

A study by Wang et al. (2024), which explores what effect ESG ratings can have on how the
market responds to earning surprises, illustrates an apparent effect. The study finds that firms

                                                                           6
with high ESG ratings see a stronger stock price reaction to positive earnings surprises and a
more limited reaction to negative earnings surprises. This suggests that investors have greater
trust in highly ESG-rated firms and, therefore, to a larger extent, believe positive earnings
surprises are genuine and do not take negative earnings surprises as a sign of a poorly run
company. Furthermore, the study finds that high ESG-rated firms are more prone to having a
large portion of institutional ownership. This leads to less drastic market reactions, resulting in
a more stable shareholder base and less volatility. These characteristics of firms with high ESG
ratings make them more attractive to investors, thus positively affecting their market
performance.

When investigating how positive and negative news about ESG factors affect stock
performance during 2002-2010, Capelle-Blancard et al. (2017) found that while positive news
will not result in a significant effect, negative news will lead to a decrease in stock price. This
indicates that investors care more about companies not being bad regarding ESG than about
them being especially good. Additionally, the study finds that investors only react to ESG-
related news from media outlets, not from the firm's statements or NGOs. This points towards
investors only trusting independent sources that have no apparent agenda. The study also found
that stock price decreases from negative news can be mitigated by operating in an “ESG strong
industry” or by using greenwashing.

Another study looking at the impact of ESG news, but focusing only on the environmental
factor for the years 1980-2009, by Flammer (2013), had relatively similar results. However,
there were some noteworthy differences and additions. One is that historically, there has been
both a price increase for positive news and a decrease for negative news, but with time, the
increases have diminished while the decreases have grown. This reflects changing expectations
from investors, where strong environmental performance is expected today. At the same time,
harmful environmental actions are seen as abnormal. Another observation from the study was
that the stock price increases from environmentally friendly actions by companies were
diminishing. This indicates that if a firm already has a reputation for being eco-friendly, from
a purely stock price-focused perspective, it is not worth investing further in positive
environmental actions. These findings further prove that ESG factors impact companies' market
performance.

Serafeim et al. (2022) explore the role of ESG ratings in ESG news and the market reaction
resulting from them. When looking at ESG ratings’ predictive ability for future news, they

                                                                           7
found that highly rated firms are more likely to receive positive ESG news in the future, setting
market expectations. When looking at how the ratings affect the reaction to different news, what
could be observed was that positive news had a larger effect on firms with lower ratings than
on highly rated firms, likely due to the already existing expectations mentioned previously.
However, when negative news occurred, the ratings did not have any effect as the study found
that all firms were affected similarly by them, regardless of their rating. It could also be
observed that different types of ESG news had differing effects. It was financially material ESG
news that had the most significant impact on stock price movements, indicating that a
company’s ESG actions have the most impact on market performance when they in some way
directly affect financial factors, which are traditionally important for investors to make their
investment decisions. Finally, it is important to mention that all of the findings from this article
about the effect of ESG ratings are only clearly visible when different rating agencies agree on
the ratings of companies. When agencies provide differing ratings, the market becomes
uncertain about interpreting ESG-related news, and therefore, the aforementioned effects
become less pronounced.

It is widely accepted that firms’ financial performance has a big impact on their stock market
performance; it is thus interesting to look at how and if there is a connection between
environmental and financial performance. This was done in an article by Margolis & Walsh
(2003), where 127 previous studies investigating the relationship between firms' socially
responsible conduct and financial performance were examined from 1972 to 2002. Though the
article never formally defines “socially responsible conduct,” it can reasonably be compared to
modern equivalents such as ESG or CSR. As mentioned, both can be used as a proxy for
environmental performance. Out of the 127 studies, the majority claim a positive relationship
exists between the two, indicating that socially responsible conduct can be motivated from a
purely financial perspective. However, when looking closer at the methodologies of these
previous studies, the article finds many weaknesses and problems that question the legitimacy
of the positive relationship. The questionable methodologies have also led to a certain degree
of self-reinforcement, which puts further doubt into the findings.

To summarise, the majority of previous literature seems to suggest that there exists a
relationship between environmental and market performance. Though most studies point to this
relationship being positive, there still exists doubt regarding whether this is the case, with some
studies also providing results that do not point towards a positive relationship.

2.3 The Impact of Disasters on Stock Markets

Disasters can come in many different forms, but what they all have in common is that they have
a vast, overwhelmingly negative effect on many parts of society. However, when looking
exclusively at the financial markets, do disasters have any notable effect? When Baker et al.
(2020) studied the reaction of the market to the outbreak of the COVID-19 virus, they found
that the pandemic had a significant effect on the market as it increased volatility drastically and
led to an overall downturn. This makes the COVID-19 pandemic stand out from previous
pandemics such as the Spanish flu or the 1968 influenza, which did not affect the markets nearly
as much. The study provides numerous explanations for this observed difference, including that
restrictions from COVID-19 strongly impacted the business environment, which today is way
more service-focused than during previous pandemics, leading to less economic activity. Also,
the faster modern-day information flow allowed the markets to react quickly. This indicates
that pandemics can affect markets to a different extent and, significantly, that it is not the
pandemics that directly impact the market but the consequences that might disrupt the market.
What could also be observed from the COVID-19 pandemic is that, even though it initially only
affected China, most major markets worldwide moved down in unison. Implying an increased
interdependence in today's markets, likely due to globalisation (Liu et al., 2020). This indicates
that disasters' economic impacts are not necessarily limited to the geographical areas they
directly impact.

Regarding man-made disasters, Yousaf et al. (2022) examine the reaction to the Russia-Ukraine
war of the G20 and additional selected markets. The study finds that though the initial shock of
the invasion harmed most global markets, when looking at more long-term effects and the
severity of the effects, it is observable that markets with stronger ties to the war were affected
to a higher degree. This was since those countries’ economic activities were more severely
disrupted, which led to high uncertainty about future market performance. Additionally, the
study finds that not all industries were negatively impacted by the event, as an increase in oil
prices resulted in a positive effect on energy stocks, proving that the same disaster can have
differing effects based on various factors. Differing impacts were also found in a study by
Chesney et al. (2010), which investigated how terrorism has impacted the financial markets by
looking at 77 different terrorist attacks. What most attacks had in common was that they led to
an immediate overall negative reaction by the market, which it recovered from relatively
quickly. However, many different effects are identified when looking at the reactions in detail.
The study finds that while some industries, such as airlines and tourism, are especially
negatively impacted, others, such as defence and pharmaceuticals, often experience a positive

                                                                           9
impact. The study also found that markets in different countries react differently; for example,
emerging markets tend to exhibit larger volatility.

Overall, previous literature indicates that disasters' effects on markets can vary greatly and are
dependent on various factors. Also, the same disaster can affect separate industries differently.

2.4 The Impact of Natural Disasters on Stock Markets

As stated above, disasters can have vastly differing impacts on the markets, often depending on
the type of disaster. Therefore, it becomes interesting to take a closer look at natural disasters
and what impact previous studies have found them to have. A study by Sakariyahu et al. (2023)
looks at the impact of the Türkiye-Syria earthquakes on different stock markets. A significant
downturn immediately impacted the Turkish market and all other investigated markets.
However, after a few days, the markets started to recover, indicating that the initial downturn
was driven by emotional reactions to the shock rather than rational financial analyses. This is
further supported by the fact that the study finds that the higher the investor attention was, the
more severe the losses were, meaning that investor sentiment played a central role. As well as
that panic selling was likely a major driver of the negative market reaction. An additional
finding was that the severity of the downturn in foreign markets was influenced mainly by two
main factors: how strong the economic ties were to Türkiye and the country’s geographical
proximity to Türkiye.

When looking at the domestic natural disasters' impact on US equities from 1960 to 2015, Malik
et al. (2020) found that the impact can look different based on multiple factors. The two most
decisive factors were which industry the equity belonged to and the nature of the natural
disaster. The same disaster usually displayed opposing effects on different industries. Though
most industries usually see a negative post-disaster effect, some industries, such as the
pharmaceutical and construction industries, often experience a positive impact. However,
though some industries more commonly see a positive effect than a negative, and vice versa,
no industry reacts the same way to all types of natural disasters. This indicates that there is no
clear market reaction pattern, which is true for all types of natural disasters, but rather the effect
of each event is case-specific. Thus, the specific circumstances of each natural disaster need to
be considered when trying to predict its impacts on the market.

The study also looks into how the predictability of a natural disaster impacts market reaction.
Meteorological and hydrological disasters are often predictable; thus, the markets are aware of

                                                                          10
their potential impact before their actual occurrence. When public warnings are issued, the
markets tend to have a slower response during the pre-disaster period than they do to news
being released about the occurrence of an unpredictable disaster. The study argues that this is
likely due to the market's awareness that the warnings could be false alarms. Thus, concerns
regarding the legitimacy of the warning led to a weaker response. Even though reactions to
warnings tend to be weak, post-disaster reactions to predictable disasters also tend to be weaker
than the post-disaster reactions for unpredictable disasters, as the warning has removed the
surprise element, which can lead to uncertainty and overreactions for unpredictable disasters.

Huynh et al. (2021) examine how investors react to natural disasters in the US markets. Like
previously mentioned studies, they find that the general immediate reaction to disasters is
negative, but as the disaster starts to ease off, the markets recover. Observations from the study
indicate that the immediate negative reaction is an overreaction. Because declines in
fundamentals do not fully justify the price drop, insider stock purchases increase during the
price drop period, indicating that it is undervalued. Furthermore, the price drops became more
extreme after 2006, when the “Stern Review” on climate change was released, which increased
awareness of climate risk. The paper also finds that these negative overreactions are lower for
firms that have a strong environmental profile, as their investors tend to have non-pecuniary
motives to a greater degree. This indicates that strong environmental performance can protect
against negative reactions from natural disasters.

This phenomenon is further researched in a paper by Malik et al. (2023), which examines
whether CSR makes a difference in how the market reacts to natural disasters. After looking at
471 separate disasters during the 1995-2015 period, the results showed that the firms with high
CSR ratings consistently outperformed the low CSR-rated firms, and this was the case for all
different types of natural disasters. There are multiple potential explanations for this: investors
feel more trust towards firms that actively invest in CSR. By making CSR investments, firms
create a reciprocal relationship with their investors, which results in investors staying with them
to a greater degree in bad times. The size of the benefits gained from having high CSR ratings
varied, but the firms that gained the most from having high ratings were firms with both low
financial constraint and low information asymmetry and high social capital. The study also
found that the environmental part of CSR was the strongest driver of resilience towards natural
disasters. CSR firms with a strong environmental profile outperformed other CSR firms.

2.5 Hypothesis

The literature points towards a noticeable difference in the stock market reactions to the
California 2025 wildfires for companies with high versus low environmental scores. The
reaction will likely be worse for low-rated companies than for highly rated ones. This is based
on the following four potential reasons:
    1. According to Wang et al. (2024) high ESG rated firms tend to have a higher share of
       institutional investors, who invest more long term and thus the reaction to a market
       shock is less pronounced for a firm with higher ESG ratings. Since environmental
       performance is one of the main drivers for ESG ratings, it is reasonable to assume that
        firms with high environmental scores also have a higher share of institutional investors
       and thus also experience similarly muted reactions to shocks.
    2. The environmental actions that lead to companies achieving high environmental scores
        are a type of CSR activity. In the article by Malik et al. (2023), it is presented that
       investors tend to have higher trust and have a feeling of reciprocity towards firms with
       high CSR performance. Since the firms are spending resources towards addressing
       social and environmental challenges, the investors are more willing to stick with them
       during bad times to “give back” for their good initiatives, leading to the market
       performance of these companies being stronger during events such as natural disasters.
    3. The extreme severity of the 2025 California wildfires could lead to the government
        taking larger action than previously done to combat future wildfires. As outlined in the
       papers by Baker et al. (2020) and Yousaf et al. (2022), there is a clear connection
       between the wildfires in the western US and anthropogenic climate change. Therefore,
       in order to combat the wildfires, anthropogenic climate change has to be combated, and
       this could potentially be done by implementing new policies and rules for how
      companies must operate in environmentally friendly ways, this could lead to companies
       having to put a lot of capital into operational adjustments to comply with new rules.
       However, firms with high environmental scores are likely better positioned for these
       changes, giving them a financial benefit as they can use that capital elsewhere. This
        benefit is something that investors will likely be aware of, and therefore, they will be
       more inclined to invest in these companies, which would boost the stock prices.
    4. Huynh et al. (2021) find that even though the market as a whole tends to overreact
       negatively to natural disasters, firms with high environmental performance tend to be
        penalised less due to their investors' higher degree of non-pecuniary motives. It is
        reasonable to believe that this was also the case during the 2025 California wildfires,

                                                                          12
       and thus, firms with a high environmental score will outperform those with a low
       environmental score.

With these four reasons in mind, we consider the most likely hypothesis to be:
   ● H1a: The stock performance of companies with high environmental performance
        exhibited a statistically significant difference in reaction compared to companies with
       low environmental performance following the 2025 California wildfires.
   ● H1b: Companies with high environmental performance experienced higher abnormal
       returns than companies with low environmental performance in the aftermath of the
        2025 California wildfires.

3. Methodology

3.1 Data Sources and Timeline

In order to examine how environmental performance affected stock market responses to
environmental disasters, we conducted an event study on the companies listed in the S&P 500,
as of January 1st, 2025. We constructed our event study in line with the procedure presented in
the classical paper by MacKinlay (1997). We made some additions to improve and adjust the
study for our specific purpose. Our event is the outbreak of the California wildfires on January
7th, 2025, and our analysis concentrates on the abnormal stock returns before and after this
date. We take this as the study date mainly because it was the beginning of the fires at Palisades
and Eaton, which were among the most impactful fires during this period. After reviewing news
coverage and search engine data, we concluded that investors’ attention and media exposure
were both at their peak just as the fires broke out, so we naturally made this date our anchor
point when analysing the market reaction (Google Trends, 2025).

We have two primary data sources: Refinitiv Eikon and Yahoo Finance. From Eikon, we collect
firm-level financial and environmental metrics for the companies in the S&P 500, including
environmental scores, market capitalisation, GICS industry names, beta values, and debt-to-
equity ratios. These data points are taken at fixed dates close to the event window to reflect
individual firm characteristics as of the event date. Specifically, the environmental scores are
taken as of December 31, 2024; beta and market capitalisation as of January 1, 2025; and debt-
to-equity as of December 31, 2024. The reason for choosing different dates was the availability
of the data.

                                                                          13
From Eikon, we got stock price data for each company in the S&P 500 from July 15, 2024, to
March 4, 2025. This stretch covers 160 trading days, enough pre-event and post-event data for
us to estimate and analyse. Together with the closing price, we also obtained dividend and stock
split data for all 503 stocks during the same period. This was done to adjust the raw price data
and ensure that our return calculations reflect actual market reactions rather than mechanical
changes, as MacKinlay (1997) stresses should be done. Dividends and stock splits can distort
price movements if not accounted for. Dividends lower the stock price on the payout date, while
stock splits change a company's nominal share price without affecting firm value. To adjust for
this, each time a dividend is distributed, it is subtracted from the closing price on that day, and
when a stock split occurs, pre-split prices are retroactively multiplied by the reported split
factor. Adjusting returns accordingly ensures that our figures are fair and consistent for
comparison across different firms over time. We also obtain daily S&P 500 adjusted index
prices from Yahoo Finance, spanning the same 160 trading days, for comparison in our analysis.

3.2 Sample Construction and Cleaning

The dataset was made up of 503 companies included in the S&P 500. Firstly, we exclude
businesses classified as “Financials” or “Energy” according to their GICS industry names.
Financial firms are excluded due to their unique regulatory structures and capital requirements,
which make their stock behaviour difficult to compare with firms in other non-financial
industries (Fama et al., 1992). Energy firms are excluded because they are likely to be directly
and disproportionately affected by environmental disasters such as wildfires, due to resulting
power outages, potentially driving price movements unrelated to investor perception of
environmental performance. Including these firms could bias the estimated relationship
between environmental scores and abnormal returns. This exclusion results in a reduced sample
of 408 firms. Out of the 95 firms that were excluded, 73 of them were classified as “Financials”,
while the remaining 22 were classified as “Energy”.

3.3 Event Study Framework

In order to estimate the reaction of the stock market to the outbreak of California wildfires, we
use an event study based on the market model, which MacKinlay (1997) recommends. This
helps us isolate firm-specific abnormal returns by comparing actual stock returns to general
market movement trends. The core idea is that, if markets respond differently to firms
depending on their environmental performance, these differences should show up as abnormal
returns around the event date.

We model the expected return for each firm using the market model:

ERi,t = αi + βi × Rm,t

ERi,t is the expected return of firm i on day t, Rm,t is the daily return of the S&P 500 index, and
αi and βi are firm-specific parameters estimated through OLS regression.

We then calculate daily rates of return for each company based on the adjusted closing prices.
To estimate the parameters α and β, we used a 90-day lookback period, ending on the last
trading day before each event window, as recommended by MacKinlay (1997). These
estimation windows are chosen to reflect market conditions in the run-up to the event itself.
This approach is particularly relevant where environmental and macroeconomic events might
affect firm-specific expectations over short periods. By keeping the estimation window free of
overlap with the event window, the event itself has not disturbed our expected return
calculations.

The choice of a 90-day estimation window was mainly motivated by several large-scale events
before the period, which could distort our results. These events were, for example, the tech-
sector layoffs, unexpected monetary policy shifts, and political uncertainty during the early U.S.
primaries, all making the market volatile. Extending the estimation window further back may
bring more robustness to our findings, but could also introduce noise unrelated to the

                                                                          16
environmental event we study. This is consistent with MacKinlay (1997), who claims one
should avoid estimation windows affected by major external shocks.

Prior research also suggests that in the case of environmental disasters, rapid shifts can occur
in investor perceptions, making a shorter estimation window more suitable (Flammer, 2013). A
more contained window can allow α and β to be estimated under market conditions that better
match investor sentiment during the wildfires, instead of being too affected by unrelated
economic shocks.

Using this estimation window, we generate expected returns for the event window
corresponding to a firm by applying its unique α and β. The abnormal returns for each firm on
each day are then calculated as:

ARi,t = Ri,t - ERi,t

We calculate abnormal returns for firm i on day t by subtracting the expected return from the
actual return. We analyse abnormal returns across three different event windows: A short-term
window covering the day before and the day after the event ([-1, +1] day); a medium-term
window running for five days before and after the event ([-5, +5] days); and a long-term window
taking in price movements thirty days ahead of the event and thirty days after it ([ -30, +30]
days). These windows reflect different possible market responses: immediate reactions, delayed
price corrections, and long-term adjustments. The short window captures any sharp reaction as
news of the wildfires breaks. The medium window allows time for investors to reassess firm
exposure. The long window captures effects that may play out over several weeks as
information about the damage, recovery, and public discourse develops.

For each company, we total the abnormal returns within each window to give CARs. Since we
have three event windows, we end up with three CARs measures per firm. These become the
primary dependent variables in the later t-tests and regression analyses.

3.4 Environmental Classification

The main independent variable in our analysis is the firm’s environmental score. These figures
attempt to quantify each firm’s environmental performance. By taking a snapshot of these

                                                                          17
scores just before our wildfire event began, we can be sure they represent firm characteristics
as known to the market at the time of the event and are unaffected by anything after it.

We use the environmental score in two ways. First, we create a binary classification of firms'
environmental status. We calculate the median environmental score for our sample and then
break the firms into two groups based on that score: the high environmental score group, which
includes all firms above this median score, and the low environmental score group, which
includes firms below it. This median split lets us conduct mean comparison tests between firms
with relatively strong and relatively weak environmental profiles.

The binary classification helps us see if investors treated firms with visibly higher
environmental scores differently from those with lower scores. This kind of setup is useful for
seeing non-linear or threshold effects, such as whether investors primarily reward firms that are
clearly environmentally responsible rather than responding proportionally.

Second, we treat it as a continuous variable in our regression models. This permits us to see
whether incremental differences in environmental performance are associated with differences
in stock market responses. A higher score indicates better environmental performance, and we
test whether this leads to a more muted or favorable market response during the event windows.

3.5 Control Variables

We incorporate the following control factors to isolate the effect of environmental performance
on abnormal returns:

Market Cap: Firm size is adjusted for by including market capitalisation. Larger firms tend to
have more diversified operations, stronger investor confidence, and greater access to capital,
which may affect their resilience to shocks. We also applied a logarithmic transformation to
normalise the values and reduce the impact of extreme observations.

Debt-to-Equity Ratio: The ratio between debt and equity measures the firm’s financial
leverage. Highly leveraged firms may find it hard to cope with sudden disruptions or keep their
operations going through uncertain periods without access to funding, making them more
vulnerable to damage from negative market reactions.

                                                                          18
Beta: Firm-level beta captures systematic risk. Firms with higher beta are more vulnerable to
overall market movements and may react more strongly during periods of chaos. Including beta
in the analysis helps us disentangle market-related volatility from environmental-specific
effects.

Industry fixed effects: By including GICS industry dummies, we are controlling for structural
features that may differ between industries. This allows us to better isolate the impact of firm-
level environmental performance from broader industry trends.

3.6 Empirical Tests and Estimation

After each firm's CAR was tallied, we tested whether the returns have a relationship with
environmental performance. Two approaches were used for the test: mean comparison tests and
multiple linear regressions.

As stated earlier, in our two-sample t-tests, we compare firms with above- and below-median
environmental scores. We test whether the average abnormal returns differ significantly
between the two groups for each of the three CAR variables, corresponding to the three event
windows. These tests give an initial indication of whether firms with stronger environmental
profiles are treated differently by investors.

We then use OLS regression analysis to control for other firm characteristics. CAR is the
dependent variable in these regressions, whereas the environmental score is our main
independent variable. Specifically, we run the regressions separately for each event window: [-
1, +1], [-5, +5], and [-30, +30].

All three models contain the same control variables: market cap, debt-to-equity ratio, beta, and
industry dummies. These aim to account for other factors that may explain firm responses to
external shocks. For further analysis of the environmental performance's impact on stock return,
we estimate three regression models for each window. The first model includes only the
environmental score variable. The second model adds the control variables market cap, debt-
to-equity ratio, and beta. The third model includes all control variables, including industry
dummies, and acts as our main regression. The main regression equation is specified as follows:

CARi = β0 + β1 × EnvScorei + β2 × MarketCapi + β3 × Debt-To-Equityi + β4 × Betai +
ΣIndustryDummies + εi

3.7 Robustness Considerations

As MacKinlay (1997) suggests, we conduct several additional tests related to model
specification and underlying assumptions to increase the robustness of our findings.

Firstly, we create an alternative version of our regression models, reducing the 90-day
estimation window when calculating alpha and beta to 30 days. We chose to focus on a shorter
time window instead of a longer one because of the risks of unrelated events distorting our
results, which we discussed earlier. By comparing the relationship between environmental
scores and abnormal returns with the original 90-day period and our alternative model of 30
days, we can see whether less historical data leads us to draw different conclusions.

Secondly, we test for multicollinearity among the variables by calculating variance inflation
factors (VIFs). We do this with and without industry dummies to see if fixed effects introduce
collinear relationships or are present among the core independent variables. We also created an
alternative model where we removed one of the most collinear variables to examine if our
results still hold.

Finally, we study the distribution of regression residuals. We plot the residuals in a histogram
to look for a normal distribution. If the distribution is linear, it validates the linearity assumption
and strengthens the argument that OLS estimates are consistent. All regression models in the
study also employ heteroskedastic-robust standard errors to rectify the likely non-constant
variance of error terms.

4.5 Robustness Considerations

To increase the robustness of our findings, we conducted several additional tests related to
model specification and underlying assumptions.

Firstly, heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors were used in all regressions to correct potential
violations of the homoscedasticity assumption in the residuals.

Secondly, we address potential multicollinearity among the independent variables using
Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs). In the full model (which includes both firm-specific variables
and industry dummies), several variables show high VIF values. Specifically, market

                                                                          28
capitalisation (38.64), environmental score (15.17), and beta (11.12) all clearly exceed the
typical threshold of 5 (James et al., 2017). Full VIF results for the complete model are presented
in Appendix B.

To see whether industry dummies mainly drove multicollinearity, we re-estimate VIFs
excluding industry fixed effects. Although VIF values fall slightly, high multicollinearity
persists, with market capitalisation (22.08), environmental score (14.28), and beta (7.29) all still
above the acceptable range. These results are shown in Appendix C.

We then re-estimate the model, excluding market capitalisation while retaining industry fixed
effects. As shown in Appendix D, this adjustment significantly reduces multicollinearity. Most
VIFs fall below 5, although the environmental score (9.96) and beta (8.27) remain moderately
high. In summary, this indicates that market capitalisation was the primary source of
multicollinearity in the original model. Although VIFs for environmental score and beta remain
above 5, no further variables were removed due to their theoretical importance to the model.

After removing market capitalisation from the model, all regressions were re-estimated across
short-term, medium-term, and long-term intervals. The results are summarised in Appendix E.
When we removed market cap, no significant changes occurred in the main findings. In the
short- and medium-term windows, the coefficient on environmental score remains statistically
insignificant, and the explanatory power of the models (adjusted R-squared) remains very low.
Environmental score and CARs are positively correlated in the long-term window, with a
statistical significance at the 10% confidence level. Debt-to-equity continues to have a strong
negative relationship with abnormal returns across all windows. Taken as a whole, these
robustness checks establish that the original findings do not emerge from multicollinearity
caused by market size and that the link between environmental performance and stock returns
is consistent with or without market cap.

We also test the sensitivity of our results to the estimation window choice. In addition to the
main analysis using a 90-day estimation window, we re-run all regressions using a shorter 30-
day estimation window. Results from the 30-day estimation window are presented in Appendix
F.

The results from our alternative model are generally in line with the original analysis. For the
short-term and medium-term event windows, the environmental score remains statistically

                                                                          29
insignificant in the full set of specifications, and the models' explanatory power remains low.
However, the long-term window shows different results. Here, the relationship between
environmental score and CARs becomes stronger, showcasing a positive coefficient at a 1%
significance level for all specifications.

Debt-to-equity shows a negative relationship with abnormal returns in the long-term window.
However, the relationship weakens somewhat compared to the 90-day estimation window. In
our main model, beta was previously insignificant. This shifted in the alternative model, where
beta now shows a statistically significant negative relationship with abnormal returns at the 5%

5. Discussion

5.1 Interpretation of Results

Our results indicate that environmental performance was not reflected in a firm's abnormal
stock returns in the immediate and medium-term aftermath of the January 2025 California
wildfires. However, the long-term results are less clear, with some signs of environmental
performance affecting firms’ abnormal returns.

In the short window ([-1,+1] day), none of the CAR models (1-3) indicate any significant
relationship between firms’ environmental score and their CAR. The same is true for the t-tests,
where, though a slight difference exists between firms above and below the median
environmental score, it is not significant. This indicates that environmental performance was
not a factor investors considered when reacting to the initial shock of the fire outbreak. This
behaviour could have a couple of different explanations. One likely reason is that, due to the
shock of the fires starting unexpectedly, the lack of available details about the fires, and the
scarcity of time to make in-depth analysis, the investors’ reactions in the short term were mainly
emotional. This emotional reaction would entail that investors decided to buy, hold, or sell
without much analysis supporting the decision, and thus, firms’ environmental scores did not
have an impact. An alternative explanation could be that, as the fires had just started and due to

                                                                          30
the uncertainty surrounding the damages they would cause, investors were unsure about how to
consider the environmental performance of firms when making investment decisions, thus
leading to no clear relationship.

The result remains unchanged in the medium window ([-5,+5] days). Significance still cannot
be found, as neither the regression nor the t-test shows any significant relationship between
environmental and market performance. This points towards the market still not considering
environmental performance as a deciding factor, even after the initial shock. As the fires were
still highly active during this period, the uncertainty about their total impact and how to
incorporate environmental scores in investment decisions could still explain this. Of course,
another possible explanation for the lack of relationship is that the market does not think that
environmental performance is relevant to how a firm is affected by wildfires. If investors do
not see a connection between the two, then it makes sense not to make any investments based
on environmental performance. Lastly, investors may consider environmental performance
relevant, but their analyses prioritize other variables. Therefore, even though it is included in
investors’ analysis, there is no significant connection between environmental performance and
CAR.

However, things change when the event window is lengthened ([-30,+30] days). Over this
approximately two-month period, the t-test still does not indicate a significant relationship, and
the same goes for models 1 and 2 regression. However, the model 3 regression, which includes
control variables and industry fixed effects, shows a slightly significant positive relationship
between firms’ environmental performance and their CAR, at a 10% significance level. The
fact that there is only a significant relationship when we control for multiple variables points
towards the fact that other factors have a more substantial impact on CAR, so unless they are
controlled for, environmental performance’s significant positive impact will not be visible.
Since significance only exists in our main model (including control variables and industry fixed
effect), omitted variable bias may exist in the simpler models. This could explain the lack of
significance in the t-test, as it does not include any controls.

This means that while investors did not consider the environmental aspect in the short- or
medium-term, in the long term, to a certain degree, investors started to reward (or give less
punishment) to firms which had a strong environmental performance. This raises the question
of why investors changed their behaviour and started considering it when making investment
decisions. One apparent potential explanation is that all fires were put out during the long-term

                                                                          31
window. This means the shock, the emotional reactions, and the uncertainty regarding the scale
of damage had passed. As the uncertainty disappeared, it likely became easier for investors to
include environmental performance in their analyses. Once they did, they may have viewed
high environmental performance companies as better investments after the wildfires, either
because they seemed more prepared for future regulations, or because they felt such firms
deserved less punishment for non-pecuniary motives. An alternative explanation for this change
in market behaviour could be that the behaviour of firms caused investors to inadvertently treat
firms with high environmental performance more favourably than those with low performance.
Likely, firms with higher environmental performance responded directly or indirectly to the
wildfires in line with their typical practices, for example, presenting certain initiatives or
precautions for the future. It is also likely that firms with low environmental performance did
not respond in the same way or to the same extent. The market may have rewarded this
difference in behaviour, which could thus have led to environmental performance having a
significantly positive impact on CAR. This is not because investors specifically look at firms'
environmental performance when making decisions, but because the market appreciates the
behaviour that results in high environmental performance in the post-disaster stage.

It is important to highlight that the significance of the environmental factor was sensitive to the
choice of estimation window for the market model. When we, as a part of our robustness
consideration, tried to run the same regression, but instead based on a 30-day estimation
window, the results were similar in the short- and medium-term, showing no significant
relationship. However, in the long term ([-30,30] days) the 30 day estimation window results
indicated a significant positive relationship between environmental performance and CAR for
all three models. The fact that the estimation window has such a large effect on the significance
level is problematic, making our results less robust. It also raises the question of the optimal
number of days for the estimation window. On the one hand, using only 30 days could be
considered too short to capture normal market conditions, as seasonal biases could influence
those days. This would make a 90-day period more robust. On the other hand, the longer 90 day
period might include unrelated events that distort the estimation, such as the US election, which
had a major market impact. In contrast, the shorter 30 day window might better reflect
expectations before the wildfires without being affected by external shocks. Many different
estimation windows could be argued for. However, the results are sensitive to the estimation
window, and thus a singular result from one estimation window should not be seen as a
definitive conclusion.

                                                                          32
The control variables in the regressions offer additional insights into what drove abnormal
returns during the wildfire event. Firm size (market cap) was not a significant predictor of CAR
in any specification, suggesting that large-cap stocks were impacted similarly to mid-cap stocks
in the S&P 500 after controlling for other factors. However, financial leverage (debt-to-equity
ratio) acted as a negative and significant predictor of CARs in model 2 for all estimation
windows and in model 3 for the long-term window. Firms with higher debt levels tended to
underperform, which makes intuitive sense in a crisis context. Highly leveraged firms may be
viewed as riskier and less flexible regarding shocks. The stock level beta (systematic risk) did
not significantly affect the CAR in any time period or model. Though one would think that beta
should have a strong effect, considering that it is connected to market movements, this result
indicates that beta has a weak effect in the event of a market shock. These effects of the control
variables seem relatively reasonable and reinforce the model's validity. They align with typical
risk factors that would exacerbate or mitigate the impact of a market-wide shock like a natural
disaster.

5.2 Comparison to Hypothesis and Previous Literature

The findings clearly indicate that our hypothesis is rejected in the short- and medium-term
windows. In the long term, evidence points towards both rejecting and accepting our hypothesis.
Though the t-test and the model 1 and 2 regressions do not show any signs of a significant
relationship between environmental performance and CAR, the third model does indicate a
slightly significant relationship. Additionally, when using a shorter estimation window, results
for both the t-test and all regression models indicate a strongly significant relationship. With
this in mind, we can neither reject nor accept the hypothesis in the long term, as the results are
inconclusive. Therefore, more research needs to be conducted to achieve clear results.

The contrasts between our hypothesis and actual results indicate that the overall positive
sentiment in academia surrounding the usefulness of companies having a strong environmental
performance might not be as clear in reality, something which is pointed out in the article by
Margolis & Walsh (2003). The article finds that much of the research that points towards the
usefulness of ESG is conducted in a doubtful manner, which puts its results into question,
indicating that environmental performance does not necessarily need to positively affect firm
performance. Our findings further support this reasoning, with only some signs of
environmental performance positively affecting firm performance in the long term and no
evidence pointing towards a relationship in the short- and medium-term.

                                                                          33
Both the articles by Huynh et al. (2021) and Malik et al. (2023) indicate that environmental
performance positively impacts companies' stock market performance in the case of natural
disasters, as it gives firms increased resilience towards sell-offs. As mentioned, in the short-
and medium-term, our results do not align with the findings from these articles. This could
potentially be explained by the nature of the disaster that our study covered. The wildfires
spanned for many weeks and resulted in a lot of uncertainty, with the total damages not
becoming clear until a long time after the fires started. In comparison, these papers investigate
multiple types of natural disasters. The average natural disaster may only have lasted for a few
days, and thus patterns in the stock market reaction might have been identified quickly. In the
long term, we have some results that point towards environmental performance, providing the
type of resilience mentioned in the previous literature. However, as has been established, our
results are inconclusive and not robust, whereas the articles indicate a clear relationship. The
difference in robustness of results can likely be explained by the fact that both papers investigate
multiple natural disaster events, while we look at one specific instance. It could be more likely
that strong patterns emerge when you observe a higher number of events.

Our short- and medium-term findings align with past research suggesting low investor
differentiation during crises. Studies such as Sakariyahu et al. (2023) note that initial market
reactions to disasters often lack differentiation due to the high emotional impact and intense
media coverage. In our case, this likely delayed the market’s recognition of differences in
environmental performance until the shock had passed and the damage became clearer.

Overall, our findings align with the previous literature in some aspects but contradict it in others.
This highlights the need for more research in this area, which has received relatively limited
attention so far.

5.3 Limitations & Suggestions for Future Research

While our findings offer valuable insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. First,
our analysis focused on a single event, the January 2025 California wildfires. The
generalisability of our findings was naturally limited as a result. Depending on the type, size,
publicity level, and effects of the disaster, investors’ behaviour and the impact of environmental
performance may differ greatly. Future research could take our line of inquiry a step further by
conducting the same comparison over more than a single environmental event to see if these
trends apply more widely.

                                                                          34
Second, we attempted to improve our empirical design by using a second estimation window
(30 days in addition to the original 90 days) to test the sensitivity of our model. The results of
this test suggest a certain sensitivity to this variation. The environmental performance
coefficient's significance and size changed depending on the window applied, especially in
long-term analysis. This suggests more work should be done to assess how varying estimation
spans may affect event studies in environmental performance contexts.

A third limitation is that scores on environmental performance may suffer from measurement
error. We rely on Refinitiv Eikon’s environmental score as a proxy for firm-level environmental
performance. Environmental scores are subjective and vary by provider, which may limit
comparability. This could result in some investors’ reactions to environmental performance not
being captured in our study, as they may have relied on other rating providers that differ from
Refinitiv Eikon’s. Furthermore, suppose investors choose to look at multiple environmental
scores, including Refinitiv Eikon’s, and they differ, as found in the study by Serafeim et al.
(2022). In that case, investors may not react to environmental performance, as the conflicting
information can create confusion about how to interpret it. Future work might compare
alternative sources of environmental performance data to see if results differ. It could also
examine ESG scores, including social and governance factors.

                                                                          35
An additional limitation of the study is that we only look at S&P 500 companies. Though these
companies make up the majority of the American market cap, this does not necessarily mean
that they accurately represent the entire market. Companies with smaller market caps or that
are less actively traded might not respond in the same ways to fires as the companies in the S&P
500. In the overall sample characteristics we also saw that most environmental scores clustered
around 64, with low variation across firms. This suggests that the S&P 500 may be too
homogeneous in terms of environmental performance, which limits our ability to detect
meaningful differences. A natural extension of our research could thus be to conduct larger
studies, including more companies than those in the S&P 500.

Finally, multicollinearity was an issue in our regression models, particularly concerning firm
size. While we conducted a robustness check excluding market capitalisation, which produced
much the same results, the presence of high-variance inflation factors in our full model indicates
that one should be cautious when interpreting individual effects from intercorrelated variables
to a certain extent. Future research may also want to try alternative methods or expand the
sample size so as not to confront these difficulties.

6. Conclusion

This thesis explored the impact of firms' environmental performance on their stock returns
during the January 2025 California wildfires. We used an event study approach on the S&P 500
companies as of 1 January 2025, where we controlled for key firm characteristics and analysed
abnormal stock returns across a short-, medium-, and long-term event window. Our
investigation aimed to determine if the market would treat firms with higher environmental
scores differently when faced with a climate-related crisis.

The results show no significant differences between firms with high and low environmental
scoring in the short- and medium-term event windows. In the long-term, the results are mixed.
Two models show no significant effect, while our main model shows a weak relationship.
Furthermore, short- and medium-term effects remained the same when conducting the same
test based on a shorter (30-day) estimation window. However, all results for the long-term
window pointed towards a strongly significant positive relationship between environmental
performance and CAR. The results' sensitivity to the estimation window adds to the uncertainty
about the long-term effect.

                                                                          36
Concerning these findings, previous research has suggested that firms with high environmental
performance are particularly resilient in times of crisis. Although initial market responses may
be non-discriminatory, evaluations indicate greater trust in businesses with strong
environmental profiles over time. This provides empirical support for the idea that
environmental performance can offer reputational or behavioural advantages in adversity,
resulting in relatively superior performance during recovery phases.

At the same time, our results also suggest that there is no immediate environmental effect, as
no significant association was found between environmental performance and stock returns in
either of the short or medium-term windows. This is consistent with existing theories of investor
overreaction and initial non-discriminatory reactions across the entire market. However, our
inconclusive results in the long-term window do not fully align with much of the previous
literature but do also not directly contradict it.

In sum, this thesis adds to the growing literature on environmental performance and financial
performance by investigating whether environmental performance affected market responses to
the January 2025 California wildfires. The results indicate no clear relationship between the
two in the short- and medium-term, while the long-term results are inconclusive. This highlights
the need for more research to clarify the long-term relationship.

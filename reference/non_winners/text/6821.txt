Talk, Walk, and the CSRD:  Evidence on the Disclosure-Performance Gap

Abstract:

   Whether mandatory sustainability disclosure reflects real corporate performance is central to
   evaluating the effectiveness of recent regulation. This paper studies whether the first year of
   the EU’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive narrowed the  gap between firms’
   sustainability disclosure (“talk”) and third-party-assessed performance (“walk”). We exploit
   the directive’s 500-employee scope threshold in a difference-in-differences design on 132
   European listed firms observed from 2021 to 2024. The composite talk–walk gap widens after
   CSRD, and the widening is concentrated in the environmental dimension. A decomposition
   shows that the change reflects a surge in disclosure language with no movement in assessed
   performance. The effect is largest among  firms with the most disclosure ground to cover,
   namely non-Nordic firms and firms whose pre-period talk and walk were already aligned. This
   pattern is consistent with compliance-driven expansion of mandated reporting rather than
   deliberate overclaiming, and highlights a measurement issue that any text-based study of
    greenwashing in regulated settings will need to confront.

Keywords:

   CSRD; ESRS; Sustainability disclosure; text-as-data; ESG performance

1. Introduction

Mandatory sustainability disclosure has become one of the most ambitious instruments
of European corporate regulation in the past decade. The EU’s Corporate Sustainability
Reporting Directive (CSRD), adopted in December 2022, requires in-scope firms to report
under the European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS) and to subject those
disclosures to third-party assurance. The directive’s stated aim is to make sustainability
information comparable, verifiable, and decision-useful for investors, regulators, and other
stakeholders. Whether the directive achieves that aim depends on a basic question: does
mandated reporting bring firms’ sustainability claims closer to their actual performance,
or does it primarily expand the volume of language without changing what firms do? This
paper studies whether Wave 1 of CSRD narrowed the gap between firms’ sustainability
disclosure (“talk”) and third-party-assessed performance (“walk”).
   Our empirical strategy uses a difference-in-differences design on 132 European listed
firms observed from 2021 to 2024, exploiting the directive’s 500-employee scope thresh-
old. The estimation sample is restricted to firms with 125 to 1,000 employees, placing
treated and control firms in a comparable size range while preserving statistical power.
Talk is measured from the text of annual reports using a keyword dictionary built on
Andrikogiannopoulou, Krueger, Mitali, and Papakonstantinou (2022) and extended with
CSRD-specific vocabulary. Walk is measured using LSEG’s ESG pillar scores, which
aggregate verified emissions, resource-use, and innovation indicators rather than relying
solely on firm self-reporting (Aswani, Raghunandan, and Rajgopal, 2024). Both measures
are standardised as z-scores using pre-period (2021–2023) means, so the gap is expressed
in units of pre-period standard deviations.
   The talk–walk gap widened, not narrowed, in CSRD’s first reporting year. The com-
posite gap rises by roughly two-thirds of a pre-period standard deviation for treated firms
relative to controls, and the widening is concentrated in the environmental dimension
where ESRS imposes its most detailed requirements. A decomposition shows that the
entire widening comes from talk: treated firms’ sustainability language rose sharply after
CSRD, while their assessed environmental performance did not move. A dynamic version
of the design places almost all of the talk adjustment at 2024, the first year of mandatory
ESRS reporting, and shows no differential pre-trend in the immediate pre-period. The
findings are robust to alternative bandwidths, the choice of controls, winsorisation, the
exclusion of 2021, restriction to a balanced panel, and placebo timing.
   The direction of the effect was not obvious in advance. CSRD pulls on two channels that
push in opposite directions. The first is a disciplining channel: by requiring standardised
reporting and external assurance, the directive raises the cost of unsubstantiated claims. A
firm that previously embedded vague commitments in a bespoke sustainability section now
faces a structured template in which omissions and inconsistencies are visible to investors,
auditors, and regulators. Under this channel, firms inflating their rhetoric relative to their
performance can scale back the talk or improve the walk; both responses narrow the gap.
This is the prediction of theories of mandatory disclosure such as Admati and Pfleiderer
(2000), in which forced disclosure improves market efficiency by constraining strategic
withholding.
   The second is a compliance channel. ESRS does not merely require firms to substan-

                                      2
tiate existing claims; it compels them to report across a comprehensive set of topics,
including environmental policies, emission targets, climate risk assessments, and transi-
tion plans, regardless of whether the firm has made meaningful progress in those areas.
This mechanically inflates the textual footprint of sustainability disclosure. Environmen-
tal performance is sticky, reflecting physical investments and verified emissions reductions
that take years to materialise, whereas sustainability language can be updated within a
single reporting cycle. If the compliance channel dominates, the gap widens in the first
reporting year not because firms are greenwashing more deliberately, but because the
regulation itself mandates new vocabulary.
   Our heterogeneity evidence is consistent with the compliance channel. The effect is
largest for non-Nordic firms, which had less developed voluntary reporting traditions
before CSRD, and for firms whose pre-period talk and walk were already aligned, which
had the most disclosure ground to cover under ESRS. Deliberate overclaiming would
predict the opposite pattern, with the largest response among firms with the highest pre-
period gaps. Our design cannot cleanly separate compliance from deliberate overclaiming,
however, and we therefore frame our findings as a disclosure–performance gap rather than
greenwashing in a normative sense.
   Our findings speak directly to recent work on the design of sustainability regulation.
Inderst and Opp (2025) argue that a mandatory taxonomy is necessary to discipline
greenwashing in ESG product markets; their analysis assumes that forced disclosure con-
strains cheap talk. Our evidence suggests that any disciplining effect is slow to appear: in
CSRD’s first reporting year, treated firms’ sustainability language expands sharply while
their assessed performance does not move. We contribute to this literature by providing
the first firm-level test of CSRD’s effect on a disclosure–performance gap, and by flag-
ging a measurement issue that any text-based study of greenwashing in regulated settings
will need to confront: when a regime mandates vocabulary, dictionary-based measures of
corporate language cannot cleanly separate substantive claims from compliance.
Related Literature
   Our paper first contributes to a theoretical literature on how mandatory disclosure
regulation shapes corporate communication. Existing work shows that forced disclosure
improves market efficiency in settings where firms would otherwise strategically withhold
or distort information (Admati and Pfleiderer, 2000), and Inderst and Opp (2025) extend
this logic to ESG product markets, arguing that a mandatory taxonomy is necessary
to discipline greenwashing because voluntary disclosure does not constrain cheap talk.
These models implicitly treat forced disclosure as a tightening device: firms compelled
to substantiate claims have less room to inflate them. Unlike these theoretical studies,
we provide direct empirical evidence on how a binding disclosure mandate affects the
gap between firm rhetoric and firm action. We document that, in its first reporting
year, CSRD widens rather than narrows this gap, and our heterogeneity results trace the
widening to a compliance channel rather than to deliberate overclaiming. Our findings
qualify the timing implicit in these models. Talk responds within a single reporting
cycle because firms can update language quickly; walk is sticky because environmental
performance reflects physical investments that take longer to materialise. Whether the
disciplining effect eventually appears once walk has had time to respond is something our

                                      3
single post-period cannot establish.
   Our paper is also directly related to a growing literature that uses text-based methods
to measure the content of corporate communication. Hoberg and Phillips (2016) estab-
lish text-based methods for measuring product differentiation; Sautner, van Lent, Vilkov,
and Zhang (2023) construct firm-level climate exposure measures from earnings call tran-
scripts using a keyword approach; and Andrikogiannopoulou et al. (2022) build an ESG
keyword library from mutual fund prospectuses. These papers consistently emphasise
that dictionary-based measures capture attention to a topic rather than the substantive
content or credibility of what firms say, and Sautner et al. (2023) label their measure
climate-change exposure for precisely this reason. Complementing these studies, we ex-
tend the Andrikogiannopoulou et al. (2022) library with CSRD-specific vocabulary and
apply it to firm annual reports, treating the resulting measure as one of disclosure in-
tensity rather than commitment quality. We also flag a measurement issue with broader
relevance: when a disclosure regime mandates vocabulary, dictionary-based text mea-
sures cannot cleanly separate substantive claims from mandated compliance. Any future
text-based study of greenwashing in regulated settings will need to confront this problem,
either through claims-level natural-language extraction or through a longer post-period
in which performance has had time to respond.
   Lastly, we contribute to a literature on the measurement, pricing, and real effects
of ESG performance. Aswani, Raghunandan, and Rajgopal (2024) document that raw
firm-reported emissions data suffer from inconsistent reporting boundaries and estima-
tion error; Bolton and Kacperczyk (2021) and Ilhan, Sautner, and Vilkov (2021) find
that carbon risk is priced in equity markets; Krueger, Sautner, and Starks (2020) docu-
ment that institutional investors care about climate risk; Pástor, Stambaugh, and Taylor
(2021) embed sustainable investing in an equilibrium asset-pricing framework; and Hong
and Kacperczyk (2009) and Duchin, Gao, and Xu (2025) study how social norms and
pollution exposure interact with corporate decisions and asset markets. These papers
establish that ESG performance varies across firms in ways that the market prices, and
that measurement choices materially affect inference. Building on these insights, we use
third-party performance measures validated in this literature rather than self-reported
emissions, and report cross-sectional evidence on how the talk–walk gap covaries with
firm size and country of incorporation.
   Most directly, we provide what is, to our knowledge, the first firm-level test of CSRD’s
effect on a disclosure–performance gap. Existing work either evaluates voluntary dis-
closure regimes and infers misalignment from the cross section of self-reported metrics,
or studies CSRD descriptively. Unlike these existing studies, we identify a causal effect
using the directive’s sharp employee threshold and a within-firm difference-in-differences
design that separates disclosure intensity from third-party-assessed performance using in-
dependent data sources. Whether the disciplining effect anticipated by Inderst and Opp
(2025) eventually materialises, narrowing the gap as walk catches up to talk, is the central
question we leave open to future research.

the structure of the paper. The remainder of the paper is organised as follows. Section 2
describes the institutional setting. Section 3 describes the data and the construction of our
talk, walk, and gap measures. Section 4 sets out the empirical strategy and presents the

2. Institutional Background

The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive was adopted by the European Parlia-
ment and Council in December 2022 (European Commission, 2022). CSRD strengthens
and extends the earlier Non-Financial Reporting Directive (NFRD) and requires in-scope
firms to disclose, first, how sustainability issues create risks and opportunities for the
business, and second, how the firm’s own activities affect people and the environment.
This is the so-called double-materiality principle. A central element is standardisation:
firms subject to CSRD must report under the European Sustainability Reporting Stan-
dards, which impose a common template across environmental, social, and governance
topics and are subject to third-party assurance.
   Implementation is phased. Wave 1 applies to firms that were already subject to NFRD
reporting, namely large public-interest entities, and requires reporting for the 2024 fi-
nancial year, with reports published in 2025. Under the NFRD, the scope is tied to
public-interest entities with more than 500 employees, including listed companies, banks,
and insurers. This pre-existing scope rule inherits directly into CSRD Wave 1 and pro-
vides the threshold-based treatment assignment we exploit in Section 4. Subsequent waves
were intended to expand coverage to smaller listed firms and non-listed undertakings, but
the EU has adopted a “stop-the-clock” delay that effectively pushed these back, leaving
Wave 1 as the main binding cohort in our near-term window. Figure 1 summarises the
adoption timeline and Figure 2 illustrates the threshold-based identification setting.
   Conceptually, CSRD and ESRS raise the cost of “cheap talk” by making sustainability
claims easier to verify and compare. The directive could, in principle, nudge firms to tem-
per exaggerated language, improve underlying performance, or both. These are precisely
the channels our talk–walk misalignment measures are designed to capture.

3. Data and Measurement

The analysis uses two samples. The first is a universe sample of European listed firms cov-
ered by LSEG’s ESG data, used for descriptive and cross-sectional evidence in Section 5.
The second is the difference-in-differences estimation sample, constructed around the 500-
employee CSRD threshold and used for the main analysis in Section 4. We describe each
in turn and then the construction of talk, walk, and the gap. Implementation details,
including the full keyword dictionary, weighting scheme, and LSEG variable definitions,
are provided in Appendix A and Appendix B.

3.1 Data sources
We draw on two primary sources: structured data from LSEG Workspace, and firm-level
annual reports. LSEG Workspace, operated by the London Stock Exchange Group, pro-
vides comprehensive data on firm financials, corporate documentation, and sustainability

                                      5
metrics. We use LSEG’s ESG pillar scores, emissions data, and policy-level indicators.
LSEG is widely used in the academic finance literature, and its ESG coverage incorporates
verified emissions data, resource-use metrics, and third-party environmental innovation
assessments rather than relying solely on firm self-reporting (Aswani et al., 2024). For the
DiD sample we additionally collect English-language annual reports for the full analysis
period (financial years 2021 through 2024), from which we extract the text-based talk
measure described below.

3.2 Sample construction
Universe sample. We apply an initial equity screen in LSEG Workspace to isolate Euro-
pean listed firms, yielding 1,475 candidates. We retain firms in LSEG’s “Clean” subset
(those with non-missing ESG pillar scores across the analysis period), reducing the sample
to 996 firms. Excluding 15 firms with more than one missing year for the core regression
variables yields a final universe panel of 981 firms over four years and 3,716 firm-year ob-
servations. We exclude 2025 because ESG data and annual report publication for FY2025
remain incomplete at the time of analysis. Table 1 reports the step-by-step attrition.
Because the universe sample contains essentially no firms below 500 employees (the min-
imum is 504), it cannot replicate the treated-control comparison underlying the DiD; we
use it for descriptive and correlational analysis only.
DiD estimation sample. We apply an equity screen to identify European-domiciled listed
firms in the 125–1,000 employee bandwidth around the CSRD threshold, yielding an initial
sample of 232 firms. For each firm we collect English-language annual reports for financial
years 2021 through 2024 where available. After processing these reports through the talk
extraction pipeline, we obtain talk scores for 208 firms; the remaining 24 either did not
publish English-language reports or yielded reports from which text extraction failed.
Merging talk scores with LSEG walk data and firm metadata leaves 205 firms, with three
lost to identifier mismatches. Re-imposing the employee bandwidth on LSEG’s employee
counts reduces the sample to 139 firms. Requiring each firm to appear in both the pre-
period (2021–2023) and the post-period (2024) removes a further seven firms, giving a
final estimation sample of 132 firms and 487 firm-year observations, of which 69 fall below
and 63 above the 500-employee threshold. Table 2 reports the step-by-step attrition.
  The panel is unbalanced: 98 of the 132 firms are observed in all four years. We
examine the implications in Section 4.3 through a balance comparison of 2021-entry firms
with later entrants and present a fully balanced-panel robustness check in Section 6.5.
Of the 208 firms with successful text extraction, 98 also appear in the universe sample.
The remaining 110 firms are absent from the universe, primarily because they fall below
the 500-employee cutoff that effectively defines the lower bound of LSEG’s ESG-covered
universe.
Treatment assignment and controls. Employee count, the variable determining treatment
assignment, is time-invariant in our analysis. LSEG reports a single employee figure per
firm (the most recent available fiscal-year value), which we apply across all years of the
panel. While this is a simplification, it is standard in threshold-based DiD designs; using
time-varying assignment would introduce endogeneity concerns if firms strategically ad-
justed employee counts in response to the regulation. We use log market capitalisation

                                      6
and log bid-ask spread as time-varying firm-level controls. The bid-ask spread is a stan-
dard proxy for market liquidity and information asymmetry (Amihud, 2002); including it
addresses the concern that liquidity differences between treated and control firms could
confound the estimated treatment effect.
   Some firms have missing ESG pillar scores for individual years. We do not fill missing
values with zero, since a missing LSEG score indicates that LSEG did not assess the
firm in that year, not that the firm scored zero. Instead we apply within-firm linear
interpolation for gaps of at most one year. In the final estimation sample, no surviving
observation relies on interpolated walk scores; main results are based entirely on directly
observed LSEG pillar scores.

3.3 Talk measure
We measure sustainability disclosure intensity using a text-based talk vector constructed
from firm annual reports via a keyword dictionary. Throughout the paper, talk refers to
the volume and vocabulary of sustainability discussion in a firm’s annual report, not to
the substantive content, credibility, or specificity of what the firm says it is doing. The
dictionary builds on the ESG keyword library of Andrikogiannopoulou et al. (2022), ex-
tended with CSRD-specific terms related to ESRS reporting standards, climate transition
planning, and regulatory language. The complete keyword list and weighting scheme are
provided in Appendix A.
   The dictionary is positive-only by design: it captures the extent to which firms make
sustainability claims, commitments, and disclosures, but does not penalise firms for dis-
cussing negative events or acknowledging shortcomings. In a greenwashing study, the
talk vector should measure how much the firm claims to be doing about sustainability,
not how optimistically it discusses the topic. Including negative keywords would conflate
disclosure quality with disclosure quantity, which is not the object of our measurement.
   The dictionary is structured across three dimensions (Environmental, Social, and Gov-
ernance), enabling both pillar-specific and composite analysis. Within each dimension,
keywords carry one of two weights: weight 2 for strong, explicit commitments or hard
targets, and weight 1 for softer disclosure, strategy, or policy language. The weight re-
flects the type of vocabulary used, not the substantive strength of the firm’s underlying
commitment; the dictionary cannot distinguish between an aspirational mention of “net
zero” and a detailed decarbonisation roadmap. To avoid counting negated claims, we
implement a window-based negation check that scores zero on keyword matches falling
within five tokens of negation words. Full details are provided in Appendix B.

3.4 Walk measure
The walk measure requires an independent, third-party assessment of sustainability per-
formance. We use LSEG’s ESG pillar scores. LSEG collects over 870 company-level ESG
measures from public sources and uses a subset of 186 measures, selected for cross-firm
comparability and industry materiality, to compute scores across ten thematic categories.
These roll up into three pillar scores (Environmental, Social, Governance); each is a per-
centile rank from 0 to 100, benchmarked against industry peers for the Environmental
and Social pillars and against country of incorporation for Governance.
   We use the base pillar scores rather than the controversy-adjusted ESGC scores because

                                      7
the ESGC discount uses size-dependent weights that would mechanically interact with our
employee-threshold identification strategy. Also, we prefer aggregated pillar scores to raw
firm-reported emissions, given the measurement concerns documented by Aswani et al.
(2024). One limitation is that some inputs to the pillar scores are Boolean indicators
that overlap with what our talk vector captures, creating talk-in-walk contamination.
Two points mitigate the concern. The scores also incorporate quantitative indicators
(actual CO2 emissions, water and energy use, environmental R&D) that reflect operational
outcomes, and any contamination biases against finding a gap, since it pushes talk and
walk in the same direction. Our gap estimates are therefore a conservative lower bound
on the true misalignment.
   We define composite walk as the arithmetic mean of the three pillar scores, with equal
weights to maintain symmetry with our equally-weighted composite talk. We also run all
specifications separately on the Environmental Pillar Score, where we expect the strongest
treatment effect given ESRS’s heavy emphasis on environmental and climate-related re-
porting. The Environmental Pillar aggregates emissions, resource use, and innovation
category scores, percentile-ranked within each firm’s industry group; further detail on
category weights and construction is in Appendix B.

3.6 Limitations of the measurement approach
LSEG’s ESG coverage, while extensive, is not universal; the unbalanced panel reflects this
and Section 6.5 tests robustness to a strict balanced-panel restriction. Moreover, industry
classification is not available in our LSEG extraction for the DiD sample, which prevents
us from including industry fixed effects; firm fixed effects in our TWFE specification
absorb time-invariant industry membership but do not absorb sector-specific time trends.
Finally, pillar scores are percentile ranks within industry peer groups, so our finding
that walk does not move in response to CSRD is a statement about relative rank within
industry, not about absolute environmental performance.

4. Empirical Strategy and Main Results

4.1 Difference-in-differences design
We estimate the effect of CSRD on the talk–walk gap using a difference-in-differences
design that exploits the directive’s employee-based scope threshold. CSRD Wave 1 ap-
plies to publicly listed European firms with more than 500 employees, so firms above this
threshold constitute the treatment group and firms below it the control group. The iden-
tifying assumption is that, absent CSRD, the talk–walk gap would have evolved similarly
for both groups; we assess this assumption formally in Section 4.3. CSRD was adopted
in December 2022, with Wave 1 requiring ESRS-based reporting for financial year 2024
onward. Our pre-period therefore spans 2021–2023 and the post-period is 2024.
   Our main estimating equation is a two-way fixed effects model:

                 gapi,t = αi + γt + β · (Treatedi × Postt) + Xi,tΓ + εi,t, (3)

where αi and γt are firm and year fixed effects, Treatedi = 1{employeesi > 500}, Postt =
1{t ≥ 2024}, and Xi,t includes log market capitalisation and log bid-ask spread as time-
varying controls. We cluster standard errors at the firm level. The coefficient of interest
is β, capturing the differential change in the talk–walk gap for treated firms relative to
controls after CSRD. A positive β indicates that the gap widened more for treated firms;
their sustainability talk increased relative to their walk by more than for comparable
untreated firms.
   We estimate this specification on two samples. The main sample uses a 125–1,000-
employee bandwidth (132 firms, 487 observations); the narrower robustness sample uses
a 250–750-employee bandwidth that improves comparability between treated and control
firms at the cost of statistical power (82 firms, 303 observations). For each bandwidth we
estimate the model using both the composite gap and the environmental gap as dependent
variables.
Threats to identification. We identify three main threats to causal identification. The
first is anticipation. CSRD was adopted in December 2022, giving firms over a year to
prepare before the FY2024 reporting requirement bound. If treated firms began adjusting
their sustainability language before formal implementation, the estimated treatment effect
would be attenuated. The dynamic DiD in Section 4.3 and the 2023 placebo in Section 6.6
bear directly on this; we interpret any resulting bias as making our main estimate a

                                      9
conservative lower bound on the true first-year effect.
   The second is threshold manipulation. If firms strategically adjust their employee count
to remain below the 500-employee cutoff, treatment assignment becomes endogenous.
Our use of time-invariant employee data mitigates this concern, as does the fact that the
threshold was defined by pre-existing NFRD scope rules rather than introduced de novo
by CSRD.
   The third is spillovers (SUTVA). If control firms voluntarily adopted ESRS-style report-
ing in response to competitive pressure or in anticipation of future waves, the treatment-
control contrast would be compressed. Any such spillover biases β toward zero, making
our estimates conservative rather than inflated.
   The 500-employee rule is a sharp threshold, but we do not implement a regression-
discontinuity design. The 125–1,000-employee bandwidth is wide by RD standards, the
running variable is time-invariant, and we have only 132 firms. Our design is a threshold-
based DiD with a cross-sectional treatment indicator, not a local polynomial around a
discontinuity. The robustness of the main coefficient to the narrower 250–750 bandwidth
(Section 6.1) speaks to the same concern that RD would address.

4.2 Main results
Summary statistics for the variables used in the DiD analysis are reported in Table 3.
Table 4 reports the main estimates. In the main 125–1,000 specification, the composite
talk–walk gap widens by 0.648 standard deviations for treated firms relative to controls
after CSRD (p = 0.026), and the environmental gap widens by 0.841 standard deviations
(p = 0.016). The larger environmental effect is consistent with ESRS’s heavy emphasis
on environmental and climate-related reporting, which requires extensive disclosure on
topics such as climate change mitigation, pollution, and biodiversity.
   The 250–750 narrow-bandwidth specification yields coefficients of the same sign and
comparable magnitude (+0.433 for the composite gap, +0.555 for the environmental gap)
but neither reaches conventional significance. The directional consistency, combined with
a 38% reduction in sample size, suggests that the loss of significance reflects imprecision
rather than a qualitatively different finding.
   Among the controls, log market capitalisation enters negatively and is highly significant
in the composite specifications, indicating that larger firms tend to have smaller talk–walk
gaps. This is consistent with the universe-level cross-sectional evidence in Section 5 and
with the intuition that larger firms face greater scrutiny and have more resources for
substantive environmental programmes. Log bid-ask spread is negative but insignificant;
adding the spread control slightly strengthens rather than weakens the main results,
addressing concerns raised in prior literature about the role of market microstructure in
ESG research.
   A positive β does not imply that CSRD increased greenwashing in a normative sense.
As the decomposition in Section 4.4 shows, the widening is driven by a sharp increase in
treated firms’ sustainability language rather than by a deterioration in their environmental
performance. Whether this compliance-driven talk surge represents genuine greenwashing
or a transitional adjustment depends on whether walk catches up in subsequent reporting
years, a question our single post-period cannot answer.

the post-treatment effect, so if anything it makes the post-CSRD reversal more conserva-

sharply once it did.

single post-period observation, the two need not coincide exactly. The sharp 2024 jump in
talk, alongside the lack of movement in walk, is visible in Figure 3. A cluster-robust Wald
test of the joint null β2021 = β2022 = 0 fails to reject for both specifications: W = 1.99
(p = 0.370) for the composite gap and W = 4.16 (p = 0.125) for the environmental gap.
Parallel trends are formally supported in both cases.

4.4 Talk–walk decomposition
Is the widening gap driven by treated firms increasing their language, by a relative decline
in their performance, or by both? To decompose, we estimate the main TWFE specifica-
tion separately with standardised talk and standardised walk as dependent variables. By
construction, βgap ≈ βtalk − βwalk. Table 5 reports the treatment coefficients.
   For the composite dimension, treated firms’ talk rose by 0.694 standard deviations rela-
tive to controls after CSRD (p = 0.012), while their walk showed no significant differential
change (+0.046, p = 0.582). The environmental dimension shows the same pattern: talk
rose by 0.829 SD (p = 0.013), while walk was essentially unchanged (−0.012, p = 0.892).
The widening is driven entirely by a surge in sustainability language; environmental per-

                                      11
formance did not move. The dynamic version of this decomposition (Figure 3) shows
that talk coefficients are modestly negative but statistically insignificant in 2021 and 2022
before jumping sharply in 2024, while walk coefficients are indistinguishable from zero
throughout.
   Two interpretations are consistent with βwalk ≈ 0 and βtalk > 0. Under compliance-
driven disclosure expansion, ESRS requires extensive reporting on environmental policies,
emission targets, climate risk assessments, and transition plans, topics that overlap di-
rectly with the vocabulary our dictionary counts. Firms newly subject to CSRD must
articulate these topics regardless of whether their underlying environmental investments
have changed, mechanically expanding the textual footprint of sustainability. Under de-
liberate overclaiming, firms exploit the expanded ESRS framework to make claims beyond
what compliance requires, using mandated reporting as cover for reputational benefit. Dis-
tinguishing the two requires either a claims-level text measure that isolates substantive
commitments from mandated disclosure, or a longer post-period during which perfor-
mance has had time to respond. Our measure cannot do the first and our data do not yet
allow the second; the heterogeneity evidence in Section 4.5 provides indirect support for
the compliance interpretation.

We re-estimate the main TWFE specification on subsamples defined by binary partitions.

   Firms with above-median market capitalisation show larger coefficients than those be-

and more pressure to demonstrate ESRS compliance. Smaller subsample sizes within each
split reduce statistical power relative to the full-sample specification. Nordic firms exhibit
positive but statistically insignificant coefficients, while non-Nordic firms show large and
highly significant coefficients. Nordic countries, particularly Sweden and Finland, have
historically maintained stronger sustainability reporting traditions, with many firms al-
ready voluntarily reporting under frameworks such as GRI or TCFD before CSRD. For
these firms the marginal increase in sustainability language required by ESRS compliance
was smaller. Non-Nordic firms (predominantly headquartered in Germany, Italy, and
Spain) had more ground to cover, producing a larger talk surge.
   If the gap widening reflected deliberate overclaiming, the response would concentrate
among firms with high pre-period gaps. We find the opposite. The effect is largest for
firms with low pre-period gaps, whose talk and walk were previously well aligned and who
therefore had the most disclosure ground to cover under ESRS. Firms with above-median
pre-period gaps show coefficients of the same sign but substantially smaller magnitude and
no statistical significance. This is the signature of compliance-driven disclosure expansion,
not strategic greenwashing, and it is consistent with the country split: non-Nordic firms,
who had less developed voluntary reporting before CSRD, show the largest effect.

   Taken together, the heterogeneity results reveal that the average treatment effect masks
meaningful variation across observable firm characteristics. The CSRD-induced widening
is concentrated among non-Nordic firms and firms with low pre-period gaps. The pattern
is consistent with a compliance mechanism in which the talk surge is largest for firms
facing the greatest adjustment burden under the new reporting requirements.

5. Cross-Sectional Universe Evidence

To complement the main DiD analysis, we examine descriptive and cross-sectional ev-
idence from the universe sample of 981 European listed firms. Because extracting and
processing annual reports for nearly 1,000 firms is infeasible, the talk measure in this
sample is the structured LSEG environmental proxy described in Section 3.5, with full
construction in Appendix B. Because the universe sample contains essentially no firms
below the 500-employee threshold, the treated-control comparison underlying the DiD
cannot be replicated; we use this sample for descriptive and correlational analysis only.
   The baseline cross-sectional specification is

                 gapE,i,t = δ0 + δ1 log(employeesi) + δ2 log(market capi,t)

where μc are country fixed effects and λt are year fixed effects. For single-year cross-

specification we cluster at the firm level.

5.1 Descriptive trends
Both talk and walk exhibit a clear upward trend over the observation period. The mean
environmental talk proxy increased from 53.6 in 2021 to 57.2 in 2024, and the mean
Environmental Pillar Score (walk) rose from 52.5 to 56.0 over the same period (Figure 4).
Talk consistently exceeds walk but the two move broadly in parallel. The resulting gap
hovers near zero across all four years, with a slight positive spike in 2022 and a return to
baseline in 2023–2024 (Figure 5). This pattern contrasts with the DiD estimation sample,
where the gap widens markedly in 2024 for treated firms. The contrast is expected, since
the universe sample is dominated by large firms (over 5,000 employees) for which the gap
tends to be negative.

5.2 Size–gap relationship
Table 7 reports the cross-sectional regression results. Larger firms have significantly
smaller environmental gaps across all specifications: the coefficient on log(employees)
ranges from −0.144 in the univariate specification to −0.046 in the most saturated panel
specification, and is statistically significant in every case. Log market capitalisation is
consistently negative and significant, reinforcing the size–gap relationship through an al-
ternative size measure. The size-group descriptive statistics confirm this pattern: firms
above 5,000 employees exhibit negative average gaps (walk exceeds talk), while firms in
the 500–1,000 employee range show positive gaps. Larger firms tend to have more devel-
oped environmental programmes and face greater scrutiny from analysts, investors, and

                                      13
regulators, so their performance is more likely to exceed their stated commitments rather
than the reverse.

5.3 Liquidity
log(bid-ask spread) is significantly associated with the gap in bivariate regressions but
the association drops to near zero and becomes insignificant once country fixed effects are
included. This suggests that the unconditional spread–gap correlation is driven by cross-
country differences in market liquidity, with Nordic markets typically more liquid and
showing different average gap levels than Southern European markets, rather than by a
firm-level relationship between liquidity and greenwashing. The finding is directly relevant
to the main DiD: it provides additional reassurance that the bid-ask spread control absorbs
country-level liquidity variation without confounding the estimated treatment effect.

5.4 Returns
We regress annual total returns on the environmental gap, controlling for log market
capitalisation, log employees, and country fixed effects. The gap coefficient is +0.009
(p = 0.33), small, positive, and statistically insignificant. The market does not appear to
differentially price high-gap firms at the annual frequency. While this is a null result it is
worth reporting: the talk–walk misalignment captured by our greenwashing proxy, despite
varying systematically with firm characteristics, is not yet reflected in equity returns. One
possible explanation is that the market has not had sufficient time to incorporate the first
year of CSRD-mandated disclosures into pricing. This is consistent with Bolton and
Kacperczyk (2021), who find that carbon risk is priced in equity markets but note that
the pricing of sustainability-related information evolves as disclosure quality improves.
We treat this test as descriptive.

5.5 Summary
The universe evidence provides useful context. The environmental greenwashing proxy
varies systematically with firm size and geography, with larger firms and firms in countries
with stronger reporting traditions exhibiting smaller or negative gaps. The secular upward
trend in both talk and walk across the full European market suggests that the aggregate
improvement in sustainability disclosure is a market-wide phenomenon, which the DiD
year fixed effects absorb. Together, these patterns are consistent with the DiD results
and support our interpretation that CSRD’s impact on the gap operates through the talk
channel rather than reflecting broader shifts in the European sustainability landscape.

6. Robustness

This section assesses whether the main DiD results are sensitive to the specification choices
made throughout the analysis. We examine six dimensions: bandwidth choice, inclusion
of controls, outlier influence, the compositionally distinct 2021 subsample, restriction to
a balanced panel, and placebo timing. Section 6.7 summarises coefficient stability across
all six. Detailed estimates are reported in Tables 8 and 9.

                                      14
6.1 Bandwidth sensitivity
The main specification uses a 125–1,000-employee bandwidth. We also estimate the model
on a narrower 250–750-employee bandwidth that improves comparability between treated
and control firms at the cost of sample size. For the narrow sample, the composite
coefficient remains positive (β = +0.433) but does not reach statistical significance (p =
0.331), and the environmental coefficient follows the same pattern. Coefficient signs are
identical to the main specification and magnitudes are around 30% smaller but in the
same range. We attribute the reduced precision to the smaller sample (82 firms and 303
observations) rather than to a substantively different effect. Directional consistency across
bandwidths suggests that the main finding is not an artefact of including firms far from
the threshold.

6.2 Sensitivity to controls
We re-estimate the TWFE specification under three configurations: no controls; log mar-
ket capitalisation only; and the full specification with both log market capitalisation and
log bid-ask spread. Coefficients are highly stable. The unconditional specification yields
a composite coefficient of +0.607 (p = 0.041) and an environmental coefficient of +0.799
(p = 0.025). Adding log market capitalisation alone produces nearly identical estimates.
The full specification slightly strengthens the result. Across all three configurations, co-
efficients vary by less than 0.05 and significance is preserved throughout. The main effect
is not sensitive to the choice of controls; neither liquidity nor firm size confounds the
treatment effect.

6.3 Outlier robustness
To assess whether extreme observations drive the result, we winsorise the gap variables at
the 5th and 95th percentiles and re-estimate the main specification. The composite gap
remains positive and significant (β = +0.640, p = 0.002), as does the environmental gap
(β = +0.775, p = 0.001). Significance increases relative to the unwinsorised specification,
indicating that the main result is not driven by outliers; removing extreme values reduces
residual variance and produces a more precise estimate of the average treatment effect.

6.4 Excluding the 2021 subsample
2021 was the first full post-COVID reporting year, during which corporate disclosure
patterns may have been atypical. As a sensitivity check, we re-estimate the main DiD
restricting the sample to 2022–2024 only. The results yield coefficients of the same sign
and comparable magnitude with statistical significance preserved (composite: β = +0.617,
p = 0.041; environmental: β = +0.808, p = 0.028). These estimates are within 0.05 of the
main specification, confirming that the main result is not driven by post-COVID-specific
dynamics.

6.5 Balanced panel
The main panel is unbalanced: while 132 firms enter the estimation sample, only 98 are
observed in all four years. The remaining 34 firms appear in three or fewer years, pri-
marily because their earliest annual reports were unavailable. To investigate whether this
compositional variation affects the results, we re-estimate the main DiD using only the 98

                                      15
firms observed across all of 2021–2024. Coefficient signs and approximate magnitudes are
preserved, but significance weakens (composite: β = +0.470, p = 0.160; environmental:
β = +0.648, p = 0.095). The composite gap loses significance at conventional thresholds,
while the environmental gap remains marginally significant at the 10% level.
   This is the most demanding robustness check we conduct. The drop in significance
reflects two compounding factors: a 20% reduction in panel size (from 488 to 391 firm-year
observations) and the loss of firms whose entry timing carried informative variation about
the treatment effect. We retain the unbalanced panel as our main specification because
it preserves meaningful variation that a strict balanced restriction discards, and because
the central finding holds across the other five robustness dimensions. We acknowledge
that the balanced-panel result introduces some uncertainty about the magnitude, though
not the direction, of the treatment effect.

6.6 Placebo tests
As a further check that the estimated treatment effect is specific to the actual CSRD
implementation date, we conduct placebo tests on the pre-period. We restrict the sample
to the pre-period (2021–2023), exclude all post-CSRD observations, and re-estimate the
DiD model as if the directive had been implemented for 2022 and 2023 respectively.
Under the null, these regressions should yield insignificant coefficients. Table 9 reports
the results.
   Neither placebo year produces a statistically significant treatment effect. With 2022 as
the hypothetical policy year, the composite and environmental gap coefficients are +0.078
and +0.104 respectively, both small in magnitude and far from significance. With 2023
as the hypothetical policy year, the coefficients are somewhat larger (+0.219 for com-
posite and +0.249 for environmental) but remain insignificant. The 2023 environmental
coefficient (p = 0.171) approaches marginal significance and could reflect early antici-
pation effects, with firms aware of upcoming CSRD requirements beginning to adjust
their reporting language in 2023. Any such anticipation biases our main 2024 coefficient
downward by shifting part of the talk adjustment into the pre-period, and the dynamic
DiD (with 2023 as the reference year) absorbs any 2023 adjustment into the baseline.
This makes the main estimate a conservative lower bound on the true first-year treatment
effect. Since neither placebo coefficient meets conventional significance thresholds, the
placebo tests support the interpretation that the statistically significant effect we observe
is tied to the actual policy implementation in 2024.

6.7 Summary
The main results are robust across all six dimensions tested. Coefficient signs are pre-
served in every specification, and magnitudes vary by less than 0.2 standard deviations
across most checks. The two specifications where statistical significance weakens (nar-
row bandwidth and balanced panel) reflect reduced statistical power from substantially
smaller samples rather than qualitatively different findings, and in both cases the coeffi-
cient direction and approximate magnitude are preserved. Taken together, these results
support the conclusion that the CSRD-induced widening of the talk–walk gap reflects a
genuine treatment effect rather than an artefact of specific modelling choices.

                                      16
7. Discussion

7.1 Interpreting the positive coefficient
The positive β is consistent with the compliance channel dominating in the first reporting
year. It does not imply that CSRD failed or that greenwashing increased in a normative
sense. The decomposition shows that walk did not deteriorate; firms subject to CSRD are
not performing worse environmentally, they are disclosing more extensively. The question
is therefore not whether CSRD caused harm, but whether the talk surge it triggered
reflects genuine regulatory compliance (firms fulfilling their ESRS reporting obligations)
or strategic overclaiming (firms inflating sustainability language beyond what compliance
requires). Our data cannot distinguish these within the current observation window.
   The compliance interpretation is reinforced by the heterogeneity results. The effect
is concentrated among firms that had the most adjustment to make: firms with low
pre-period gaps and firms headquartered outside Nordic countries. This pattern is more
consistent with adaptation to new regulatory requirements than with strategic behaviour,
since deliberate greenwashers would presumably be found among firms with high pre-
period gaps rather than among firms whose talk and walk were previously aligned.
   Our findings speak to the framework of Inderst and Opp (2025), who argue that a
mandatory taxonomy is necessary to discipline greenwashing. Their analysis implicitly
assumes that forced disclosure constrains cheap talk; our evidence suggests a nuanced
sequencing. In the short run, forced disclosure first increases the volume of sustainability
language as firms adapt to a new reporting framework, before the disciplining mechanism
takes hold in subsequent reporting cycles. Whether this disciplining effect eventually
materialises, narrowing the gap as walk catches up to talk, is the central question open
to future research.

7.2 Short-run versus long-run effects
The most consequential limitation of our analysis is that we observe only one post-
treatment year. Our estimates are therefore inherently short-run. Under the compliance
interpretation, the gap should narrow in subsequent years as two forces converge: treated
firms’ walk catches up through actual environmental investments, and the initial language
adjustment plateaus, since firms can only adopt ESRS terminology once and the marginal
talk increase should diminish in later reporting cycles. Under the deliberate overclaiming
interpretation, the gap would instead persist or widen further as firms continue to exploit
the expanded disclosure framework for reputational benefit without corresponding action.
Distinguishing between these trajectories requires extending the analysis to FY2025 and
beyond, a natural and important direction for future research.

7.3 External validity
Our sample is restricted to European listed firms with 125–1,000 employees, a narrow size
band around the CSRD threshold. Results may not generalise to very large firms, which
were already subject to NFRD reporting and had established sustainability infrastructure,
or to non-listed firms, which face different disclosure incentives and market pressures. The
universe cross-sectional evidence in Section 5 provides partial reassurance: the size–gap
relationship at the threshold is consistent with broader patterns across the European

                                      17
market, where larger firms systematically show smaller or negative gaps.
   Geographic concentration is also relevant. Around 27% of our DiD sample consists
of Swedish firms, with Germany and Finland as the next-largest country groups. The
country heterogeneity analysis showed that the effect is concentrated in non-Nordic firms,
but coverage within that group is dominated by a few countries. Results may differ
for Southern or Eastern European firms with less developed capital markets and differ-
ent regulatory traditions. Sector-level heterogeneity also remains untested: the absence
of industry classification in our LSEG extraction prevents us from examining whether
the treatment effect differs across high- versus low-emission industries, an important di-
mension given that ESRS imposes particularly detailed requirements on climate-intensive
sectors.

7.4 Measurement limitations
Several measurement choices could influence interpretation. The talk measure, while
grounded in Andrikogiannopoulou et al. (2022) and extended with CSRD-specific termi-
nology, is a simplified representation of corporate sustainability language. It captures
keyword frequency but not semantic content, rhetorical tone, or the specificity of claims.
A firm that mentions “net zero” once in a vague aspirational statement receives the same
weight-2 score as one providing a detailed, science-aligned decarbonisation roadmap.
Moreover, the positive-only design of the dictionary may amplify the measured com-
pliance effect, since ESRS compliance generates precisely the kind of affirmative policy
and target language the dictionary is designed to capture. More sophisticated natural-
language-processing approaches, for instance large language models trained on sustainabil-
ity disclosure, could capture qualitative distinctions in disclosure quality but are beyond
the scope of this thesis.
   LSEG’s Environmental Pillar Scores aggregate multiple indicators using proprietary
weighting, which introduces opacity into the walk measure. Pillar scores are also relative
rankings within industry peer groups: a firm’s walk score reflects its position relative to
peers rather than its absolute environmental impact. A firm whose emissions increase
in absolute terms may nonetheless see its walk score improve if industry peers deterio-
rate faster, a subtlety that complicates the interpretation of walk as a pure measure of
environmental performance. Finally, the gap construction is a simple linear difference of
z-scores, which treats a one-unit increase in talk identically regardless of starting level.
In practice, the marginal greenwashing content of an additional unit of talk may differ
between a firm already disclosing extensively and one previously silent.

7.5 Summary
Despite these limitations, the core finding (that CSRD’s first year produced a signifi-
cant, compliance-driven widening of the talk–walk gap) rests on a credible identification
strategy, is robust to a range of specification choices, and is supported by an economi-
cally coherent mechanism. The results contribute to an empirical literature that, to our
knowledge, has not yet tested firm-level effects of CSRD using talk–walk gap measures.

                                      18
8. Conclusion

Wave 1 of the EU’s Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive did not narrow the gap
between what firms say about sustainability and what third parties say they do. In the
directive’s first reporting year, the composite talk–walk gap widened by 0.65 standard
deviations for treated firms and the environmental gap by 0.84, driven entirely by a surge
in disclosure language with no movement in performance. The effect concentrates among
firms with the most disclosure ground to cover, namely non-Nordic firms and firms with
initially aligned talk and walk, which is the opposite of what deliberate overclaiming
would predict.
   We interpret the positive coefficient as compliance-driven expansion of mandated dis-
closure, not as evidence that CSRD increased greenwashing in a normative sense. Treated
firms are disclosing more, not performing worse. Two caveats shape the conclusion. Our
talk measure captures disclosure intensity rather than the substantive content of firm
claims, so a compliance-driven expansion and a deliberate overclaiming response are ob-
servationally equivalent under a dictionary-based text measure. We also observe only one
post-treatment year. Whether walk catches up from FY2025 onward, or the gap persists,
is a question our data cannot yet answer.
   The paper contributes to existing research in two ways. Empirically, it provides the
first firm-level test of CSRD’s effect on a disclosure–performance gap, documenting a
compliance-driven widening in the first reporting year rather than the narrowing that dis-
ciplining theories of mandatory disclosure would predict. Methodologically, it flags a mea-
surement issue that any text-based greenwashing study in regulated settings will need to
confront: when a disclosure regime mandates vocabulary, dictionary-based measures can-
not cleanly separate substantive claims from compliance. Three directions would sharpen
what our design cannot resolve. Extending the post-period to FY2025 and beyond would
distinguish the compliance and overclaiming trajectories. Claims-level natural-language
extraction (using large language models to isolate substantive commitments and targets
from mandated risk disclosures) would resolve the interpretive ambiguity. Broader cov-
erage across Southern and Eastern Europe, and across industries with different climate
exposures, would speak to external validity in settings our estimation sample does not
represent.

A. Keyword Dictionary

This appendix documents the full keyword dictionary used to construct the talk measure
(Section 3.3). The dictionary builds on the ESG keyword library of Andrikogiannopoulou
et al. (2022), extended with CSRD-specific terms related to ESRS reporting standards,
climate transition planning, and regulatory language. Keywords are organised across
three dimensions (Environmental, Social, Governance) and assigned one of two weights.
Weight 2 keywords indicate strong, explicit commitments or hard targets; weight 1 key-
words reflect softer disclosure, strategy, or policy language. Keywords appearing in more
than one pillar are marked with a dagger (†); a single mention contributes to each pillar
it appears in but does not affect the composite disproportionately, since the composite is
the arithmetic mean of the three pillars.
   The negation logic complements the dictionary. For each keyword match we examine
a window of five tokens on either side and check for negation words (“not”, “no”, “never”,
“without”, “unable”, “lack”, “failure”). If a negation word is found within this window the
keyword match scores zero rather than its dictionary weight. The window does not cross
sentence boundaries: a negation word at the end of one sentence cannot nullify a keyword
at the beginning of the next. For example, “We have not yet set a science-based target”
correctly scores zero for “science-based target”, while two adjacent sentences such as “[. . . ]
challenges remain. Our science-based target covers Scope 1 and 2 emissions” correctly
score the keyword at full weight.
   After scoring, raw pillar scores are normalised by dividing by the total word count of
the annual report and multiplying by 1,000. The composite score is the arithmetic mean
of the three pillar scores. The dictionary is positive-only by design: it captures the extent
to which firms make sustainability claims, commitments, and disclosures, but does not
penalise firms for discussing negative events or acknowledging shortcomings. Including
negative keywords would conflate disclosure quality with disclosure quantity, which is not
the object of our measurement.

“sustainability reporting” and “integrated reporting” appear in the Governance dictionary
despite having environmental relevance, reflecting their primary association with gover-

both the E and G pillar scores but does not affect the composite score disproportionately,
since the composite is the arithmetic mean of the three pillars.

B.2 Structured environmental talk proxy
Processing annual report text for 981 universe firms is not feasible. For the universe
analysis we therefore construct a structured environmental talk proxy using three con-
tinuous LSEG scores that capture the same conceptual dimensions as the annual-report
dictionary: whether the firm formally articulates environmental policies, sets quantitative
targets, and assesses climate-related risk. The proxy is
                  1                                                              
   talkproxy E,i,t = 3 PolicyEmissionsi,t + TargetsEmissionsi,t + ClimateRiskOppi,t . (7)

   The three components parallel the sustainability-topic structure of the annual-report
dictionary: Policy Emissions corresponds to policy-level vocabulary (climate policy, en-
vironmental policy), Targets Emissions corresponds to target-setting vocabulary (emis-
sions reduction target, net zero), and Climate Change Risks/Opportunities corresponds
to strategic assessment vocabulary (climate risk, transition plan). All three range from
0 to approximately 100 with meaningful cross-sectional variation (means 57.6, 57.2, 52.1;
standard deviations 18.6, 32.8, 29.2). The proxy is environmental-only because the LSEG
structured fields available for the social and governance dimensions do not offer a compa-
rable set of commitment-focused scores.
   We considered and excluded two binary alternatives. Waste Reduction Initiatives is
coded “yes” for over 92% of firm-year observations, providing virtually no cross-sectional
variation. Climate Change Strategy suffers from severe missingness in the early years of
the sample (265 non-null observations in 2021 and 731 in 2022, out of approximately 1,000
firms), which would introduce a compositional break in the middle of the pre-period.

B.3 LSEG walk score construction
LSEG’s Environmental Pillar Score aggregates three category scores: emissions (com-
mitment and effectiveness in reducing environmental emissions in production and op-
erations), resource use (reductions in materials, energy, and water consumption), and
innovation (development of environmental technologies and eco-designed products). Cat-
egory weights vary by industry through LSEG’s materiality matrix; emissions receive a
magnitude weight of 10 for metals and mining but 1 for banking, for example. Categories
are percentile-ranked within each firm’s industry group, so the score captures relative
performance among peers rather than absolute impact.
   We use the base pillar scores rather than the controversy-adjusted ESGC scores. The
ESGC discount uses severity weights that scale with market capitalisation: large firms
receive a weight of 0.33, mid-cap firms 0.67, and small firms 1.0. Because our identification

                                      37
strategy exploits a firm-size threshold, incorporating a size-dependent media penalty into
the walk measure would introduce a mechanical confound.

B.4 Bid-ask spread and missing-value handling
The proportional bid-ask spread is constructed from daily LSEG bid and ask prices in
EUR as Spread% = (Ask − Bid)/[(Ask + Bid)/2] × 100. The bid-ask spread is a standard
proxy for market liquidity and information asymmetry (Amihud, 2002). Including it as
a control addresses the concern that differences in liquidity between treated and control
firms could confound the estimated treatment effect, an issue raised in prior work on the
relationship between ESG disclosure and market microstructure.
   Some firms have missing ESG pillar scores for individual years. We do not fill missing
values with zero, since a missing LSEG score indicates that LSEG did not assess the firm
in that year, not that the firm scored zero. Instead we retain missing values as NaN and
apply within-firm linear interpolation for gaps of at most one year. For a firm observed
in years t − 1 and t + 1 but missing in year t, the interpolated value is the simple average
of the adjacent observations. For missing values at the boundary of a firm’s observed
period, we forward- or backward-fill within the firm. An imputation flag allows us to
verify that results are not driven by imputed observations. In the final estimation sample,
no surviving observation relies on interpolated walk scores; all interpolated values were
dropped by the subsequent bandwidth and pre/post-period filters, so the main results are
based entirely on directly observed LSEG pillar scores.

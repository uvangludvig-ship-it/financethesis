Are Forward Contracts on Swedish Stocks Correctly Priced on
             NASDAQ OMX Nordic?

                             Bill Sawarell* & Sebastian Berggren**

                                     Abstract

This paper examines if forward contracts on Swedish stocks are correctly priced on NASDAQ
OMX Nordic. We compute what prices are predicted to be according to financial theory and
compare these to actual prices observed in the marketplace. We find that forward contracts on
Swedish stocks exhibit behavior similar to what has previously been documented for single-
stock futures and stock index futures in the United States. We also find that more liquid
contracts on average are less mispriced than less liquid contracts and that forward contracts on
stocks that pay out a relatively large dividend compared to the stock’s price are more
mispriced in an absolute sense than contracts on stocks that pay out a relatively small
dividend. We conclude that the latter finding likely can be ascribed to uncertainty with
regards to dividend payments, and we also conclude that liquidity plays a key role in
explaining the observed mispricings.

    1. Introduction

This paper examines if forward contracts on Swedish stocks are correctly priced on NASDAQ

OMX Nordic.

The reason we chose to examine this is because it helps us understand if derivatives actually

are priced the way financial theory explains they should be, and it helps us get a better

understanding of how financial theory actually is implemented in reality. If it should turn out

that these types of securities are not priced according to what is to be expected it is among

other things important from a trading perspective, but it can also help us learn about the

limitations of financial theory and the limits of arbitrage.

We chose stock forwards because these are fairly simple to price in theory, and it is an

analysis that could be carried out with a high degree of accuracy since very few assumptions

need to be made. We will however discuss if the findings reasonably can be extended to other

types of derivatives as well and also if the analysis reasonably can be relevant for markets

other than NASDAQ OMX Nordic. We chose NASDAQ OMX Nordic because it is the main

Nordic financial market, and hence the one that we are closest to as students at SSE. Also,

similar tests have been done for example in the US, but we have not found any comparable

test for the Nordic region.

The paper is structured as follows: Part 2 describes the underlying theory, Part 3 explains the

method we have used to carry out the analysis, Part 4 states our main questions and

hypotheses, Part 5 contains the actual statistical analysis, Part 6 discusses the key findings and

concludes, followed by a bibliography and an appendix which contains all tables and graphs.

   2. Theory

Prices of derivatives are exclusively determined via arbitrage arguments, and the assumption

that there are no arbitrage opportunities is generally used in quantitative finance to calculate

risk-neutral prices for derivatives. A fundamental assumption in pricing of derivative

instruments is the assumption of rational pricing, which states that the price of an asset is the

arbitrage-free price of the asset and that any deviations from this price quickly will be

exploited by arbitrageurs. There may however be differences between the theoretical and

actual pricing of derivatives due to factors such as commissions, lack of liquidity, borrowing-

and lending constraints for certain types of investors, etc. There are multiple factors that may

limit arbitrage opportunities and their respective differences differ from market to market.

As stated earlier, pricing of stock forwards requires very few assumptions and this is one of

the main reasons we chose these types of securities for our analysis. This is in contrast to

securities such as stock options, index options and index futures, which require at least some

assumptions about factors such as volatilities and dividend yields.

A forward contract on an investment asset with a perfectly predictable income (commonly

referred to as ‘known income’), such as a known dividend, is theoretically priced according to

the pricing relation

The pricing relation relies on the following set of assumptions, that all need to hold for some,

                                     2
but not all, market participants :

    1. The market participants are subject to no transaction costs when they trade.

    2. The market participants are subject to the same tax rate on all net trading profits.

    3. The market participants can borrow money at the same risk-free rate of interest as they

       can lend money.

    4. The market participants take advantage of arbitrage opportunities as they occur.

The reason that these assumptions are not required to be true for all market participants is that

it is enough for them to hold for a few key participants for arbitrage opportunities to be

arbitraged away. If these assumptions are not fulfilled it can lead to mispricings, and this

should be evidenced by deviations from theoretical prices in the market.

To see first why the second relation (2) has to hold, consider first a situation where the left

hand side of the equation is higher than the right hand side. An arbitrageur can then short the

forward contract at the higher price, borrow to buy the stock, and at expiration pay back the

loan with interest and make a profit equal to the difference between the left- and the right

hand side. Secondly, if the right hand side is higher than the left hand side, an arbitrageur can

short the stock, invest the proceeds from the short-sale at the risk-free rate and enter into a

long forward contract. At expiration, again, a profit equal to the difference between the right-

and the left hand side is made since the proceeds from the short sale is greater than the price

paid for the long forward contract.

The logic behind the first relation (1) is similar to the second. The difference is that an

investor either receives the dividend if he is long the stock, or pays it if he is short the stock. If

Prior research

Most of the previous work that has been done in the field of forwards and futures focuses

mainly on the American market. Jones & Brooks (2005) analyzed the trading of single-stock

futures in the United States and among other things concluded that it is still a growing market

which needs to increase in volume for the market to be efficient. Others, such as Yadav &

Pope (1994), MacKinlay & Ramaswamy (1988) and Lo & MacKinlay (2001), have studied

the market for stock-index futures, and although various markets have been examined such as

the German DAX, (see for example Bühler & Kempf (1994)) and the Asian Nikkei 225 (see

for example Lim (1992)) this type of research seems as well to gravitate toward the American

market, and most commonly the S&P 500 Index. Our experience is that there is a general lack

of research that focuses on futures- and forward contracts on single stocks and the reason for

this is likely that the lack of volume evidenced by Jones & Brooks, and the resulting lack of

available data makes for a more time-consuming and less reliable analysis. As will be

explained further below, we for example have had to collect all our trade history data

manually one contract at a time since there are no ready datasets to use. We do however

believe that this fact may present us with an opportunity to make somewhat of a more unique

analysis since we in effect have created our own dataset.

Limitations

Although forwards are traded on a range of underlying Nordic stocks on NASDAQ OMX

Nordic we have chosen to include only Swedish stocks as underlying instruments in this

paper. This is mainly because we set out early on to target Sweden specifically, but also

because we found it easier to work with companies that were subject to the same accounting

rules since we have collected dividend data from annual and quarterly reports. For example

these are published around the same time, and the process of proposing and accepting

dividends are the same for all listed Swedish companies.

We have chosen the first four months of 2012 as the time period to examine, and this is

mainly because of two reasons: The first one is that data is only available on futures contracts

that have not yet expired, meaning that if we go far back in time very little data is available.

The second reason is that because the pricing relation requires a known dividend, we need to

be reasonably sure that investors are aware of what the dividend payouts will be. Dividends

for 2011 to be paid out in 2012 were proposed in the last quarterly reports for 2011, and these

were published either late 2011 or early 2012. We are thus making an assumption here, and

that is that all investors were aware of the proposed dividends for 2011 in January 2012. A

justification for using proposed dividends as proxies for known dividends is provided later in

this part.

We have also excluded contracts that have more than a year left until expiration, counting

from the day when the dividend is paid out in 2012. This is because there reasonably will be

additional dividend payouts before those longer contracts expire, and since we do not know

how big the following dividends will be we cannot calculate the fair forward prices

accurately. We have taken into account the fact that some companies pay out dividends more

                                        6
than one time per year, but our sample consists entirely of companies that usually pay out

only one dividend.

Also excluded are contracts expiring more than a year from the trading day, since the longest

published maturity for the risk-free asset (STIBOR Fixing, explanation follows below) is 12-

months and thus 12 months is the longest period we can reliably include in the analysis.

Risk-free rate ( ࢘  )

There is no general consensus regarding what constitutes a risk-free asset when pricing

financial instruments. Some, such as Damodaran (2008), argue that for an asset to be

completely risk-free there has to be absolutely no default risk. This implies that the entity

issuing the asset has to be a government because these are the only entities that can print

Others, such as Hull (2012), argue that in actuality the most widely used proxies for risk-free

rates are interbank rates, but that lately OIS (Overnight Index Swap) rates are becoming more

widely adopted. This is primarily a consequence of the fact that spreads between interbank-

and OIS rates increased dramatically towards the end of 2007 as a consequence of the

financial crises, as evidenced among others by Thornton (2009). The increase in the

interbank/OIS-spread implied that interbank rates are not in fact risk-free, since the higher

interbank-rate showed that a premium were required to make up for the possibility of defaults

                               6
(credit risk) among banks.  Hull also argues that rates such as treasury rates may be

artificially low due to high demand caused by factors such as favorable tax-treatment and the

possibility to get a better credit rating for an entity having treasury-bonds on its books, and

thus not suitable to use as proxies for risk-free assets. Naranjo (2009) finds that the risk-free

rates implied from S&P 500 Index futures generally are higher than the treasury rates, but also

generally lower than the interbank rates.

The closest thing to an OIS-rate we have found in Sweden is the Tomorrow Next (T/N)

STIBOR Swap (STINA). Unfortunately this rate is not publically quoted, since it is not traded

through a centralized exchange but rather between two counterparties and not reported to any

exchange. We have been in contact with Riksbanken, but they could not provide us with any

data on the STINA swap-rate and referred us to the interbank rate instead.

In our analysis we have exclusively used the interbank rate (Stockholm Interbank Offered

Rate, STIBOR Fixing), which is consistent with Hull and Naranjo, given the fact that we do

not have access to a Swedish equivalent of an OIS-rate.

    4. Questions and hypotheses

The main question we seek to answer is “Are forwards contracts on Swedish stocks correctly

priced on NASDAQ OMX Nordic?”, that is, we want to know if there is a difference between

what financial theory predicts prices to be and what they actually are. Since we have a pricing

relation that theoretically should hold, we start out by assuming that it does, and thus our main

hypothesis is “Forwards contracts on Swedish stocks are correctly priced on NASDAQ OMX

Nordic.”

The main hypothesis is fairly broad in the sense that even if prices are correctly priced on

average there may be certain types of contracts that are more or less mispriced than others.

We have decided not to state questions or hypotheses for all possible sub-sample

discrepancies that may be found, but examples of such questions are “Does the level of

mispricing depend on time until expiration?”, “Does the level of mispricing depend on the

price of the underlying stock?” and “Does the level of mispricing depend on how close the

underlying stock is to a dividend payment?”

This paper only tests if stock forwards are correctly priced on NASDAQ OMX Nordic, but it

is possible that the findings can be relevant for other types of derivatives, as well as for other

marketplaces. The main questions we ask with regards to this is “Can the findings reasonably

be relevant for other types of derivatives?” and “Can the findings reasonably be relevant for

other marketplaces?”

These are the main questions we seek to answer. We also want to try to review if the findings

may be incorrect due to certain assumptions we have made or because of the choices we have

made with regards to the data we have chosen to use as input.

Test if the mean difference is significantly different from zero

To see if the prices of the actual contracts traded significantly differ from the theoretical

prices, we run a t-test for the mean difference to see if the difference on average is

significantly different from zero. The output can be found in Table 1. We find that we cannot

reject the null hypothesis at the 5 % level, as is evidenced by the t-statistic and the

corresponding confidence interval. This is a promising finding in a number of ways. First, it

lends credibility to the way financial theory suggests derivatives should be priced in actuality.

Secondly, it shows that on average stock forwards on Swedish stocks are correctly priced on

    6. Key findings and conclusion

The main similar paper we have found is by Jones & Brooks (2005) in which they examine

the market for single-stock futures in the United States. They argue that the fact that the

volume of single stock futures are drastically lower than the volume of underlying stocks

traded is one possible reason for the many cases of mispricings, and this is a conclusion that

can be extended to our analysis. For example a stock’s closing price may not be representative

of what the stock price was when the forward trade was initiated, and thus we should have

apparent mispricings in random directions, which is what we largely have seen. We also find

that contracts that are traded more frequently, and thus are more liquid, are less mispriced

than contracts that are traded less frequently and this we believe supports the argument that

the lack of liquidity in these types of derivatives leads to mispricings when we look at closing

price data.

Yadav & Pope (1994), MacKinlay & Ramaswamy (1988) and Lo & MacKinlay (2001) find

that stock index futures in the United States that are further away from maturity are more

mispriced in an absolute sense than ones that are closer to maturity, and we find this to be true

for forwards on single stocks on the NASDAQ OMX Nordic as well. We believe, as stated

above, that this mainly is due to uncertainty with regards to the sizes of the dividend payouts

and this is in accordance with the conclusion reached by Lo & MacKinlay. We do however

believe that there is less uncertainty in our analysis and that this leads to a less significant

relationship between absolute level of mispricing and time until expiration. We also find that

stocks that are expected to pay a relatively large dividend as a percentage of the stock price

are more mispriced in an absolute sense than stocks that pay a relatively small dividend. This

is in accordance with the belief that uncertainty with regards to dividends is a cause for

mispricings, because if dividends are a larger part of the pricing relation, uncertainty with

regards to these will reasonably lead to more significant mispricings. The fact that we find

                                        21
some apparent similarities with analyses made on stock index futures and single-stock futures

in the United States leads us to believe that some of the findings in this paper can be extended

to other types of derivatives as well as other markets, which is one of the questions we asked

earlier, since their findings come from another marketplace and another, although fairly

similar, type of derivative.

We also find that contracts tend to be priced higher than expected more frequently than they

are priced lower than expected, and we find that contracts seem to be skewed towards being

priced higher than expected on average as well, although this finding is not significant.

There can be different reasonable explanations for why the forward contracts in our sample

tend to be priced higher than predicted. It may be the case that investors’ beliefs with regards

to what dividend payments will be are skewed in a particular direction for certain companies

and this may be the explanation as to why we find this type of distribution. It may also be the

case that the predicted prices are too low because the risk-free rate we are using is too low,

and it may be the case that certain types of investors use a higher rate than the one we have

used in our analysis. Since we do not have access to investors’ perceptions about dividends

nor what risk-free rate they are using, and that the average mispricing is not significantly

different from zero we feel it is appropriate to be careful not to draw too strong conclusions

with regards to this. Overall, for reasons explained earlier, we have a fairly limited number of

observations and the time period examined is fairly short, and we believe that it is important

to take this into consideration. As Lo & MacKinlay (2001) wrote in their paper, “… the short

time period and the small number of contracts considered prohibit one from drawing strong

conclusions.”

Overall we feel confident in our analysis and the fact that contracts on average were priced

similarly to what the theory predicted suggests that the analysis was carried out in an accurate

                                        22
way. We have not made many assumptions throughout the analysis, and we believe that some

of the mispricings evidenced can be attributed to variables that we have not, and could not

have, had access to, such as the characteristics and workings of different types of investors.
